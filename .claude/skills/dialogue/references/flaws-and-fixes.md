# Flaws and fixes

*Sources: the Implied World theory (the reserve dumped onto the surface; exposition dialogue as film's typical failure; surplus in passing; productive gaps), the Bond theory (care can't be ordered; visible levers backfire; the Humour, Interiority and Specificity levers and where each breaks; "out of character" as a broken bond; a villain who is a function), the Gap theory (principle 6, the teller's limits; the perspective gap; nested inference) and the Anticipation theory (the ratchet; the uncertainty term); McKee, Dialogue (the four families of flaws, credibility, language, content and design, with the cause and repair of each; the paraphrasing trap; where to start a repair), Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing (his cautions on dialogue and exposition; melodrama as missing steps; filler, the frozen duel and talkiness) and Truby, The Anatomy of Story (the two opposite mistakes about dialogue; subtext as a choice); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 How to use this file. 2 Credibility: no reason under the line. 3 Language: the words themselves. 4 Content: what the talk carries. 5 Design: the shape of beats and scenes. 6 Egri's cautions. 7 Fix the story before the words. 8 Rivals. 9 Traps.

McKee sorts dialogue flaws into four families *(built: his through-line is that nearly every flaw is a mismatch between what drives a character underneath and what comes out of their mouth)*. Each entry gives the symptom a reader would report, why it fails, and the fix, with the provenance of the rule. Terms as in `SKILL.md` and `line-and-scene-design.md` (line, speech, move, beat, scene want, value, charge, turn).

## 1. How to use this file

**Case.** A Science Fiction draft opens with two engineers on a colony ship telling each other that the ship left Earth two hundred years ago, that the crew is in its eighth generation, and that the reactor is failing. Readers say "stiff; I felt briefed". Both engineers know all of it, so neither has a reason to say it: a credibility flaw (section 2). The fix is not better wording. Give the facts a job. The older engineer wants the council told today; the younger wants it kept quiet until after her daughter's naming day, and she fires the eight generations at him as an accusation: eight generations kept this ship calm, and he wants to be the one who starts the panic? Now one fact arrives as a blow while the audience watches his face. The two hundred years need not be said at all: the child's toy, carved from a part nobody on the ship can make any more, carries it in passing.

**Point.** Work in this order.
1. **Pin the symptom.** To whom (which reader), where (the line or page it starts), compared with what (an earlier draft, a scene that works). "The dialogue is bad" is not yet a symptom; "the second reader skimmed from the phone call on" is.
2. **Find the family.** A first guess from what readers say, to be tested:

| Readers say | Check first |
|---|---|
| "stiff", "I felt told", "nobody talks like that", "over the top" | credibility, section 2 |
| "clichéd", "they all sound the same", "writerly", "flat" | language, section 3 |
| "on the nose", "preachy", "the speech is too long" | content, section 4 |
| "drags", "goes round in circles", "nothing happens", "I saw it coming" | design, section 5 |

3. **If the whole scene fails, check the story first** (section 7). Most word-level flaws in a failing scene are symptoms of a missing want, wall or turn.
4. **Test each fix with `hard-to-vary`.** *Swap the speaker*: if another character could say the line unchanged, the voice is not holding it. *Remove the line*: which job stops being done (`what-dialogue-does.md`, section 3)? If none, cut it. If removing it loses nothing, check whether another line does the same job: keep the one that also does a second job, cut the other, then run the test again on the line you kept. *Move it*: if the line could sit anywhere in the scene, its timing is loose. Then check the fix on the line that forced it and on the lines it must leave alone.

## 2. Credibility: no reason under the line

Audiences read every line as a person's act and infer a reason behind it (the Gap theory's nested inference, at the scale of a line). When they can infer none, or only the author's, the line stops belonging to the person and belief in the scene goes with it. McKee groups these as credibility flaws *(built)*; the entries below run by what a reader reports.

- **"I didn't believe her."** First ask which kind. If readers distrust what she says because they sense she is lying, the story may be working; if they distrust it because they do not believe in her, the draft needs work *(McKee, built)*. *Fix:* fix only the second. It often comes from a line that contradicts who she has been without the story earning it, which the Bond theory reads as a broken bond ("out of character").
- **"I felt briefed."** Two people tell each other what both know. *Why:* the only reason the audience can infer is the author's need, and the line is the reserve dumped onto the surface, the failure the Implied World theory names (and lists as film's typical one). *Fix:* the honesty test, the outsider with a reason to ask, the fact fired as ammunition, the fact that opens a question, surplus in passing, all in `what-dialogue-does.md`, section 7. Insiders talk in shorthand, so explanation between them rings false, while an outsider genuinely needs it *(McKee, built; ignore the labels he pins on whole nations)*.
- **"How would she know that?"** (McKee's *overly knowing* talk). A teenager explains the causes of a war like a historian; a narrator looking back sees more than their life could have taught them. *Why:* the audience keeps a running account of what each character could have learned, and the teller's limits set what can fairly be known (Gap theory, principle 6) *(McKee, asserted)*. *Fix:* ask where they learned each thing; cut it, or give them the source.
- **"Nobody sees themselves that clearly."** (*overly perceptive* talk). A character names their own wound, pattern and drive, accurately. *Why:* read through the workshop's frame, a person's unsayable is reserve that even they cannot reach; stating it closes the perspective gap the audience was enjoying and leaves nothing to infer *(McKee, built on his model of mind)*. The pull comes from good practice: writers research more psychology than they use and want to spend it. *Fix:* move the insight to what others notice, to action, or leave it to the audience. The research is reserve; show the tip.
- **"Why is he so upset?"** (McKee's *overly emotive* talk). *Why:* the audience looks for a cause big enough for the feeling on show, and when the visible one is too small it settles on the nearest other explanation, that something is wrong with the character or with the writing *(McKee, built)*. *Fix:* rate the stakes, then the heat of the words. Raise the stakes earlier or cool the words, unless overreaction is a trait you mean to show (the trait exception, section 3).
- **"It lurches" or "it's over the top."** Melodrama, with two checks, and a scene can fail either. *Size (McKee, built):* the act is larger than what is at stake for the character; going to extremes is not in itself the fault. *Fix:* raise what is at stake to match the act, then choose whether this person would say it loud or quiet (`voice.md`, section 5). *Steps (Egri, built):* the character leaps between extreme states with none of the in-between states shown. A woman icily polite to her brother all scene is sobbing in his arms two lines later, with no crack in the ice along the way. *Fix:* find each big swing and write the moments that carry it, or cut the swing. A sudden kindness with no steps before it is also the Decency lever breaking: kindness put on for the audience's benefit (Bond theory).
- **"The backstory is doing all the work."** A buried trauma is produced to explain an extreme act. *Why:* a cause that would explain any act explains none (easy to vary, in `hard-to-vary`'s terms); what the character tells themselves is a justification, not the drive *(McKee, built)*. The all-purpose trauma is the kind of visible lever the Bond theory says feels cheap when it serves the audience's tear ducts rather than the character. *Fix:* write what drives the character and the reason they give, separately. The speech voices the reason; the drive leaks through what the character does.
- **"Stop telling me how wonderful she is."** *Symptom:* characters keep telling each other how brilliant or kind someone is, and readers resist. *Why:* care can't be ordered; telling us a character is brilliant or kind, or having others praise them, makes audiences resist, because they believe what they see (Bond theory). The workshop reads a chorus of warnings about how dangerous someone is the same way; that extension is its own reading. *Fix:* show the deed and let any praise follow it, or make the praise contested or wrong. Building a reputation before an entrance works only if the character then earns it in view *(Truby, fitted; inside the Bond theory's limit)*.

## 3. Language: the words themselves

**The trait exception.** Several fixes below, and the heat entry in section 2, allow the flaw when it is the character's habit. The exception holds only if the habit runs through this character's other lines, breaks only for a reason (`voice.md`, section 5, a voice is a personal normal), and fails the swap test: the line would sound wrong from another character. Otherwise fix the flaw.

- **Stock phrases.** *Symptom:* the reader can finish the line first. *Why:* a phrase that once worked has been used until it gives nothing *(McKee, fitted)*; it rides on the audience's defaults the way a generic setting rides on its genre's (Implied World theory, baseline). *Fix:* write the first version, then many more, some deliberately odd, and keep the best *(McKee, asserted)*. Allow a stock phrase where this character would lean on one; real people do.
- **Lines any character could say.** *Symptom:* "they all sound the same". *Fix:* the swap test, then `voice.md`, sections 1 and 6.
- **The writer on show.** *Symptom:* "writerly"; the reader admires a line and stops believing the scene. *Why:* the audience agrees to treat invented people as real only while the talk seems theirs, and visible craft breaks the agreement *(McKee, built)*. Egri: talk must never outshine the play *(built)*. *Fix:* mark the lines you are proudest of and ask whether the character or the author is speaking. Before cutting, check the medium and the genre's tradition of heightened speech (`prose-stage-screen.md`).
- **Dry, abstract talk.** *Symptom:* speeches read like a report. *Why:* abstract nouns, long Latin-rooted words and long sentences slow the ear and hide the person *(McKee, asserted, as guidance)*. *Fix:* name the actual thing in everyday words; prefer short words; in a fight, prefer verbs of doing to "is" and "was", including the "was doing" forms. Keep the abstraction when the register is the character's trait: someone formal, pompous, or trained to talk that way.
- **Long-windedness.** *Fix:* cut the speech by half and see what is lost; keep the inflation only when the inflation is the joke *(McKee, asserted)*.
- **Small talk.** *Symptom:* greetings, the weather, how are the children. *Why:* chat with nothing under it gives the audience's inference nothing to work on *(McKee, built; see the rival in `what-dialogue-does.md`, section 9)*. *Fix:* ask what the characters are doing with the pleasantries (stalling, testing, avoiding, courting); if something, make that move readable; if nothing, cut. Chat that takes the world for granted may be surplus doing its job; that rival is in `what-dialogue-does.md`, section 9.
- **Vague words.** *Symptom:* the listener has to decode. *Why:* general nouns and verbs propped up with adjectives and adverbs blur the sense *(McKee, built)*. *Fix:* one precise noun or verb in each place, unless vagueness is the point.
- **Echoes.** Unplanned repeats of a word or a sound slip past the eye; read the draft aloud or record it *(McKee, asserted)*. A related flaw McKee finds in weak improvisation, and drafts show it too: a speaker throws the other's last word back as a question to buy time. Unless it characterises, cut it and make the reply move the scene.
- **Talk copied from life.** *Why:* real talk circles, pads and repeats; dialogue is chosen speech that only gives the impression of real talk *(Truby and McKee, built)*. Truby names the opposite mistake too: loading talk with work the structure should do, which makes it stiff *(built)*. *Fix:* ask of each line what it adds beyond realism; heighten it or cut it.

## 4. Content: what the talk carries

- **On the nose.** *Symptom:* characters say exactly what they think and feel. McKee ranks it the commonest and most harmful flaw *(asserted)*; when saying it outright is right is in `what-dialogue-does.md`, sections 5 and 9. One repair to add: McKee's actor's question *(built)*. For a scene whose surface is all there is, ask what in it is unusual for these people: candles on the table mean something is wrong. Play the scene with that problem underneath and leave the words almost as they are.
- **The head-on row.** *Symptom:* both people shout the real issue at each other, and the scene thuds. McKee's term for it is the *duelogue* *(built)*: in life people circle their problems and fight through something else, and the audience's pleasure is seeing through to what the fight is really about (the Gap theory's nested inference). *Fix:* route the fight through a *third thing*, McKee's *trialogue*: an object, a pet, a shared memory, a film, a subject both know. A divorcing couple dividing their books can fight the whole marriage over who keeps one of them. The strongest third thing is the subject the speaker knows best, talked about so that it describes the speaker *(McKee, built)*.
- **The speech no one hears.** *Symptom:* "the speech is too long"; attention leaks away halfway. *Why:* nothing reacts and the charge never changes, so the speaker has become a mouthpiece *(McKee, built)*. *Fix:* mark where the listener reacts, even silently, and where the speaker adjusts; break the speech into beats; give it a turn and its weightiest line last (`line-and-scene-design.md`, section 9). If it still runs long, check whether the situation expects a speech; if not, cut.
- **The sermon.** *Symptom:* "preachy". *Why:* a character steps out of themselves to deliver the author's point *(Egri, built)*; the moral argument should be made mainly by what characters do, with talk adding the shading *(Truby, asserted)*. *Fix:* ask whether this speaker, provoked by this moment, would say it. If not, cut it and let an event, or the irony of a situation, carry the point.
- **The joke with one job.** *Symptom:* the laugh stops the scene. *Why:* wordplay earns its place only when it fits the speaker and does a second job, revealing them, moving the fight, planting something *(Egri, fitted)*; and constant quips undercut stakes (Bond theory, Humour lever). *Fix:* name the second job, or cut. More in `voice.md`, section 9, and the rival in section 8 below.
- **Plain meaning in a symbolic story.** McKee exempts stories that deal in symbols rather than imitate life (Myth, Fantasy, Farce, allegory and more) from the subtext rule: archetypes speak plainly, and irony under a solemn line can draw an unwanted laugh *(built on his model of mind; his list of genres is a sweeping catch-all)*. Decide early where the story sits between the mirror of ordinary life and the symbol, and set the density of subtext to match. See section 8.

## 5. Design: the shape of beats and scenes

- **Repeated beats.** *Symptom:* the scene reads well and feels dead. The label test and the fix are in `line-and-scene-design.md`, section 2 *(McKee, built)*.
- **Diminishing returns.** Each repeat of an effect lands less, and a third in a row can swing into its opposite, tears into laughter *(McKee, fitted; the numbers he gives are asserted)*. The ratchet says the same of any pattern the audience has learned. Refrains and running gags use repetition on purpose: the check is whether each return escalates or arrives in a new situation.
- **Meaning that lands at the wrong moment.** *Too late:* the speech wanders before its point, so the audience skips or guesses early and waits. *Too soon:* the point comes first and the speech rambles on *(McKee, built)*. *Fix:* read aloud, mark where each speech's meaning lands, and choose front, centre or end loading on purpose (`line-and-scene-design.md`, section 7).
- **The buried cue.** The speech's pivot word sits mid-speech and the other actor has to wait. *Fix:* move the pivot word to the end, where it cues the reply *(McKee, built)*.
- **A turn too soon, too late, or never.** *Too soon:* the first beat settles it and the rest is rehash. *Too late:* repeated beats drag on after the audience has seen the turn coming. *Never:* however much friction, the charge at the end matches the charge at the start *(McKee, built)*. In Anticipation theory terms, an early turn sets uncertainty to zero for the rest of the scene, and a late one spends it before the turn arrives. *Fix:* name the value and its charge at the start and the end; if they match, cut the scene or give it a turn.
- **Want and words come apart.** The four questions in `SKILL.md`, Diagnosing Step 2, are the check, after McKee's causes of a scene that splinters *(built; a fairly tight set)*. Each "no" tends to show as its own symptom: bland lines over a strong want, overheated lines over a weak one, lines that stop following on, or a scene that never turns.
- **Filler between fights.** *Why:* the writer skipped the step that should link two clashes, and chat fills the gap *(Egri, built)*. *Fix:* write the missing step.
- **The frozen duel.** Two opponents trade clever thrusts that neither can win, and harden into types *(Egri, built)*. *Fix:* each exchange must gain or lose someone ground.
- **Talkiness.** *Why:* too many words usually means nobody in the scene is changing position *(Egri, built)*. *Fix:* fix the conflict first, then cut.

## 6. Egri's cautions

Egri's chapter on dialogue gathers checks that cut across the families. Several are covered elsewhere: with voice, speech from all three of a character's dimensions, the idiom of their trade, background in the first lines, the seed of the last speech in the first, orchestrating the cast, and character before cleverness (`voice.md`); with exposition, the makeshift devices (the servant briefing a new servant, the convenient phone call, the letter read aloud) and exposition as a revealing of character that never stops (`what-dialogue-does.md`, section 7); and in this file, the sermon and the one-job joke (section 4) and filler, the frozen duel and talkiness (section 5). The rest, as checks on a draft:
- **Plant in talk what the climax will use** *(built)*: an object, a skill, a fact, first met through the character's own habits, not by chance. List everything the climax uses and find where the audience first learns of each.
- **Entrances and exits need the character's own reason** *(built)*. Sending someone for a glass of water so two others can talk is the author's convenience showing. Important characters should be talked about before they appear, and then live up to it.
- **Talk must not outshine the play** *(built)*: a showpiece speech outside the character's own idiom hurts both the person and the scene.

## 7. Fix the story before the words

**Case.** A writer redrafts a flat break-up scene five times, each time making the lovers explain themselves more clearly, and each pass is more on the nose than the last. The trouble was never the words: neither lover wanted anything in the scene except to be understood, so there was no wall and no turn. Given a scene want each (he wants to leave before she can make it her decision; she wants him to say the name of the other woman), the scene needs half the words.

**Point.** McKee's *paraphrasing trap* *(asserted, with a plausible mechanism)*: each rewording of a scene that fails as a whole tends to spell out a little more, and the subtext drains away. All three books treat talk as the last layer and bad talk as usually a symptom of a weaker layer beneath *(McKee and Egri, built; Truby, asserted)*. Repair in this order:
1. **Want and wall.** What does each speaker want here, and what stands in the way? The four questions in `SKILL.md`, Diagnosing Step 2, belong to this layer.
2. **Beats and turn.** Does the charge change, and where?
3. **Subtext.** What does each speaker know, feel and want that they will not say, and what stops them?
4. **Voice.** Could each line be only this speaker's?
5. **Words.** Only now the language flaws of section 3.

**Where to start the repair** *(after McKee)*. If you can already hear a better line, write it, then check that a want in the scene holds it in place; if none does, the new line is decoration. If you cannot hear it, stop writing lines and settle what each speaker wants and fears here; the words usually follow. The second route is slower and more often right. For a line that rings false because the writer cannot hear the character, use the Magic If in `voice.md`, section 10.

The order is for scenes that fail as a whole. McKee's own chapters on vague words and misplaced pivot words show that a sound scene can still be spoiled by its lines; for a bad line in a working scene, go straight to section 3.

## 8. Rivals

Two rivals that bear on flaws are recorded in `what-dialogue-does.md`, section 9: whether a character may say what they feel (McKee against the Bond theory's Interiority lever), and whether small talk is ever worth keeping (McKee against the Implied World theory's surplus). Two more:

**Plain talk in symbolic stories, against indifference.** *McKee:* in stories that deal in symbols, characters are archetypes and may speak on the nose; that is the form, not a flaw *(built)*. *The Bond theory:* indifference is the enemy; a villain fails by being a function rather than a person, and the ladder of care starts from recognising a particular person, in any genre. *What tells them apart:* in a Fantasy draft, give the mentor one private scene want under one solemn line, and leave the line itself unchanged. If care for the mentor rises and the line is still taken straight, the owner theory holds and the plainness was a choice, not a law of the form. If the line now reads as ironic and the moment is lost, McKee's warning holds for that story.

**Wit that only amuses.** *Egri:* a joke with no second job breaks the play and should go *(fitted)*. *The Bond theory:* Humour is a lever of care (we like good company, and charm offsets cruelty), and it breaks when the quips are constant, because they undercut the stakes. They meet if making the audience enjoy a character's company counts as the second job, as long as the jokes are not so constant that nothing seems to matter. *What tells them apart:* cut a scene's one-job quips and leave everything else. If the audience likes the character less and nothing else changes, the humour was doing care work, as the Bond theory says; if nothing is lost, Egri was right about those lines.

## 9. Traps

- **Polishing words in a scene with no want.** The paraphrasing trap: every pass more on the nose.
- **Treating every stated feeling as a flaw.** When nothing makes a character hide it, or when saying it is itself a costly move, saying it can be right.
- **Cutting all small talk.** Chat with a move under it, and surplus in passing, both earn their place.
- **Choosing the family from the list instead of the symptom.** "Stiff" may be credibility or design; pin down where and for whom first.
- **Fixing a lie as if it were a failure of belief.** A lying character is meant to sound false; fix only the disbelief in the person.
- **Counting flaws.** Ten small language flaws in a scene that turns matter less than one missing turn.
- **Letting a book's taste decide.** McKee's ranking of flaws and Egri's verdicts on the witty society plays of his day are fitted from the works they admired: patterns to question, not laws.
