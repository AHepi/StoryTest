# Voice

*Sources: the Bond theory (the Recognise rung; the Specificity, Humour, Competence and Interiority levers and where each breaks; care is social; care can't be ordered; the books-and-film table, where a voice is prose's fastest route to recognition), the Gap theory (principle 2, the breach; principle 5, the promise; the teller and the perspective gap; Part 2 section 5, where prose's natural gap is voice) and the Implied World theory (surplus mentioned in passing; a slang as a departure; the attention budget; productive gaps); McKee, Dialogue (character-specific talk, naming and colouring words, culture as a store of images, what a character would never say, how talk changes under pressure, larger-than-life and comic characters, narrators and inner talk, writing in character), Truby, The Anatomy of Story (key words and taglines, first lines, characters at the top of their range, a narrator's stance towards the reader) and Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing (dialogue from all three dimensions, the idiom of a trade, orchestrating the cast, character before cleverness, the seed in the first speech); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 What a voice is for. 2 What they know, and how they see. 3 Where the words come from. 4 What they would never say. 5 Voice under pressure. 6 Voices against each other. 7 Key words and signature lines. 8 Narrators and inner talk. 9 Wit, and the comic voice. 10 Finding a voice. 11 Rivals. 12 Traps.

A **voice** is the way of speaking that only this character has: the words they reach for, how they build a sentence, their rhythm, what they know and what they will not say. In the Bond theory a distinct voice builds the first rung of care, Recognise ("that's a particular person"), and in prose it is the fastest route there. It is the Specificity lever working through dialogue, so it breaks where Specificity breaks: when quirks pile up and decorate instead of reveal.

## 1. What a voice is for

**Case.** Mr Collins in *Pride and Prejudice*. His speeches run long, stacked with deference to his patroness and with self-regard dressed as humility. When he proposes to Elizabeth he sets out his reasons in order, as if drafting a document, and her feelings come last. The novel hardly needs to tell us he is pompous and servile; his words do it. Nobody could hand his lines to Darcy, whose sentences are short and whose pride shows in what he will not bother to explain. Each voice carries class, values and history at once.

**Point.** A voice earns its place when it reveals something: what the character knows, how they see, where they come from, what they want and what they hide. That is the Bond theory's test for Specificity: details should reveal, not decorate. Three checks, each a `hard-to-vary` swap aimed at dialogue:
- **Swap the speaker** *(McKee, Egri)*. Hand the line to another character. If it fits them just as well, the line or the character is under-built.
- **Cover the names** *(Truby)*. Read a page with the speakers' names hidden. Can you still tell who is talking?
- **One fact, many mouths** *(Egri, McKee)*. Write one plain fact (the car won't start) as each main character would say it. If the versions match, the cast needs contrast, not new words.

**Scale the voice to the role.** McKee holds that the richer a character's inner life, the more unlike anyone else they must sound *(fitted, from Greek drama's move from stock roles to individuals)*. So a waiter can speak plainly; a lead cannot. His further ideal, that a lead should sound unlike every other character in fiction, is asserted; treat it as a direction, not a test.

## 2. What they know, and how they see

McKee splits a line's words into two kinds.
- **Naming words show what they know** *(built: a precise name needs knowledge behind it)*. The nouns and verbs. On home ground a character names the exact thing: a sailor the knot, a nurse the drug and the dose, a joiner the kind of nail. On ground they do not know, they fall back on general words. The reverse reading has exceptions: a general word can also be evasion, tact, or talking down to a listener.
- **Colouring words show how they see** *(the mappings are asserted: suggestive, untested)*. The words that qualify: adjectives, adverbs, whether the doer or the thing done comes first, and helper verbs of possibility and duty (can, might, must, ought). A character who says things happened to them sees life differently from one who says they did them.

**Case.** Holmes and Watson. Holmes's naming words are exact (kinds of tobacco ash, soils, what calluses say about a trade) and his colouring words few and cool. Watson's are warmer and more qualified, full of wonder at his friend. The Bond theory's reading of the pair shows up in how they talk: Watson supplies the Connection and the admiring view, and we learn to care about Holmes through someone who does.

**Moves.**
- Take one plain fact and write how each character would qualify it.
- Count the hedges (maybe, sort of, I think, could) in a key speech *(McKee, built by a controlled rewrite)*: strip them for a forceful character, add them for a timid one. The count works as a rough gauge of the Agency lever in dialogue, which is the workshop's reading.
- On each character's home ground, swap general nouns for the precise ones they would know; keep them general where the character knows nothing.

## 3. Where the words come from

Egri *(built)*: what a character says should come out of all three of their dimensions: body, place in society, and mind. McKee *(built)*: people explain themselves by borrowing from what they know, and what most people know best is the culture they have soaked up (family, work, faith, school, sport, songs, films). So when a character reaches for a comparison, they reach into their own store.
- **Trade and place.** Egri *(asserted, a common rule of thumb)*: people draw their images from their own line of work, as the sailor, the nurse and the joiner of section 2 draw their names for things. He treats imagery borrowed from a world the speaker does not belong to as a comic device, so use it for a laugh and not by accident.
- **Training.** How someone was taught to speak (pulpit, courtroom, parade ground, street corner) sets their word length and rhythm *(McKee, fitted)*.
- **The life they are living now.** A stalled life can sound stalled. McKee reads the young woman in *Lost in Translation* as speaking in passive, hedged, uncoloured words that mirror where she is, while the actor has taken on the tough-guy idiom of the roles he plays *(built)*.

**Case.** *Frasier*. The brothers insult each other in the language of opera, wine and psychiatry. Their snobbery lives in their comparisons, and their shared training tells each exactly where the other's sore spots are.

**Owner link.** A reference a character drops without comment is delivered the way the Implied World theory says surplus works best: taken for granted, it implies a whole life, as "The door dilated" implies a society. In an invented world, comparisons should come from that world's own songs, foods, trades and rites, which shows a world with business of its own (the indifference principle). A whole invented way of speaking is a departure (the theory lists a slang as one), so it spends attention the plot also needs and has to follow from the world's rules and hold steady. That design belongs to `.claude/skills/story-world/SKILL.md`. McKee agrees from the other side *(fitted)*: an unfamiliar setting licenses more colourful talk, provided it stays within the world's own limits across the whole work.

**Move.** For each main character, list what they have worked at, read, watched, eaten, worshipped and played. Draw their comparisons, insults and praise from that list.

## 4. What they would never say

A voice is also what a character holds back.
- **The ties they cannot risk.** McKee's *background desires*: the relationships a person already has and wants to keep (job, marriage, reputation, safety, self-image) follow them into every scene and limit what they will say to get what they want *(built; his claim that more good ties bring more restraint is plausible but unmeasured)*. Someone with nothing left to lose will say anything. To unleash a character, strip their ties first.
- **Manners and class.** People raised where nobody bares their soul will not start now; their politeness can carry orders and threats (`what-dialogue-does.md`, section 6).
- **Shared background.** McKee *(the mechanism is built)*: people who share a long background can say little and mean much. An insider can make a threat in one soft phrase, then must spell it out for an outsider, as happens more than once in *The Godfather*. His labels for whole nations and regions as more or less explicit are broad stereotypes; do not design characters by them.

**Owner link.** This is why insiders explaining things to each other rings false (the Implied World theory's reserve dumped on the surface), and why the outsider, one of the two guides the theory gives a world with many departures, can ask what insiders never would. It is also McKee's unsaid (`what-dialogue-does.md`, section 4): what the ties keep down is what the audience senses under the line.

**Move.** For each speaker in a scene, list what they would never say aloud to this listener, here, and why: fear, shame, manners, a tie they would lose. Check that no line breaks the list without a reason. Where nothing on the list applies, let them say it (`what-dialogue-does.md`, section 5).

## 5. Voice under pressure

McKee's tendencies *(fitted; he offers them as tendencies, not rules)*:
- Feeling and action shorten words and sentences; reasoning and reflection lengthen them.
- The speaker in control tends to use longer, complete sentences and to set the pace. The one losing control asks more questions and breaks into fragments. McKee reads the therapy scene in *The Sopranos* this way: Tony's questions and fragments against Melfi's measured statements.
- Hedges show a speaker fixed on what is possible or allowed; bare verbs show will.

Egri agrees *(fitted, from Shakespeare)*: the sound of a play's lines should track its conflict, weighty while characters reflect, clipped once events speed up. The Anticipation theory's prose table says the writer steers time with sentence length; here the character's own sentences do it.

**A voice is a personal normal.** McKee's example: a foul-mouthed gangster who suddenly speaks politely reads as danger. The workshop reads this through the Gap theory's breach, by analogy: a break from a person's own normal marks an event, as a break from the world's normal does. Make every break mean something. A break with no cause reads as out of character (`.claude/skills/character/references/change-and-growth.md`, section 8).

**Shouted or quiet.** Two characters doing the same terrible thing at the same heat become different people depending on whether they shout it or say it quietly *(McKee, built)*: the ruler who rants a sentence of death seems ruled by his feelings; the one who orders it in a bored murmur seems in full command. The quiet version leaves more to the audience (the Implied World theory's productive gaps).

**Case.** *Breaking Bad*. When Skyler begs Walt to go to the police because he is a man in over his head, his voice changes: short flat statements, no hedges, himself as the source of danger rather than its target. The change of voice is the turn, and it shows a self the nervous surface had kept hidden since the first episode.

## 6. Voices against each other

Egri's *orchestration* *(built)*: choose and contrast the cast with care, and much of the dialogue follows from the contrast. His case is *Riders to the Sea*: a shared dialect need not mean shared voices, since each speaker can keep a rhythm of their own.
- **Put a third thing between two speakers** *(McKee, built)*: a group they watch, an object, a topic. Their different attitudes to it, and their different ways of thinking (cool and abstract against hot and concrete), show at once, as in a fair comparison. McKee's case is the two detectives of *True Detective* watching a revival meeting, one sneering in long abstract words, the other defending the crowd in plain vivid ones. This is the swap test run live.
- **One larger voice** *(McKee, fitted)*. A character who feels and thinks bigger than everyone around them can take the story's richest language (Jack Sparrow, Walter White at full height), with the others kept plainer so the contrast marks them out. In Bond terms verbal brilliance is Competence and Humour, and it can carry a cold character: Hannibal Lecter has little screen time and all the best lines.
- **The top of their range** *(Truby, asserted and genre-dependent)*: characters talk sharper, funnier and better argued than people do in life, but within their own vocabulary. Deliberately flat, naturalistic work rejects this; decide which you are writing.

**Case.** In *Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid* one man talks constantly and the other barely at all. Truby's reading *(built)*: the contrast defines both of them and shows them working as a team.

## 7. Key words and signature lines

- **A character's key words** *(Truby, built)*. Words a character says far more often than people normally would, returning in new situations, gather meaning. Give a lead two or three words or phrases only they use, and track each through the draft so that every return comes in a new context. Truby's full account of dialogue's layers (story, moral argument, key words) is in `line-and-scene-design.md`.
- **The tagline** *(Truby, fitted)*. One line that returns across a story, meaning more each time. In *Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid* a puzzled question about who the pursuers are comes back with rising desperation, until answering it is a matter of life and death.
- **The first line** *(Truby, fitted)*. A character's first words can carry their attitude in as few words as possible: Sundance's opening is a single cheeky word.
- **The seed in the first speech** *(Egri, fitted)*. Plant a character's end in their first words: the trait that will decide their last scene should already be audible when they first speak, as with Shylock's greed. This supplies half of the Bond theory's principle 7: a late act that surprises still has to be traceable to the first speeches. Test: set a character's first and last major speeches side by side. Can you see the second in the first?
- **Background in the first lines** *(Egri, fitted)*. One small, specific want or grievance, taken for granted by the speaker, can carry a family's circumstances. That is implication: the Implied World theory's delivery for surplus, in passing, used here for facts the story needs (`what-dialogue-does.md`, section 7).

**Owner link.** When a returning line poses an open question, as the puzzled question in *Butch Cassidy* does, its returns are emphasis, and in the Gap theory emphasis makes an open question a mystery the story must answer. A key word or tagline that asks nothing sets no such promise, though each return should still add meaning. If you mean a question to stay open, as a wonder, mention it in passing and stop pressing it; do not make it a tagline.

## 8. Narrators and inner talk

The Gap theory: prose's natural gap is between the telling and the truth, which means voice, inner life, and the slippage between what a narrator says and what we suspect. So in prose, voice is where the perspective gap lives. What a teller knows and may fairly hold back is in `.claude/skills/plot/references/telling-and-perspective.md`; this section is about how they sound.
- **A narrator who is a character has a listener** *(McKee, built)*. Decide their relationship with the reader: guide, witness, confessor, defendant, opponent. It sets register, sentence length and rhythm. The narrator of *The Museum of Innocence* guides the reader round his museum of a lost love in long, mournful sentences whose polish implies he has told it many times. Holden in *The Catcher in the Rye* is openly hostile to the reader and will not beg for sympathy (Truby's reading), which makes him seem honest by his own lights.
- **A first-person narrator cannot hear their own unsayable** *(McKee, built)*. Let it show through what they notice, praise or dodge, as with Stevens (`what-dialogue-does.md`, section 4).
- **Close third can borrow a voice** *(McKee, built)*. A narrator outside the cast can take on a character's favourite words and rhythms, as *Mrs Dalloway*'s narrator does with Clarissa's, and come near inner talk without switching to "I". Keep the borrowing deliberate. Interiority is built in here; it breaks when every thought is narrated.
- **Inner talk is a scene** *(McKee, built)*. A character talking to themselves, overheard. Treat the mind as a place with several selves who argue (critic, child, dreamer, conscience) and a quiet self who listens. Let the talk move by association, and leave spaces between the images where the unsayable can be glimpsed (the Implied World theory's productive gaps, at the scale of a mind). The strongest inner talk makes the effort to escape feed the trouble: in *Fräulein Else* every argument the heroine has with herself tightens the bind.
- **Know who is speaking to whom** *(McKee, definitional)*. A narrator outside the cast imitating a mind is not the same as a character's own "I" talking to themselves. Decide which; mixing them without meaning to blurs the voice.

What each medium allows (voice-over, soliloquy, looks to camera) is in `prose-stage-screen.md`.

## 9. Wit, and the comic voice

The Bond theory's Humour lever: we like good company, and charm can offset cruelty; it breaks when constant quips undercut the stakes, because you cannot fear for someone who takes nothing seriously. McKee's reading of *Lost in Translation* sharpens where the break is *(built)*: the actor mocks his own whisky advert, and the joke is how he admits his contempt for himself. Humour undercuts the stakes when it denies them, not when it is how a character admits them.
- **Character before cleverness** *(Egri, asserted but sound)*. If a brilliant line is not this person's, cut it or give it to someone whose line it is. Watch children and plain speakers for the writer's wit leaking into their mouths.
- **A joke needs a second job** *(Egri, fitted)*: reveal the speaker, push the fight, or plant something.
- **The comic mind** *(McKee, after Ben Jonson's "humours"; asserted, but checkable)*. A comic character is driven by one fixation they cannot see, and it colours nearly every line; the moment they see it, they turn dramatic. State your comic lead's fixation in a phrase, then look for it in their word choice: the snob's comparisons, the hypochondriac's nouns.

How jokes are built and timed, and comic exchanges as a kind of conflict, are in `line-and-scene-design.md`.

## 10. Finding a voice

- **Inside out** *(McKee, built)*. Picture what the character sees and feels at this moment, including what they will not or cannot say; then find the words; then judge how they land on a listener. The first phrase that comes is usually the generic one. See the thing first, then hunt for the words that make the reader see it *(McKee's reason, about which parts of the mind work in words and which in pictures, is asserted)*.
- **Ask the Magic If from inside** *(Stanislavski, by way of McKee; built)*. Imagine being this person, in this place, at this moment, and answer in the first person what you would do; then put the answer into their words. Answering as yourself makes every character sound like the writer.
- **Say it aloud.** Act the character before fixing the line, as Dickens is said to have done at his mirror *(McKee, fitted from a few writers)*.
- **Listen in depth** *(McKee, built)*. When you overhear real talk, write two columns: what was said, and what each speaker was doing with it. A good ear is depth of listening, not quick transcription.
- **A voice sheet**, the workshop's grouping of the above, one per main character: where their vocabulary comes from; whether they act on life or it acts on them; their usual qualifiers and hedges; their usual sentence shape; what they would never say, and to whom; their key words; how the voice changes under pressure.

## 11. Rivals

**Voice as the chief trait.** *McKee:* how a character talks is the single most important trait, and dialogue is the strongest tool for giving a story depth *(asserted)*. *The Bond theory:* traits, a voice included, make the relationship easier or harder to build; a distinct voice builds Recognise, and care needs the rungs above it, Understand, Side with, Worry and Miss. *What tells them apart:* take a character with a vivid voice and nothing to want or lose, a quotable sidekick. (a) Give them a want and something to lose and keep the voice. (b) Flatten the voice and keep the want and the loss. The theory predicts (a) raises care most, and (b) costs recognition but not worry. McKee's ranking predicts the voice change matters most. Readers quoting a character they never worry about is the theory's case.

**Comic distance.** *McKee:* laughter needs detachment, so comic characters carry fewer inner contradictions and the audience must never feel real pain *(asserted as near universal)*. *The Bond theory:* indifference is the enemy, and Worry is where care bites. *The workshop's reading:* a laugh can suspend Worry for a beat, but Comedy still needs its stakes, so the story's Worry must stay live. A joke that is how a character admits pain keeps the stakes; constant quips that deny them are the Humour lever breaking. Watching from a distance is not free either: laughing with a character keeps Side with, but looking down on them can block that rung. In a long run Comedy builds Miss; audiences grieve for sitcom families. How Comedy spends care as a genre is recorded in `.claude/skills/genre/references/genres/comedy.md`, sections 8 and 13; this section keeps the line-level test. *What tells them apart:* in a comic scene, let the lead's fixation cost them something real and lasting, and keep their voice as it is. McKee predicts the laughter stops at that point. The workshop's reading predicts it survives where the voice still admits the pain wittily instead of denying it. That is the prediction that tells the two apart, so this test sets McKee against the workshop's reading, not against the theory; the Bond theory predicts only that care deepens (something to lose builds Worry), which can happen whether or not the laughter stops.

## 12. Traps

- **Tics for a voice.** Catchphrases, dropped letters and verbal quirks that reveal nothing are Specificity breaking.
- **The writer's voice in every mouth.** Everyone equally witty, well read or cynical.
- **Dialect spelled out word by word.** A whole way of speaking written phonetically spends the reader's attention (the attention budget). A few chosen words and the rhythm usually carry it; that is the workshop's reading.
- **One voice for every pressure.** A character who sounds the same calm and cornered has a costume, not a voice.
- **A voice that changes for no reason.** A break from a character's normal marks an event; an unearned one breaks the bond.
- **The stock type's stock lines.** A minor character squeezed into their type (the gruff sergeant, the nagging mother) says what the type always says. List those lines and ban them.
- **Ordering care through voice.** Characters admiring someone's wit makes the audience resist; let the wit show.
- **Every voice at the same volume.** Minor roles can be plainer; the contrast is what marks the leads.
