# What dialogue does

*Sources: the Implied World theory (surface, implication and reserve; Part 2 section 3 on surplus mentioned in passing; section 4 on dumping the reserve onto the surface; the prose-and-film table's typical failures), the Gap theory (the story term, agents with desires; principle 1, nested inference; the knowledge and perspective gaps; principle 6 and section 5 on the teller), the Bond theory (the Desire and Interiority levers; care is social; care can't be ordered) and the Anticipation theory (principle 6, the knowledge position; characters' reactions cue ours); McKee, Dialogue (the wide definition, talk in and out of scenes, the three jobs, exposition, the said, unsaid and unsayable, text and subtext, activity and action, reported talk), Truby, The Anatomy of Story (subtext as a choice; open and covert moves; exposition carried by a character with a reason to ask) and Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing (dialogue's jobs; exposition as character revealed through conflict); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 Talk is action. 2 To whom a line is aimed. 3 The jobs a line does. 4 Said, unsaid, unsayable. 5 Text and subtext, and when to hide. 6 The words and the move. 7 Exposition in dialogue. 8 Reported talk. 9 Rivals. 10 Traps.

Terms as in `SKILL.md`: **dialogue** is any words a character says to anyone; a **line** is one character's go at speaking; a **move** is what a line does to its listener, named with an *-ing* verb; **subtext** is everything under the words.

## 1. Talk is action

**Case.** Darcy's first proposal in *Pride and Prejudice*. On the surface he asks Elizabeth to marry him. Under the asking he does several other things: he confesses a love he has fought against, he lists why it lowers him to feel it, and he plainly expects a yes. Elizabeth's refusal is a counter-move, and it arrives as accusations: that he parted her sister from Bingley, that he ruined Wickham. Each is a fact the reader half holds already, now fired as a weapon. The scene turns: he leaves having lost, and the letter he hands her the next morning is his reply. Nothing in the scene is information for its own sake. Every line is someone trying to get something.

**Point.** McKee's founding idea *(built, on the view that saying is a way of doing)*: talk is something a character does to get something, so the want and the move exist before the words, which only carry the move out. That gives two measures for any line, both his: has the scene moved on a beat, and has the speaker gained ground or lost it?
- **Fill three blanks** for any line: this character needs ___, so they are trying to ___, and this line does it by ___. A line that leaves the blanks empty is idle.
- **Mark each line plus or minus**: after it, is the speaker nearer to what they want, or further? A run of lines that leaves everyone where they started is a stalled scene.
- **Want before words.** McKee and Egri argue it from a mechanism *(built)*, and Truby's order of work assumes it: the want, the obstacle and the move come first, and the words last. If you are stuck on a line, go back a step. More on building a scene's beats in `line-and-scene-design.md`.

The owner theories hold this in place. In the Gap theory, story is agents with desires causing and suffering change; a line is one such cause, in small. In the Bond theory, wanting drives attention: a character who wants something from the person in front of them gives the audience something to want on their behalf.

McKee allows no purposeless talk at all *(built, but absolute: some aim can always be named afterwards, so it cannot fail)*. Use it as a stance, not a law. When a line seems aimless, look for its move before cutting. If the only move you can find is vague ("chatting", "explaining"), the line has none.

## 2. To whom a line is aimed

McKee widens dialogue to three directions: to another character, to oneself, and out to the reader or audience. It comes in two kinds.
- **In-scene talk** (McKee's *dramatised* dialogue): lines passed between people in one time and place, as if a fourth wall stood between them and the audience.
- **Out-of-scene talk** (his *narratised* dialogue): a character leaves the scene's frame and speaks straight to the reader, the audience or themselves. The soliloquy and the aside, the voice-over, the look to camera, the first-person narrator.
- **Inner talk**: a character talking to themselves, which the audience overhears (McKee's *inner dialogue*). Section 4 covers what it can put into words; `voice.md`, section 8, how it sounds.

Narration by a teller who is not a character is not dialogue in McKee's sense. The Gap theory still counts it as a teller with knowledge and bias (section 9).

**Case.** In *Fleabag* the heroine keeps turning to the camera. Each look is a move: she recruits the audience into her jokes and keeps them on her side, which is the Bond theory's point that talking to camera is one way film borrows Interiority, and buys alignment with it. In the second series another character notices her doing it, and the device itself becomes a turn: the one person she hides from has seen her hiding.

**Point.**
- **Out-of-scene talk is still a move** *(McKee, built)*. Name what the speaker wants from that listener: to inform, confess, justify, seduce or recruit. What a teller wants from the audience, and what they may fairly hold back, is in `.claude/skills/plot/references/telling-and-perspective.md`.
- **The listener shapes the words.** McKee rewrites one diary passage three ways (to the self, to the reader, as a scene with a friend) and the diction, sentence shape and tone all change *(built from one demonstration; the particular style links are illustrations, not rules)*. When a passage feels wrong, ask who it is really aimed at, and rewrite it for that listener.
- **There is no true monologue** *(McKee, built)*. A speech made alone still has a receiver: an object, an absent person, another part of the self. Name the receiver and what the speech is trying to do to it. An inner passage is a scene between two sides of one person, and one side should gain or lose ground by the end.
- **Out-of-scene talk moves the knowledge position.** A soliloquy hands the audience what the other characters do not know, which puts the audience ahead (the knowledge gap; `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`, section 6).

Long speeches and inner talk as craft are also in `flaws-and-fixes.md` (a speech with no reaction) and `prose-stage-screen.md` (what each medium allows).

## 3. The jobs a line does

McKee names three jobs *(asserted, as an organising frame)*: a line carries exposition, builds the speaker's surface personality, and acts. Egri's three are close *(built)*: prove the premise (the story's central claim), reveal the person, push the conflict. The owner theories point to jobs the books do not list. The workshop's list:

| Job | What the line does | Where the owner theories hold it | Remove the line, and... |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Move** | does something to the listener to get what the speaker wants | Gap: agents with desires cause change; Bond: Desire | the scene loses a beat, and the reply has nothing to answer |
| **Fact** | carries exposition, at the moment it changes something | Gap: plot as a schedule of knowledge; Implied World: show the tip | a later turn is unearned, or the audience is lost |
| **Voice** | shows who is speaking: what they know, how they see | Bond: Recognise, Specificity | the speakers blur |
| **World** | a detail taken for granted shows how things are here | Implied World: surplus, in passing | the world thins to set-dressing |
| **Knowledge** | shows what a speaker knows against what the audience knows: a lie, a feint, a slip | Gap: the knowledge gap; Anticipation: principle 6 | the audience loses its position, ahead, level or behind |
| **Cue** | a reaction tells the audience how to take someone | Anticipation: characters' reactions cue ours; Bond: care is social | a moment lands flat, or the audience cannot tell what it means |

A line doing no job is idle: cut it. A line doing one job well is fine. When two lines do the same job, removing either one alone loses nothing; keep the one that also does a second job, cut the other, and test the survivor again. A line doing several jobs is very hard to replace, which is what `hard-to-vary` means by a choice held in place (`.claude/skills/hard-to-vary/references/by-domain.md`, section 5). The jobs overlap (McKee's exposition is often done through a move), so the split is loose; it is a tool for the remove test, not a scoring sheet.

**A gauge for the World job.** Any line can be said to "show how things are here", so the World job needs a check or it excuses everything. Swap the detail for a neighbouring one from another place or time. If the implied world does not change, or the audience would have assumed the detail anyway (the baseline), the line is not doing the World job.

## 4. Said, unsaid, unsayable

McKee pictures a character's inner life at three depths *(asserted as a model of mind; a working frame, not a finding)*:
- the **said**: what they choose to voice;
- the **unsaid**: what they know and think, but keep back;
- the **unsayable**: what drives them that they cannot put into words, even to themselves.

The workshop reads this through the Implied World theory, by analogy: the same layers, applied to a person instead of a world. The said is the surface. The unsaid works like reserve, except that the character holds it, not the creator. The unsayable is like reserve hidden even from the one who holds it. The audience infers the lower two from the first, as it infers a world from its surface (the Gap theory's nested inference), and a person feels deep in proportion to how much the audience senses beneath what they say (the iceberg ratio). The unsayable is also a perspective gap turned inward: between what a person says about themselves and what is true.

**Case.** Stevens in *The Remains of the Day*, the Gap theory's own example. The said: a butler's account of duty and dignity, including the night his father dies upstairs while he serves downstairs, and his correct exchanges with Miss Kenton. The unsaid: what he half knows and steers round, his employer's politics and the chance he had with her. The unsayable: that he loved her, and that his loyalty was spent on the wrong man. He comes near to admitting it only once, briefly, near the end. His evasions are the story.

**Point.**
- **Write three notes for a key line**: what they say, what they are holding back, what drives them that they could not name. Check that the line lets an attentive audience sense the second and glimpse the third.
- **Who can state the subtext** *(McKee, built)*. A narrator outside the cast can simply tell the reader what went unsaid. A character narrating their own life cannot see their own unsayable, so it must show through what they notice, praise or dodge. This sharpens the Gap theory's principle 6: what a teller can reach sets what can fairly be told.
- **Can drama state the unsaid?** McKee: prose can state it, drama must imply it. The Bond theory is less strict: on screen Interiority is borrowed, through a voice-over, a confidant, therapy or a look to camera, and each of these can put the unsaid into words. Either way, a character who names their own deepest motive, accurately, strains belief (McKee's *overly perceptive* talk, in `flaws-and-fixes.md`), and Interiority breaks when every thought is narrated.
- **The unsayable shows in choices.** McKee's *Principle of Choice*: what someone chooses, at a cost, under rising pressure, shows what they could not say. More in `.claude/skills/character/references/building-a-character.md`, section 8.

## 5. Text and subtext, and when to hide

The **text** is the words spoken; the **subtext** is the thoughts, feelings, wants and moves under them, both unsaid and unsayable. McKee *(built)*: good dialogue hides a character's inner life from the others in the scene while letting the audience see through it. In owner terms that is a knowledge gap, with the audience ahead of the listener.

**Case.** *Jaws*, the night on the boat when Quint and Hooper compare scars. The text is boasting. The moves are sizing each other up, then offering a kind of fellowship between the old fisherman and the rich young scientist. Brody looks at his own small scar and says nothing, and that silence is a move too: he stays out of a contest he cannot win. Then Quint tells the story of the *Indianapolis*, and the game gives way to what drives him. The audience reads all three men at once; each man hears mostly the boast.

**Point.**
- **Read each important line twice** *(McKee, built)*: once as the listener takes it, once as the audience should. If the two readings match, the line has no second layer. If the first reading is not something this speaker would plausibly say to this listener, the surface is broken.
- **Subtext is a choice, not a virtue** *(Truby, built)*. People hide what they want because fear, pain or shame stops them saying it. McKee adds the ties a person cannot risk (a job, a marriage, a name), manners, and shared background (`voice.md`, section 4). Where nothing makes a character hide, ask one more question: is saying it a move that costs the speaker something with this listener, or a report of what the audience has already inferred? Let them say it when it is a costly move (section 9). A scene that needs the most open clash may want it said. A character talking round something for no reason in them is the writer being coy.
- **McKee ranks saying the inner life outright (*on the nose*) as the commonest and most harmful fault** *(built on his model of mind; that most of the mind is below awareness is asserted)*, and he exempts stories that deal in symbols rather than imitate life. Truby and McKee pull different ways here; section 9 has the owner theories' view.
- **Keep the burning topic out of the words** *(McKee, asserted from one case)*. The thing a character most needs to say and cannot, kept unspoken, bends everything else they say. In *A Raisin in the Sun* Ruth's secret never surfaces in the breakfast quarrel, and it weighs on every line.
- **For the most subtext** *(Truby, built)*: give several characters secret wants that clash, each pursued by a covert move (section 6). Then decide whether the audience is told the secret wants (ahead) or must infer them (level). That is the knowledge position, set scene by scene (`.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`, section 6).

## 6. The words and the move

McKee's pair: the *activity* is what can be seen or heard happening, such as talk over a meal; the *action* is what it does to someone, which this skill calls the move. Do not call a line finished before you can name its move.

**Case.** Take an invented line: "You're home early." Between a wife and a husband who has been lying about late meetings, it accuses. From a nurse to a patient's son, it warns that his father is worse. From a teenager to a parent, it complains that the house is no longer hers in the afternoons. To someone back from a job interview that went badly, it asks a question they will dodge. The words never change. The relationship, and what lies between the two people, set the move *(McKee, built)*. So when a line seems bland, do not reword it first. Decide its move, then make sure the relationship on the page points to that move.

**Point.**
- **Name each move with an *-ing* verb aimed at the listener**: pleading, cornering, flattering, stalling, testing. If you can only write "saying" or "explaining", the line has no move.
- **Name the move, not the manners** *(McKee, built)*. Polite people rarely give orders outright. A manager's "would you mind staying late?" is an order dressed as a question. Label the beat by what is done, and write the polite surface so the order is still readable under it.
- **Silence is a move** *(McKee, built)*. A daughter asked whether she will come home for Christmas who lets the question hang has answered it, and may have punished her mother as well. Ask of every pause whether it is a reaction to the move just made or a gathering for the next one; a pause that is neither is dead air. Placing pauses and silences is in `line-and-scene-design.md`.
- **Open or covert** *(Truby, built)*. A character can go after a want openly, saying what they want, or covertly, seeming to want one thing while after another. Truby's rule of thumb: an open move raises the heat and pushes people apart; a covert one lowers friction now and stores up a bigger clash for when the trick comes out. (His two outcomes leave out a third: the listener who sees through the trick and refuses.) A covert move hides the real want from the listener; whether the audience is let in on it sets the knowledge gap, and with it the knowledge position.
- **The move must fit the want** *(McKee, built)*. A move with no want under it sounds hollow. Words hotter than the want behind them are melodrama, in McKee's sense of behaviour bigger than its cause (`flaws-and-fixes.md`).

## 7. Exposition in dialogue

*Exposition* (McKee's term) is any fact the audience must be handed, out of the reserve, to follow the story and care how it comes out.

The owner frame comes first. The Implied World theory names exposition dialogue as film's typical failure, and two characters explaining to each other things they both already know as the reserve dumped onto the surface. Build as much reserve as you like; show the tip. When each fact should come out belongs to the plot's schedule of knowledge (`.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 5). This section is about getting a fact through a line without the line going false.

**Case.** Early in *Casablanca*, the police captain Louis questions Rick about his past as though it were part of his job of stopping Laszlo. The audience learns that Rick ran guns and fought for losing causes; Rick keeps insisting he was well paid for it, which stops him looking soft. The fact arrives inside a move (Louis probing, Rick deflecting), from someone with a practical reason to ask *(Truby, built)*.

**Point.** Ways to get a fact through dialogue without a dump:
- **Pass the honesty test** *(McKee's, built, in the workshop's sharper form)*. Does each speaker have a reason of their own to say this, to this listener, now, or is the line there only because the audience needs the fact? A hidden move is fine; most good lines hide one. A hidden author is not. When two people tell each other what both know, the only real reason in the scene is the author's (briefing the audience), dressed up as the characters' (fond remembering). Cut it, or give them a real conflict that makes the facts matter to them.
- **Give the fact to someone who lacks it and has a reason to ask.** An outsider learning the rules is the Implied World theory's guide for a world with many departures; Truby notes the guide can be a one-scene minor character, like the undertaker who drives the opening scene of *The Godfather*. Or a character with a practical reason to dig: an interrogation, an interview, a suspicious relative, with the person questioned deflecting.
- **Fire it as ammunition.** McKee's *exposition as ammunition* *(built)*: a character who already knows a fact uses it at a pivotal moment to win something. The weakest delivery is minor characters gossiping; the strongest is a character firing it at a climax.
- **Let it open a question** *(McKee's narrative drive, built)*. A fact that raises a question about what comes next is taken in while curiosity runs ahead. Once the reader of *Catch-22* grasps the rule that traps the airmen, they want to know how anyone escapes it, and they never notice they were told a rule.
- **Mention it in passing, taken for granted.** This is how the Implied World theory says surplus is best delivered: a detail the speakers have lived with all their lives, never explained, implies more than a paragraph would, as Heinlein's dilating door does. The same delivery works for a fact the story needs, though then it is implication, not surplus, since the detail has a purpose. Egri's case *(fitted)*: in *Awake and Sing!* one small want in a character's first lines lets the audience infer a family's poverty.
- **Keep revealing people through conflict** *(Egri, built)*. Exposition does not end after the opening. What someone does or refuses under pressure keeps showing who they are. Egri's stock devices (a servant briefing a new servant, a telephone call, a letter read aloud) are rarely needed when a clash can reveal the same thing.

**Drill** *(McKee, built)*. Take a scene in which two people recite shared history, and rewrite it twice with the same facts: once as a quarrel, one pressing the other into something unwanted, the facts as weapons; once as a coaxing, one gently steering the other with what they both know. Keep whichever hides the facts best.

**When plain telling is better.** In prose, a narrator can give background openly and fast, provided the sentences hold the reader; on stage and screen, exposition should pass unnoticed inside the spoken words *(McKee, built)*. More in `prose-stage-screen.md`.

## 8. Reported talk

Any medium can stage a past exchange or describe it. Described, it becomes reported talk (McKee's *indirect dialogue*): summarised, not quoted *(built)*.
- **Reported by a character**, it gives the audience that character's reading of the other person rather than the words. Whether the report is accurate hardly matters; the slant is the content, which is the perspective gap at work. A son telling his sister what their father said to the consultant shows how he sees the father, and what he needs his sister to believe.
- **Reported by a narrator**, it keeps the pace up and lets the narration state or hint at what lay under the talk. In owner terms, leaving the exact words out is a productive gap: the reader supplies the sound of the quarrel, and a quarrel heard in the reader's own head rarely sounds overdone.
- **Summary can still be showing** *(McKee's test, built)*: it is, if it happens inside a scene and changes the balance between the people in it.

**Move.** When a quoted quarrel reads as melodrama, try reporting it instead and let the rhythm of the summary carry the heat.

## 9. Rivals

**Does a third-person narrator have a voice?** *McKee:* a narrator who is not a character has a style, not a voice, and there is nobody behind it for the reader to feel for *(built from his definition, partly by fiat)*. *The Gap theory:* every teller, character or not, carries knowledge and bias, and the perspective gap between what the teller says and what is true is where irony and voice live. The workshop follows the theory and treats a narrator's slant as something to design. *What tells them apart:* take a third-person narrator who judges freely, as the narrator of *Pride and Prejudice* does, and let one of its confident judgements be proved wrong by later events. If McKee is right, readers take it as the author's slip, since there is no one there to be curious about. If the theory is right, readers take it as the teller's bias and start weighing everything else the narrator says: the perspective gap opens, and with it the irony of voice.

**May a character say what they feel?** *McKee:* characters who state their feelings in scene go dead; naming a feeling while in its grip drains it *(asserted as absolute; he exempts only stories that deal in symbols)*. *The Bond theory:* Interiority, access to how a character thinks, is the most powerful lever, and film borrows it through confidants and therapy, as in *The Sopranos*. *The workshop's reading:* stating a feeling works when saying it is itself a costly move made to a listener, and dies when it is a report to the audience. *What tells them apart:* take Tony Soprano's feelings as he states them to his therapist and move the same words into a family dinner where they cost him nothing and aim at no one. Read strictly, McKee's rule predicts both versions go dead, since in both the character names his feeling; the reading predicts only the dinner version does. McKee's own close reading of a *Sopranos* therapy scene finds a move under nearly every line Tony speaks, which fits the reading.

**Is small talk ever worth keeping?** *McKee:* chat with nothing under it should be deepened or cut, because the audience will search it for a meaning and give up *(built)*. *The Implied World theory:* surplus, detail that serves no purpose, is what makes a world feel real, and it works best mentioned in passing. *The workshop's reading:* both hold, for different things. A line the audience hears as dialogue usually wants a move, even when its surface is small talk; a detail inside it that takes the world for granted, and passes the World job's gauge (section 3), is surplus doing its job. *What tells them apart:* in a scene of weather chat, keep the chat but make the weather something only this world has, complained about the way we complain about drizzle. If the owner theory is right, the line now earns its place even with no move; if McKee is right, it still stalls the scene until someone is doing something with it. The workshop's bet is that it works best when it does both.

## 10. Traps

- **Hunting for a move afterwards to excuse any line.** "He's stalling" can be said of anything. The move must be specific and must fit this want in this scene.
- **Rewording a bland line before naming its move.** The move comes from the relationship; fix that first.
- **Demanding every job of every line.** A line that does one job well is doing its work.
- **Subtext everywhere.** Hide only what fear, shame, manners or a tie makes the character hide.
- **Letting a first-person narrator explain their own hidden motives accurately.** They cannot see their unsayable; let it show in what they notice and dodge.
- **The convenient newcomer.** An outsider who exists only to ask the questions the audience needs answered is the dump in disguise. Give them a want of their own.
- **Cutting all small talk.** Cut the chat that does nothing; keep the chat that shows how things are here, or give it a move.
- **Reading subtext as fact.** A book's confident reading of what a character really wants is one inference. The work may mean the question to stay open, as a wonder rather than a mystery (Gap theory, principle 5).
