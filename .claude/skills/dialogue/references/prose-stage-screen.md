## 1. Fix the medium before judging the talk

**Case.** One line, invented: a woman tells her brother she is fine, the day after a bad diagnosis.
- *On screen* the line can be almost nothing. The camera stays on her face a second too long, or finds her hand turning a cup, and the audience knows before her brother does. Film's natural gap, between what the audience knows and what a character knows, does the work.
- *On stage* her face is thirty feet from the back row. The actor can hold a pause the whole house will hear; the writer can give her a second line that overdoes the reassurance.
- *In prose* told in the third person, the narrator can say what she did not: that she practised the sentence in the car. Told in her own first person, she cannot see her own depths, so the lie has to show in what she notices, dwells on or leaves out.

**Point.** The same words do different work in each medium, so decide the medium before judging whether a line says too much or too little *(McKee, built from which sense each medium mainly addresses)*. If the writer has not said, ask. If you cannot, read script format as screen and prose as prose, and say so; where stage would change the advice (a held look, an aside), say what would differ. The owner theories give the frame. Prose builds by adding and film by taking away: on the page nothing exists until it is named, in the frame everything shows at once (Implied World theory). Prose's natural gap is between the telling and the truth, film's between the audience and the characters (Gap theory, section 5). In prose a voice is the fastest route to recognising a person, on screen a face, and Interiority is built into prose but has to be borrowed on screen (Bond theory).

## 2. What each medium hands the talk

| | Prose | Stage | Film | Television |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leads with | the reader's mind | the ear | the eye | the face, and talk |
| Tone, face and pause come from | the writer, who must describe them | the actor, at a distance | the actor, magnified | the actor, in close-up |
| Where subtext can live | a third-person narrator can state it or leave it; a first-person narrator can only let it show | under the words, carried by the actor | in the face; the camera can show what the speaker hides | as film, across many hours |
| Heightened speech | freest in the narration; quoted talk tends to be plainer | licensed by the shared pretence | only within a distinct world, a symbolic genre or a larger-than-life character | as film, and hard to keep up over a long run |
| Silence | costs a sentence, or white space | must be held, and the audience can look elsewhere | cheap and strong: the close-up | as film |
| Talk to the audience | first-person narration; address to "you" | soliloquy and aside | voice-over; a look into the lens | voice-over; a look into the lens |
| Typical failure | the info-dump; quoted talk more ornate than the narration | a soliloquy that repeats what the scenes show | exposition dialogue; lines that sound written | a way of speaking that drifts across the run |

The first row is McKee's *(fitted; broad tendencies)*. The info-dump and exposition dialogue are the Implied World theory's typical failures for prose and film; the rest joins the owner theories' tables with McKee's cases.

## 3. Stage

**Case.** *Richard III* opens with Richard alone, telling the audience what he is and what he means to do, and inviting it to enjoy the plan with him. When he then woos a widow over the coffin of her father-in-law, a man he killed, the audience watches as his accomplice, knowing what she does not.

**Point.**
- **Theatre is for the ear.** On stage the words lead and the picture follows, and the stage's shared pretence (everyone agreeing to treat live actors as the people they play) lets talk be loftier or rawer than anyone's real speech *(McKee, built)*. Do not flatten stage talk to screen plainness by reflex. The pretence holds only while the world stays consistent and each character keeps to their own way of speaking *(McKee, built)*. In Implied World terms, by the workshop's reading, the stage's shared pretence is part of the baseline (the habits of the form, as genre habits are), so heightened speech is not the costly departure there that it would be on screen, but any invented style still spends attention (section 11).
- **Keep the soliloquy and the aside for what can be said to no one in the story**: a confession, a secret, a true wish *(McKee, asserted from theatre practice)*. One that repeats what the scenes show is dead. What it hands the audience is knowledge the other characters lack (the knowledge gap, with the audience ahead) and access to a mind (the Interiority lever). Richard's opening buys alignment on purpose: the Bond theory's point that alignment beats virtue, used as a weapon.
- **A lone speaker is still in a scene.** In a one-person play the figure splits into selves and plays scenes between them, and even reminiscence works best as an act with a purpose (burying the past, talking oneself into a lie) *(McKee, built)*.
- **A stage narrator is a device or a character, and should not drift between them.** Decide whether it wants anything *(McKee, asserted)*. If it does, its talk is dialogue and needs a move. If not, it is a guide: in owner terms, the kind of outside aid the Implied World theory says a world with many departures needs.
- **Cues matter most here.** On the page a pivot word buried mid-speech, where it cannot cue the reply, is a small fault; in performance it breaks the rhythm (`flaws-and-fixes.md`, section 5). A song is talk in character too: test it like a speech, for its want, its move and its turn *(McKee, built from his wide definition)*.

## 4. Film

**Case.** The coin toss in *No Country for Old Men*. A killer makes the owner of a petrol station call heads or tails without telling him what rides on it. The words are few and ordinary, small talk about the weather and the man's marriage turned slowly into an interrogation. The threat lives in the pauses, the coin on the counter and the actor's stillness, which the camera enlarges until the plain talk is hard to bear.

**Point.** Start from the owner tables. Film builds by taking away: everything in the frame exists at once, so texture, crowds, objects and sound come cheap, and abstraction (institutions, rules, history) comes dear (Implied World theory). Its natural gap is between the audience and the characters (Gap theory); its fastest route to recognition is a face, and Interiority has to be borrowed (Bond theory). Screen talk earns its place by doing what the frame cannot.
- **Let the frame carry the world.** Film fails when talk tries to carry what prose carries cheaply: institutions, rules, history. That is exposition dialogue, the theory's typical film failure. Put the world in the frame and explain little, as *Children of Men* and *Mad Max: Fury Road* do.
- **Screen talk must sound unrehearsed** *(McKee, built)*. The camera enlarges a false note until the audience hears the writer behind the actor. Read each line aloud and ask whether this person could come out with it, unprepared, right now.
- **When richer talk is allowed** *(McKee, fitted)*: a world whose own limits license it, a symbolic genre whose audience expects stylised speech, or a larger-than-life character set against plainer ones (`voice.md`, section 6). Keep a short style sheet for any distinct way of speaking and hold every scene to it. In Implied World terms an unusual way of speaking is a departure (the theory names a slang as one), so it spends attention the plot also needs; designing it is `story-world`'s work.
- **Write what an actor can play** *(McKee, built)*. Mark any line knotted in its syntax, over-explained, or naming an emotion instead of giving the actor one to play.
- **Let the face and the object work.** Replace plain yes and no lines with a look, leave room for the actor, and ask of each spoken move whether a deed could make it instead *(McKee, built)*. The close-up is film's cheapest silence.
- **Earn a long speech with a situation that expects one**: a trial, a toast, a eulogy *(Truby, fitted)*. Otherwise film's rhythm fights it (`line-and-scene-design.md`, section 9).
- **Expect the words to change** *(McKee, fitted)*. On a film or television set the lines often shift in performance, and an actor who is stalling tends to echo the other's last words back as a question.

## 5. Television

**Point.**
- **Television leans on the face and on talk** *(McKee, built, though partly tied to the screens and budgets of his time)*. The Bond theory's table puts the face as the screen's fastest route to recognition; in a medium of close faces, give each face something to hide.
- **Time makes the deepest bonds.** Dozens of hours with a character are why television bonds run deepest (Bond theory, books-and-film table), and many of those hours are spent in talk. A way of speaking the audience knows well builds the Recognise rung, and later the Miss rung.
- **Hold the way of speaking across the run.** Keeping an unusual but believable style of speech steady over many episodes is hard *(McKee, fitted; he names *The Wire* as a series that managed it)*. Audit each episode against the style sheet.
- **Comic leads need a fixation that lasts.** A lead's recurring blind spots thread through a long series and give each episode something to test *(McKee, fitted from* Frasier*)*.
- Egri, writing when television was new: the principles are the stage's, with more sets, cutting at will and fewer characters, and the first exchange should already hint at the coming conflict *(asserted)*.

## 6. Prose

**Case.** Hemingway's "Hills Like White Elephants". A man and a young woman wait for a train at a station in Spain and talk, almost nothing but quoted lines, around an operation neither will name. The narrator never enters either mind and states nothing. The reader has to build what is at stake from dodges, repetitions and one bitter joke. Prose can stage talk as bare as a play; it simply chooses to less often.

**Point.** Start from the owner tables. Prose builds by adding: nothing exists until it is named, so history, law, belief and inner life come cheap, and every sensory detail costs a sentence (Implied World theory). Its natural gap is between the telling and the truth (Gap theory); its fastest route to recognition is a voice, and Interiority is built in (Bond theory).
- **Choose how each exchange reaches the page** *(after McKee, built)*: quoted, reported, as inner talk, or folded into the narration (free indirect style). With no actor in between, every option is open; do not default to quotation marks.
- **Quoted talk tends to be plainer than the narration around it** *(McKee, fitted, with exceptions admitted)*. Prose writers spend their richest language in the narrating voice. If a character's quoted lines are more ornate than the narration, ask whether that is the character or the author showing off.
- **Report talk when summary serves** (`what-dialogue-does.md`, section 8).
- **Who can state the subtext.** A third-person narrator can tell the reader what went unsaid; a first-person narrator cannot see their own depths, so their subtext can only show *(McKee, built)* (`what-dialogue-does.md`, section 4). Decide in third person, line by line, whether to state or to leave; in first person, never let the narrator explain their own hidden motive accurately.
- **Describe voice and body, sparingly.** Actors supply tone, face, gesture and the distance people keep; on the page each must be named, and one precise, figurative detail (a man who keeps smoothing a tie he is no longer wearing) often carries what the words hold back *(McKee, built)*. The Implied World theory gives the price: in prose every sensory detail costs a sentence.
- **Mix quoted talk with the narrator's asides.** Short quoted lines, with the narrator's asides carrying the heat and telling the reader what the other character does not know, make a small knowledge gap inside one exchange *(McKee, fitted)*.
- **Close third can borrow a voice** (`voice.md`, section 8).

## 7. Talk aimed outside the scene

**Case.** *The Wolf of Wall Street*. Jordan Belfort tells his own story in voice-over, then in the middle of a scene turns to the camera, starts to explain how one of his share deals works, then breaks off, telling us we would not follow it anyway. The move is contempt and intimacy at once, and it recruits us to his side of the fraud.

**Point.** Talk aimed at the audience or at oneself is still a move with a listener (`what-dialogue-does.md`, section 2). What changes by medium is what it can do.
- **Each medium has its door.** On stage, the soliloquy and the aside; on screen, the voice-over and the look into the lens; in prose, first-person narration and address to "you". Inner talk is rare on screen, common on stage and the very stuff of first-person prose *(McKee, fitted)*. On stage and screen the actor carries the unsaid silently unless the writer borrows a route (voice-over, a confidant, therapy, talking to camera), which is the Bond theory's point that Interiority is borrowed on screen.
- **Name what the voice wants from the audience**: to inform, confess, justify, seduce or recruit. A look into the lens is usually a move to win the audience over *(McKee, fitted)*; in Bond theory terms it buys alignment.
- **Set a voice-over against its pictures.** Two questions settle most of its design: does the audience trust it, and does it agree with the pictures or pull against them *(McKee's catalogue, fitted; more in `.claude/skills/plot/references/telling-and-perspective.md`, section 6)*. A voice-over that only says what the pictures show is doing no job.
- **A dull voice-over has no perspective gap to work in.** Narration that only strings events together hands the audience the story twice and leaves nothing to infer between the telling and the pictures, which is the bullet above seen from the other side: the perspective gap needs some distance between what the voice says and what is true or shown (Gap theory). Open one somewhere. Between the voice and the pictures: in *Badlands* Holly's flat, dreamy narration runs under a killing spree, and the mismatch is the meaning (McKee's term for this is *counterpoint*, which he rates highest, *fitted*). Inside the voice: let the narrator argue with themselves. Inside the telling: let one small remembered exchange play out in the middle of it *(McKee, built)*.
- **Enact it now or report it later.** Inner conflict told in the present puts the audience inside the struggle; told later, it brings reflection, irony and mood, with hindsight between the event and the telling *(McKee, built as a sorting)*. The later telling opens a perspective gap between the self then and the self now.

## 8. Exposition, medium by medium

- **Prose can tell openly.** A novelist can set out background as plainly and at such length as the sentences can hold the reader *(McKee, built)*; direct telling is fast, folding a life into a clause. On stage and screen, exposition should pass unnoticed inside the spoken words, because every spoken line must also be a character's move *(McKee, built)*. So judge prose exposition by whether the sentences hold, and dramatic exposition by whether anyone notices it.
- **Film's shortcuts, in small doses.** A title card, an opening crawl or a place-and-date caption trips the story for a moment, and it moves on; newspaper headlines, a radio that happens to announce what the audience needs, and crammed montages are the same shortcut in disguise *(McKee, built)*. The Implied World theory holds both sides: a crawl or a glossary can be the outside aid a world with many departures needs, and title crawls sit on its list of film's typical failures. The test is the dose, and whether a guide inside the story (an outsider who learns alongside us) could do it instead.
- **What never passes** in any acted medium is two characters telling each other what both know (`flaws-and-fixes.md`, section 2).

## 9. How much talk

**Case.** *Mad Max: Fury Road* against *12 Angry Men*. The first is a chase across a desert; its conflicts are with distance, machines and an army, and it needs very few words. The second is twelve men in a hot room deciding a young man's fate; every conflict runs between people, and almost nothing but talk carries it.

**Point.** The level of the conflict predicts the amount of talk *(McKee, fitted; a tendency to question, not a law)*. McKee names four levels, physical, social, personal and inner. What each tends to ask of talk, keyed to this section's case:

| Where the fight is | Talk it tends to ask for | In the case |
|---|---|---|
| against the world and the body | the least | *Fury Road*'s chase |
| against an institution | guarded, public talk, rising to set speeches | *12 Angry Men* while the jurors act as a jury |
| between people close enough to wound | the most, in exchange | *12 Angry Men* once the jurors' own grudges come out |
| inside one mind | much, but in thought, narration or soliloquy | a first-person novel, not this pair |

Use it to diagnose: an action story heavy with dialogue, or a family drama carried only by images, may be using the wrong tool for its conflict.

- **On screen, genre sets the balance**, from near-silent survival stories to films that are one long conversation *(McKee, fitted)*.
- **Weigh picture against word, and word against silence**, scene by scene *(McKee, asserted, as a question to keep asking)*. The Implied World theory supplies the rule the question lacks: each medium fails when it tries to do what the other does cheaply. Film fails when talk tries to carry what prose carries cheaply, institutions, rules and history, which is exposition dialogue (section 4); prose fails when it tries for sensory density, which costs a sentence each.
- **Too much talk is usually a symptom** of a scene where nobody changes position *(Egri, built)*: `flaws-and-fixes.md`, section 5.

## 10. Moving talk between media

**Case.** In the novel of *Gone Girl*, the wife's diary entries run as chapters of her own first person. The film turns them into voice-over laid over scenes of the marriage, and when the diary is revealed as invented, the pictures turn out to have been lying along with the voice. The adaptation kept the device by moving it from the page's perspective gap into the screen's voice and image.

**Point.**
- **A camera cannot film thought**, so a novel's inner talk cannot simply cross to the screen. List everything the narrator conveys that no character says aloud, and for each item find a scene, a deed or an exchange that dramatises it *(McKee, built)*.
- **Every open gap becomes a decision.** Filming a book forces a choice on every gap the author left open, which is why adaptations often feel smaller than the book (Implied World theory). For talk, the choice is among voice-over, a confidant, a reaction shot, or picture set against sound.
- **Adjust the density.** Stage talk is the most ornamented, screen talk the most compressed, prose talk the most varied in form *(McKee, fitted)*. Moving a play to film usually means cutting and plainer words; moving a film to the stage usually means giving the voice more to do.

## 11. Rivals

**How far does the stage's pretence stretch?** *McKee:* the shared pretence seems to have no outer limit, provided the world stays consistent and the characters keep to their own way of speaking *(built for the pretence; fitted to avant-garde examples for the missing limit)*. *The Implied World theory:* every departure, a heightened or invented way of speaking included, spends attention the plot also needs (the attention budget). Both can hold at once: accepted, but costly. *What tells them apart:* stage one scene twice for a new audience, once in a dense invented idiom and once in plain speech, with the same events. If the audience believes in both equally but follows the plot of the first less well, the owner theory's cost is real and consistency alone is not enough.

**Is Horror a genre of stylised speech?** *McKee:* he lists Horror among the symbolic genres whose audiences accept stylised, on-the-nose talk *(asserted; his list is loose)*. *The workshop's reading of the Anticipation theory's residue* (fear outlasts the story when the threat could exist in the audience's own world and something stays unresolved): a threat that could exist in the audience's world may be undercut by speech no one in that world uses. The theory itself says nothing about how characters talk. *What tells them apart:* two versions of one Horror scene, one with stylised dialogue and one with plain, the events and the open ending unchanged. If the plain version leaves more fear behind afterwards, the workshop's reading holds for that kind of Horror; if not, McKee's grouping does. This tests the workshop's reading against McKee, not the theory. It likely depends on the kind: gothic and folk Horror may welcome heightened talk that a haunted-suburb story cannot afford.

**Does a narrator who is not a character have a voice?** Recorded in full in `.claude/skills/plot/references/telling-and-perspective.md`, section 8; the workshop follows the Gap theory and treats such a narrator's bias as something to design.

## 12. Traps

- **Judging talk before fixing the medium.** A line too thin for the page may be exactly right under a close-up.
- **Screen plainness on stage by reflex, or stage rhetoric on screen.** Each medium licenses a different height of speech.
- **A soliloquy or voice-over that repeats the scene.** Out-of-scene talk earns its place by giving what no one in the scene could say.
- **Characters who talk in the narrator's images.** In prose, keep the richest language where it belongs, unless the character is that kind of speaker.
- **A first-person narrator who explains their own subtext.** They cannot see it; let it show.
- **Describing every gesture.** Each costs a sentence; choose the one that carries what the words hold back.
- **Adapting the words instead of the telling.** Moving lines across media without moving what the narration did leaves the new version hollow.
- **A long run that drifts.** A way of speaking the audience learned in the first season should not wander by the fourth without a reason.
