# Line and scene design

*Sources: the Anticipation theory (principle 7, the ratchet; the time term; principle 6, the knowledge position; the suspense equation; its questions for a scene), the Gap theory (principle 4, scheduling, at the scale of a sentence; the withholding and knowledge gaps; principle 2, the breach, and principle 7, settlement, at the scale of a scene), the Implied World theory (the attention budget; productive gaps; the iceberg ratio; surplus) and the Bond theory (care can't be ordered; care is social; the Humour and Agency levers); McKee, Dialogue (where a sentence's pivot word falls, economy, the pause and silence, beats and turns, the six shapes of scene conflict in his case studies, how jokes work, the long speech), Truby, The Anatomy of Story (the scene as a funnel ending on its weightiest line, the verdict on a character's nature, the three layers of dialogue, moral dialogue and the opponent's case, the tagline, the monologue as a small story) and Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing (building inside a speech, the frozen duel, the sermon); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 The scene comes before the lines. 2 Beats, and how the talk climbs. 3 The turn and the last line. 4 Six shapes of conflict, as moves. 5 Comic exchanges. 6 Three layers in one exchange. 7 Shaping a sentence: where the pivot word falls. 8 Economy, the pause and silence. 9 Long speeches. 10 Rivals. 11 Traps.

Terms as in `SKILL.md`: a **line** is one character's go at speaking, a **speech** a long one; a **move** is what a line does to its listener, named with an *-ing* verb; a **beat** is one move and the reaction it draws; the **scene want** is what a speaker is after in this scene; the scene's **value** is what it puts at stake, named as a pair of opposites (trust and suspicion, alone and close), its **charge** is where the value stands at a given beat, and the **turn** is the beat where the charge changes; a sentence's **pivot word** is the one its sense hangs on, while **key words** are Truby's repeated words (section 6). Building the scene itself (its place in the order, the event it holds) is `plot`'s work, in `.claude/skills/plot/references/scenes.md`; this file is about the talk inside it.

## 1. The scene comes before the lines

**Case.** The opening scene of *Glengarry Glen Ross*. Shelley Levene, an ageing salesman on a losing streak, wants the good leads; Williamson, the office manager, holds them and will not hand them over. Levene tries one move after another: he recalls his great years, he pleads, he loses his temper, he offers a cut, then cash. Each refusal forces a new move, so the scene climbs even though Williamson hardly moves at all. It turns when the plea becomes a bribe and the manager names a price, and it lands when Levene cannot pay it there and then. Few of the lines would mean much quoted alone. What holds the scene is the want, the wall and the run of moves, all fixed before a word was written.

**Point.** Fix four things for each speaker before drafting. McKee and Egri put the words last and argue why *(built: a line is the outward form of a want meeting resistance, so the want and the resistance come first)*; Truby's order of work assumes it.
1. **The scene want, and the larger want under it.** McKee's check *(built)*: grant the scene want and the scene should end at once; if it would not, you have named something else. If the scene want and the larger want are the same for every speaker, nothing lies under the talk for an actor or a reader to find.
2. **What stands in the way, and what the speaker thinks stands in the way.** In McKee's model of how a speech forms, the speaker senses the obstacle before choosing a move *(built; a model of behaviour, not a finding)*. That sensing is where irony comes cheapest: a speaker who misreads the obstacle while the audience reads it rightly opens a knowledge gap inside one exchange.
3. **What they would never say to this listener, here.** The ties a speaker cannot risk decide what stays under the words (`voice.md`, section 4).
4. **Open or covert.** Going after the want openly raises the heat; seeming to want one thing while pursuing another smooths things now and stores up a larger clash (`what-dialogue-does.md`, section 6).

The books' scene checklists leave out two questions the owner theories ask of every scene: does the audience care yet about what could be lost, and does it know more than, the same as, or less than the speakers (the Anticipation theory's questions for a scene)? Ask both before the lines.

## 2. Beats, and how the talk climbs

**Case.** An invented scene. A mother wants her grown son to move home and care for his failing father; the son wants to get out of the kitchen without saying no. The value is duty against freedom.

| Beat | Mother | Son | Charge |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | hinting | missing the hint | freedom |
| 2 | flattering (he was always the patient one) | joking it away | freedom |
| 3 | hinting again, harder | missing it again | freedom |
| 4 | shaming (his sister visits every week) | counter-accusing | freedom, under strain |
| 5 | confessing (she can no longer lift his father) | falling silent | duty |
| 6 | asking outright | agreeing, and hating it | duty |

Beat 3 is beat 1 in new words, so it is not a beat at all: cut it, or change the move. Beat 5 is the turn, a revelation, and the son's silence is his reaction. The table shows the scene's shape before a line is polished.

**Point.** Name each beat as an *-ing* pair, one verb for the move and one for the reaction, and read down the list *(McKee, built)*.
- **A pair that repeats is one beat** *(McKee)*, however new the words, as beat 3 shows. Turning a pair round, so that the dodger presses and the presser dodges, is still no new beat. Only a new move makes one. When a scene reads well and still feels dead, look for this first.
- **Tension rises in steps, with partial releases, and its pattern breaks before the audience learns it** (Anticipation theory, principle 7, the ratchet; *tension* as the plot skill defines it, the felt level of suspense or fear at a given moment: `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`, section 7). Tension that only rises goes numb. McKee and Egri would have every beat top the last *(asserted; see section 10)*. McKee's own beat list for a long scene in *The Sopranos* has an easing beat before its climax *(fitted)*, which fits the ratchet better than his rule.
- **Lift before a drop.** A brief high just before a downturn makes the fall land harder *(McKee, fitted)*.
- **Starting the beats is not steering the scene.** Mark who opens each beat and, separately, who decides where it goes. McKee *(fitted from one scene)*: a speaker who mostly responds can still steer where a beat goes. In Bond terms the one who steers is acting, not only reacting; whether a mostly silent steerer earns the Agency lever's respect is untested.
- **Offer an easy way out, and have it refused.** Put a cheap exit on the table (a polite excuse, the late hour) and let the speaker turn it down; the refusal shows how much they want what they came for *(McKee, fitted)*.
- **When one value is spent, hand the scene to a deeper one.** Once the first value has fully swung, a deeper value can take over, as pride gives way to winning and losing *(McKee, fitted)*.
- **The larger want can hijack the scene.** A line that touches a speaker's lifelong wound can switch what they are chasing in the middle of the scene *(McKee, built on the two layers of want)*.

## 3. The turn and the last line

**Case.** The opening of *The Social Network*. In a noisy bar Mark talks fast at his girlfriend Erica, and every topic slides back to the exclusive clubs he wants to get into. Each time she tries to follow him, he belittles her; each time, she gives him another chance, with less patience. The scene turns when she ends the relationship, and it closes on her verdict: not on what he did tonight, but on what kind of person he is. The film then holds that verdict open for two hours, and in its last scene another woman contests it, telling him he is not what Erica called him, only trying hard to be.

**Point.**
- **Label each turn: a deed or a revelation.** McKee's two kinds *(built)*: someone does something that shifts the balance, or something comes out that shifts it, and a spoken line can be either. If you can name neither, compare the charge at the first and last beats; if it has not moved, the scene is a nonevent (`flaws-and-fixes.md`, section 5).
- **Write the last line first.** Decide the line the scene exists to reach, check that the scene want is settled on it and not a beat earlier, then make everything before it narrow towards it. Truby's image for this is a funnel *(built on emphasis by position, then fitted to examples)*. In owner terms this is settlement at the scale of a scene: how the scene ends gives it its shape.
- **At the peak, a character may say who the other really is.** Truby's shift from doing to being *(built)*: a quarrel about an act becomes a verdict on a nature, and the verdict prompts the audience to judge for itself. It sits uneasily with the Bond theory's limit that care can't be ordered (section 10). Save it for scenes that carry the story, and decide whether the story will bear the verdict out or overturn it.
- **A scene can have two movements.** When a speaker's want is beaten outright mid-scene, let them fall silent, regroup and take up a new want with its own turn, then give the scene a short tail to let the tension down *(McKee, fitted)*.
- **Two values can move in opposite directions**, so that closeness rises while hope sinks, and the ending carries an irony no one states *(McKee, fitted)*.
- **Open on a small upset.** A scene works best set off by a small event of its own that tips it off balance *(McKee, built)*: by analogy, a breach at the scale of one scene (Gap theory, principle 2).

## 4. Six shapes of conflict, as moves

McKee's case studies show six shapes a conversation's conflict can take *(fitted to the cases he chose; he does not show the list is complete)*. The shape sets the tone of the talk: clipped between equals, oblique when the fight is indirect, sparse when it is quiet. If every scene in a draft has one shape, vary some. Each shape below is given as moves, with a case of the workshop's choosing.

**Balanced: equals who both press.** In *Heat* a detective and the thief he is hunting sit down for coffee, trade confessions about their lives, then tell each other plainly that neither will back off. Moves: give both the same strength, pass control back and forth, and make every concession cost the one who makes it. Guard against Egri's frozen duel *(built)*: clever thrusts that neither side can win, so the talk goes nowhere and the people harden into types. Each exchange must gain or lose someone ground.

**Comic: a mind locked on one thing.** See section 5.

**Lopsided: one presses, one dodges.** Levene and Williamson (section 1). Moves: give the presser active verbs (pressing, charming, accusing) and the dodger evasive ones (deflecting, soothing, withdrawing); let the turns land on the presser; when the dodger yields a little, have the presser read it as progress and switch move *(McKee, fitted)*.

**Indirect: the fight goes through someone else.** In *Hamlet*'s play scene, Hamlet teases Ophelia and needles his mother while his real target is the king, watching actors perform a murder like his father's. Moves: when manners or danger close the direct route, ask who else is present who could carry the message, and what act in front of them would carry it *(McKee, fitted)*. Or route the fight through a *third thing* both care about, an object, a memory, a subject they both know (McKee's *trialogue*; `flaws-and-fixes.md`, section 4).

**With oneself: the self split in two.** At night in *The Two Towers*, while the hobbits sleep, Gollum and Sméagol argue over whether to betray them. Moves: name the two sides and what each wants, and let one gain ground by the end *(McKee, built)*. For the deepest version, make the character's efforts to get out feed the trouble, so that brooding on an absence sharpens it (McKee's *reflexive* conflict, *built*). The voice of inner talk is in `voice.md`, section 8.

**Minimal: pressure from a lack, not from an opponent.** In *Brief Encounter* the lovers have their last minutes together in a station café, and a chattering acquaintance sits down and will not stop talking; he leaves for his train with only a touch on her shoulder. Nobody fights anybody. Moves: let earlier scenes teach the audience what each person lacks, then let a few plain words, or none, carry it *(McKee, built)*. In owner terms the scene is still full of suspense: the threat is small but real (the goodbye that cannot be said), and it is multiplied by care built over the whole film, by doubt that anything will be said, and by a train that is due.

## 5. Comic exchanges

**Case.** "The Germans", in *Fawlty Towers*. Basil, concussed and desperate to be correct with a party of German guests, has been told on no account to mention the war. Every effort to avoid it steers him back to it, and every effort to cover the slip makes it worse. The scene climbs by sinking: from a stray word, to open mockery, to a goose-step across the dining room.

**Point.** McKee's account of how comic talk works, regrouped under three jobs of the workshop's own. His short list of comic techniques (clarity, exaggeration, timing, incongruity) is spread across them.
- **Build the charge.** Lock a comic character on one fixation they cannot see in themselves (McKee's *blind obsession*, after Ben Jonson's comic "humours"), so that chasing what they want is exactly what keeps it from them *(built)*; the comic mind as a voice is in `voice.md`, section 9. Or give two characters the same want over something only one can have: the credit, the blame, the last word *(fitted)*. Every joke stores energy in its setup and lets it out in its payoff, and the energy is mostly fear, hostility and desire *(built on a release model of laughter; the three sources are asserted)*, so a weak laugh usually means a thin setup rather than a weak punch line. By analogy, the build and release has the shape of the ratchet.
- **Release it.** Land the laugh on the sentence's pivot word, placed last with nothing trailing (section 7), and leave room for the laugh. Make the response wildly out of scale with its cause, too big or too small. Pile up grand things and finish on the pettiest *(built)*; Egri's rule of putting the strongest item last, turned upside down, is the same device. A comic scene can also spiral down through levels of attack, each beat lower than the last, with a laugh at each step *(fitted)*; after heavy threats, an absurdly small blow can pop the tension.
- **Keep the distance.** The audience must grasp at once that mischief is afoot, even before it knows what; pity and muddle both kill the laugh. Name the scene's core mismatch, the two things that do not belong together, in one line before writing it.

How far Comedy must hold back care is a rival recorded in `voice.md`, section 11; the Bond theory's Humour lever and its limit, that constant quips undercut stakes, are in section 9 there.

## 6. Three layers in one exchange

**Case.** The lunch scene in *Jurassic Park*. On its surface the scientists whose approval the investors need are giving their verdict on the park: that is the talk carrying the story. Under it runs an argument about how to live: the mathematician accuses the park's makers of taking a power they never earned, and the owner answers with a case of his own (he has done what no one has done before, and nobody would object if it were a lost bird brought back). And a phrase the mathematician uses on the tour, that life will find its way round every control, comes back later in another man's mouth when the first hatched eggs are found.

**Point.** Truby's *symphonic dialogue* runs three layers through the same lines *(built by analogy with music; a sorting scheme more than a mechanism)*. Most weak dialogue uses only the first.
1. **Story talk:** the speakers' moves on the story's main line of action. Check that someone wants something and someone resists; talk without resistance is only exposition.
2. **Moral talk:** argument over whether a deed is right, and under it over how to live. Tie it to one concrete act the speakers can fight over, so it stays personal *(Truby, built)*. Make the opponent's case strong enough that a reader half agrees, and leave one crack in it *(Truby, built)*: an opponent with reasons is a person, which is the Bond theory's point that a villain fails as a function. To place moral talk, ask who in this story has standing to object to what the hero does, and at what moment *(after Truby)*. Egri's caution *(built)*: a protest has to suit the one who makes it and the thing that set it off, or it turns into a sermon; often the bare facts of the situation make the point better than a speech would.
3. **Key words:** a word, line or sound said more often than people normally would, in changing situations, until it carries the theme. A *tagline* is one line that returns many times with a new sense each time *(Truby, built and fitted)*. In *The Princess Bride* the farm boy's two-word reply to every order sounds like obedience, turns out to have meant love all along, and the recognition scene lands on it. Pick three to five theme words and track every return; each should come in a new context. A character's own key words: `voice.md`, section 7.

Truby drafts in layers, story talk first *(asserted; a workflow)*; marking in the margin which layer each line serves is the useful part.

## 7. Shaping a sentence: where the pivot word falls

**Case.** One warning, shaped three ways (invented).
- *End-loaded:* "Before you sign anything, ask who else has a key to your flat." The sense waits for the last words.
- *Front-loaded:* "Someone else has a key to your flat, has had for months, and you never once asked." The point comes first and the rest piles up, like talk still being thought.
- *Centre-loaded:* "For a careful man you've been generous with your keys, and it will cost you." The judgement sits in the middle, set up on one side and weighed on the other.

**Point.** Every sentence hangs on its **pivot word**, the one its sense depends on (McKee's *key word*; this workshop keeps "key words" for Truby's repeated words, section 6), and where that word falls sets the sentence's job *(McKee, built)*. McKee calls the three shapes the suspense, cumulative and balanced sentence. This workshop keeps "suspense" for the Anticipation theory's sense (section 10), so it says end-loaded, front-loaded and centre-loaded.
- **End-load** what must land: a threat, a reveal, a punch line, a line that must cue the reply. Holding back the pivot word keeps the listener attending to the last word; it is, by analogy, a withholding at the scale of a sentence (Gap theory, principle 4). Its risk *(built)*: held too long, the listener has to carry too many pieces, and the delay shows.
- **Front-load** what should sound unplanned: memory, description, easy talk, an afterthought.
- **Centre-load** a judgement that needs setting up and then qualifying. A *parallel* pair, two phrases of matching shape, marks a before and after or a contrast.
- **Mix them.** One shape repeated sounds mechanical; talk that grips and still sounds unrehearsed needs a blend, with the end-loaded sentences placed where the scene must land *(McKee, built)*. An image at the end of an end-loaded sentence can cap a speech.
- **End each speech on its cue.** A listener reacts only once they catch the sense, so a speech should close on its pivot word, which then works as the cue for the reply. If the pivot word comes early, the other actor has to wait out the rest, and the rhythm breaks in performance *(McKee, built)*. Watch for phrases trailing after it.
- **Build inside a speech.** In a list, put the strongest item last *(Egri, built)*.

How sentences shorten or lengthen as a speaker's pressure changes is in `voice.md`, section 5.

## 8. Economy, the pause and silence

**Case.** *There Will Be Blood* runs for many minutes before anyone speaks: a man alone in a mine shaft, a broken leg, a crawl to an assay office, a well, a death. When he finally talks, it is a smooth pitch to a room of townspeople, and after all that silence the polished voice reads at once as a tool he picks up to get what he wants.

**Point.**
- **Economy** is the most meaning for the fewest words, not short lines: every word must earn its place *(McKee, after Strunk and White; asserted)*. Remove words one at a time and keep any cut that makes the line land harder. The owner theories supply the reason McKee leaves implicit: spare words leave room for inference (Implied World theory, productive gaps and the iceberg ratio), and spare words save attention the plot needs (the attention budget). A word of surplus, a detail taken for granted that makes the world feel real, earns its place by doing that.
- **Pause before the turn, and just after it.** A wordless beat before the turn holds back the audience's curiosity and concern and lets them break on the turn; one after it gives the change time to sink in *(McKee, built)*. In owner terms a held beat stretches the time term.
- **Ration pauses.** A scene that keeps stopping to signal weight teaches the audience to ignore the signal, and the pause fails where it is needed *(McKee, built)*; the ratchet says the same of any learned rhythm. Count the pause marks in a scene and keep those tied to a turn.
- **The pause for thought.** A small hesitation before a line (a lead-in word, a glance away) gives the audience room to sense what goes unsaid *(McKee, built)*. Watch that it does not harden into a tic.
- **Silence is a move**; test each pause as in `what-dialogue-does.md`, section 6.
- **Could the scene play without words?** Imagine it done only in looks, gestures and deeds, then keep only the lines the silent version cannot replace *(McKee, asserted)*. Replace plain yes and no lines with a nod or a look, above all on screen *(McKee, built)*.
- **A reaction to a reaction** (a joke answered by a laugh, the laugh by a smile) can mark two people drawing close *(McKee, fitted from one scene, and loose: the same chain can mark mounting hostility)*.

What silence costs in each medium: `prose-stage-screen.md`, section 2.

## 9. Long speeches

**Case.** Jules's Bible speech in *Pulp Fiction*. The first time, in an apartment, it is a ritual before a killing, and nobody thinks about its words. At the end of the film, in a diner, he says it again with a gun on a frightened robber, then stops and wonders aloud what it means, tries two readings, and settles on a third that changes what he will do next. The same words become a small story with its own turn, and the robber's face, listening for his life, keeps the audience with every line.

**Point.**
- **Make a long speech a small story.** Give it a want, something that resists it, a turn, and its weightiest line last *(Truby, asserted and fitted)*. A speech alone with one's own thoughts is a fight inside one person, so it can lay a mind bare; the Bond theory's Interiority lever breaks when every thought is narrated, so spend it sparingly.
- **No speech runs without a listener.** A speech that pours out with no reaction and no change of charge turns its speaker into a mouthpiece *(McKee, built)*. Mark where the listener reacts, even silently, and where the speaker adjusts. The listener's face steers the audience too: characters' reactions cue ours (Anticipation theory), and care is social (Bond theory).
- **Know the length you are asking for.** Two minutes of talk runs to roughly three hundred words *(McKee, fitted; a rule of thumb)*.
- **Find a situation that expects a speech**: a trial, a eulogy, a briefing, a toast. The length then needs no excuse, and the public case can secretly trace the speaker's private story *(Truby, fitted)*.
- **Two long speeches can face each other** when each side argues a whole way of life and personal enmity keeps it hot; without the enmity it is a lecture *(Truby, asserted and fitted)*.
- **For a character who never stops talking**, give the impression of endless talk without running on, and build the scene around the listeners *(McKee, fitted)*.

The flaw, a speech no one hears, is in `flaws-and-fixes.md`, section 4.

## 10. Rivals

**Is a held-back pivot word suspense?** *McKee:* suspense is curiosity loaded with concern for someone, and it works at every scale down to one sentence *(asserted, as a definition)*. *The Anticipation theory:* suspense is care times threat times uncertainty times time, so with no threat there is none; and the Gap theory, after Sternberg, keeps suspense (about the future), curiosity (about the past) and surprise apart. By analogy in owner terms, an end-loaded sentence is a tiny withholding, and a punch line is surprise. *What tells them apart:* the threat term, which McKee's definition lacks. Take one end-loaded sentence spoken to a listener the audience cares about equally in two scenes, and move its pivot word to the front in both. In one scene the held word brings a threat (the brakes have been cut); in the other it brings news with nothing at risk (the flat is theirs after all). McKee predicts an equal loss, since concern and curiosity are the same in both. The owner theory predicts a loss mainly where there is a threat.

**Always topping, or a ratchet?** *McKee:* every beat should top the last; *Egri:* every scene should hit harder than the one before *(both asserted)*. *The Anticipation theory:* tension that only rises goes numb; rise in steps with partial releases, and break the pattern before the audience learns it. *What tells them apart:* write a key scene twice, once climbing without a break and once with one easing beat before the last rise. If the second lands harder, the ratchet holds. The books' own cases lean the owner's way: the easing beat in McKee's *Sopranos* list, and the third act of Ibsen's *Ghosts*, which Egri praises for restarting lower before its final climb. General form: `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`, section 11.

**A verdict on a character, against "care can't be ordered".** *Truby:* at a scene's peak, one character's judgement on another's nature deepens the scene and prompts the audience to judge *(built)*. *The Bond theory:* telling us a character is brilliant or kind, or having others praise them, makes audiences resist; they believe what they see. The workshop reads a hostile verdict the same way, since it too is others telling rather than the audience seeing; that extension is the workshop's reading, not the theory's. They meet if the verdict is a move inside the scene, contested or possibly wrong, rather than the story's own ruling. *What tells them apart:* follow a verdict scene with two versions of the next one, one in which the story simply bears the verdict out and one in which the character does something the verdict did not predict. If the audience's interest in the character is stronger in the second, the Bond theory's limit is doing the work; if the first plays as well, Truby's device survives even when endorsed.

## 11. Traps

- **Polishing lines before the beats exist.** The words cannot supply a want the scene lacks.
- **Beat labels that are verbs of speech.** "Saying", "explaining" or "asking" names no move.
- **One shape for every scene.** A draft of nothing but open quarrels, or nothing but quiet ones, goes flat by repetition.
- **Treating the six shapes as the full list.** They are fitted to McKee's chosen cases.
- **End-loading everything.** Talk made only of held-back pivot words sounds built, not spoken.
- **Pauses as punctuation.** A pause not tied to a turn teaches the audience to ignore the next one.
- **A tagline repeated in the same situation.** It gathers nothing and goes stale; each return needs a new context.
- **A long speech with a silent, blank listener.** Give the listener a face that changes.
- **The verdict the story rubber-stamps.** A judgement handed down and simply confirmed is the author ordering care.
