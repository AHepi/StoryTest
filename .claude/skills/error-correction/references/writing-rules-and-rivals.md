# Writing rules and rivals: the metric where the workshop makes its claims

Open this when you write or change a rule in a skill (a method step, a quick version, a row of a diagnosing table), a rival entry, or a worked example, and when you write a new module.

Contents: 1 Why here. 2 The rule card. 3 The rival form. 4 Worked examples. 5 A new module. 6 Traps.

## 1. Why here

**Case.** The plot skill used to say that a fact a twist depends on should be shown "at least twice" before it. Swap "twice" for "three times" and the rule read just as well. Its module tied the idea to a book's reasoning about weighting facts, but nothing held the number, and writers were given it as method. What was held was "noticeable before the turn" (a set-up the turn pays off), and the rule now says that, with no number (correction C12). The first rewording then made a second error: it gave the bomb under the table as grounds that one showing is enough, and added "not so much that the turn is guessed". The Gap theory uses the bomb for both schedules: shown first, it gives suspense, and the audience waits for it; simply going off, it gives surprise. Walking the shown bomb through the rewording showed the caution was wrong for a suspense turn; walking the unshown bomb through it (which both theories call a surprise) showed that a bare threat's arrival needs no set-up. A later rewording then filed that bomb under the Anticipation theory's Shock without saying the name is open (owner question S13), and was caught by walking the theories' own cases again. Another defined the bare arrival as one "that rests on no fact shown earlier", which let dragons from nowhere burn a village in a world like ours; walking that case showed the line is what a first-time audience can make sense of from what it already has; the rule now also says, as the workshop's reading, that a threat deciding the climax needs its set-up however it arrives. The rule now says how much emphasis depends on whether the turn is meant to surprise or to be waited for.

**Point.** Hard to vary was applied to writers' stories, and never to the workshop's own rules. The errors that did the most damage were made when rules and rivals were written. A book rule was stated as law. A theory's own example failed a rule written from a book. A rival's test could not tell the two views apart. Walking the theory's own cases through the rule before it went in would have caught them. So the metric runs at the moment a rule or rival is written, and its result stays visible in the text.

## 2. The rule card

For every rule you write or change in a skill, four steps. Do them in your head or in the review receipt. The result shows in the rule's wording and tags.

1. **What does it rest on?** Say which, and label it in the text:
   - an owner theory's principle or section, cited (in hard-to-vary's words, a dependency);
   - a book, with *fitted* (drawn from past stories), *built* (argued from how the effect works) or *asserted* (just stated), which is its provenance: where it came from, not a proof;
   - the workshop's reading, labelled, with its owner question number if there is one.

   Resting on something is not yet being held. A rule whose only support is "a book, asserted" rests on an assertion, and an assertion holds nothing in place; steps 2 to 4 say whether the rule is held.
2. **Remove it, and mark it.** What would a writer do differently without it, and in which situation? Remove it alone, and together with its neighbours in the same section: two rules can each be removable alone but not together. If something is lost, it is *held*, by that job: say which. If nothing, even in groups, it is *idle*: cut it (hard-to-vary's `by-domain.md`, section 8, on written instructions). Use hard-to-vary's six marks (held, held if, two routes, loose, idle, unknown); "free" is not one of them.
3. **Swap its specifics.** Replace any number, list or example with a near neighbour, and ask whether the swapped rule still does every job on the theory's cases. If it does, the specific is *loose*. Write what is actually held instead ("noticeable before the turn"), or, if the looseness is harmless, say so plainly ("twice is a rule of thumb"; hard-to-vary's `by-domain.md` calls a harmless loose part *free*).
4. **Walk the theory's own cases through it.** Take the cases the owner theory itself names (the bomb under the table, Joffrey, *Psycho*, "the door dilated", *Chronicle of a Death Foretold*). First say what each case is a case of, in the theory's own words, since one case can serve more than one point (the bomb is the theory's case of both suspense and surprise). Then walk each through the rule. If one of them comes out wrong under the rule, the rule contradicts the theory.

Do not mass-edit the existing rules to apply the card. Apply it to rules in any section you touch for another reason, and record what it found in that change's review receipt.

## 3. The rival form

When a book's view differs from an owner theory, the skill follows the theory and records the book's view as a rival (CLAUDE.md). A rival entry:

- **The theory's side**, stated with its source line.
- **The book's side**, stated fairly, with where in the book it is argued, and whether it is fitted, built or asserted.
- **One outcome**, on one story change, where the two predict differently.
- **Each side's prediction** on that outcome. Check that the two predictions cannot both come true. If they can, the test tells the views apart no better than no test.
- **Status:** *untested*, or *run on <case>: <what happened>*.
- **One home.** Each rival lives in one place. Other files point to it. Copies drift.

When a kept case or a real draft is run, check which recorded rivals it can serve, and add the result to their status. A failed prediction shows only that the side's rule, how the case was read, and how the case was chosen cannot all be right (foundation, Part IX, K3); which to change is a new guess. The theory stays frozen. A result against it goes to the owner as a question.

## 4. Worked examples

A worked example shows a rule at work, so it must be a case where the rule could have come out otherwise. It must never be a kept case, a trial's conclusion, or the owner's draft under test: an example drawn from the material a test will run on gives that test its answer in advance. One of the Catch critique's lessons was that the skills' examples came from an earlier draft of the same script.

## 5. A new module

A new module is a new explanation. Build it with hard-to-vary's `building.md`:
- start from a real case;
- write the jobs before the parts;
- note what was cut;
- walk the parts through earlier cases.

Put every rule through the card and every rival through the form. Give the module a row in its skill's map and a node in the diagram. Then get it reviewed by theory-checker and use-tester.

## 6. Traps

- **A number that looks like method.** If "twice" could be "three times", say what is held.
- **A book rule dressed as the theory's.** A book idea that agrees with a theory is still the book's. Credit the workshop only for linking them.
- **A rival that both sides pass.** Both predictions on one outcome, or it is not a test.
- **A straw rival.** State the book's view as its author would, or the theory is never really tested.
- **An example borrowed from a test.** It turns the test into an answer key.
