# Owner terms: a sheet for checking a changed passage

The workshop's commonest error has been drift in the owner's terms: a word used in a neighbouring word's sense, a claim credited to the wrong theory, or a theory narrowed or turned round. The same pairs kept going wrong. Each block below takes one term and gives the theory it belongs to, the theory's own words, the neighbour it gets confused with, and one test that comes out differently for the two.

**How to read it.**
- The four owner theories in `sources/` are the authority. Words in curly quotes followed by a place in brackets are the theory's exact words (never more than 15). Plain quotation marks ("like this") mark examples, workshop phrases and names, never the theory's exact words. Everything else is a summary.
- *The workshop's reading* means how the skills read a point the theory leaves open. It is not the theory's own claim. Where the owner has been asked, the question number is given (Q1 to Q8, S1 to S13, in `22 Questions - meanings only you can settle.md`). While a question is open (its status in that file says so), keep the skills' labelled stopgap reading (the one they use until the owner answers), and never write it as settled; once it is answered, the answer governs.
- *Owning skill* names the craft skill whose Words paragraph defines the term for use. (A Words paragraph is the paragraph near the top of a skill's SKILL.md that fixes what each word means.) Where no Words paragraph defines it, the block says where the term is used.
- H numbers (H5, H7 and so on) point to errors in the workshop's error history, so a reviewer can see what went wrong before. H23 is the general case: one word used in several senses across the skills.

## 1. Promise, guarantee, expectation

### 1.1 Promise
**Owner:** Gap theory (`gap-theory-of-narrative.md`), principle 5 Promise; Part 2 section 4.
- **Own words:** “Every open question carries a promise, and emphasis is what sets it.” (Gap, Part 2 section 4)
- **In plain words:** an open question the story presses (lingers on, has characters chase, keeps bringing back) is promised an answer; one mentioned in passing and never pressed is promised to stay open. Breaking either promise feels like betrayal (principle 5).
- **Not to be confused with:** a guarantee (1.2); an expectation (1.3); the Bond theory's promise (1.4).
- **Telling test:** If it went, would the audience feel *cheated* (a promise) or *unsafe* (a guarantee)? And where did it come from: set by emphasis inside this story (the theory's promise), or brought by the genre before page one (the workshop's stretch)?
- **Open:** for open questions, settled by the theory. Stretching *promise* to the moments a genre owes and to set-ups is the workshop's reading, Q7. The name *expected mystery* is S8.
- **Owning skill:** plot (`plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 2) for the theory's own sense, open questions set by emphasis; genre (Words: *promise*, *expected mystery*) for the workshop's stretch to the moments a genre owes, Q7. The cheated-or-unsafe test is the workshop's, in `genre/references/how-genres-work.md` section 2.

### 1.2 Guarantee (and the theory's "contract")
**Owner:** Anticipation theory (`anticipation-theory.md`), principle 5 Broken contract; Part 2 section 5.
- **Own words:** “Genres carry unspoken guarantees: the star survives, the child is safe, the dog lives.” (Anticipation, Part 2 section 5)
- **In plain words:** a protection the audience assumes, so part of it relaxes. The principle's name, *contract*, means this set of protections: “Fear needs the audience to believe the genre's usual protections are gone.” (Anticipation, principle 5). It is not a promise to answer anything. Break one early and convincingly and every later threat is real.
- **Not to be confused with:** a promise (1.1); the bond as a promise (1.4).
- **Telling test:** cheated or unsafe, as in 1.1. A second test catches the reading turned round (H14): after the break, *could* anyone die (the aim of the principle), or *do* characters die all the time? Only the second makes care collapse: “Break one guarantee convincingly rather than all of them.” (Anticipation, Part 2 section 5)
- **Open:** early breaks, settled by the theory. A guarantee broken at the end giving weight or grief is the workshop's extension, S5.
- **Owning skill:** genre (Words: *guarantee*).

### 1.3 Expectation
**Owner:** not an owner term. The theories use *expect* in two ways, and the workshop adds a third.
- **Own words:** “They come from what the audience expects *might* happen.” (Anticipation, central claim) And the genre's habits are part of the baseline: “the real world plus the habits of the genre” (Implied World, Key terms, Baseline)
- **In plain words:** in the Anticipation theory, to expect is to predict what might happen, the fuel of suspense. The Gap theory's “what breaks from the expected” (Gap, Part 2 section 2), from Bruner, means breaking the world's normal. In the workshop, an *expectation* is a genre habit that is neither owed nor a protection (a battle at the climax, the suspects gathered); overturning it is a twist.
- **Not to be confused with:** a promise; a guarantee.
- **Telling test:** Overturn it. Cheated: it was a promise. Unsafe: a guarantee (grief, if it is broken at the end, is the workshop's extension, S5). Surprised but satisfied: an expectation, and the change is a twist.
- **Open:** the workshop's reading; not yet put to the owner (nearest is Q7).
- **Owning skill:** genre (`references/how-genres-work.md` section 2; not in the Words paragraph).

### 1.4 The bond as a promise
**Owner:** Bond theory (`bond-theory.md`), The limits of care.
- **Own words:** “The bond is a promise.” (Bond, The limits of care)
- **In plain words:** a writer may hurt, change or kill a beloved character, but it has to mean something for *that character*. Killing one only to motivate someone else, which the theory names “fridging” (Bond, The limits of care), breaks it.
- **Not to be confused with:** the Gap promise (about an open question); a guarantee (the star survives).
- **Telling test:** Kill the beloved character. If the death means something for them, the bond promise is kept even though a guarantee broke. If it only drives someone else's story, the bond promise is broken, whatever the guarantees.
- **Open:** settled by the theory. How it relates to the Gap promise is not said (section 13).
- **Owning skill:** character (`references/change-and-growth.md`; not in the Words paragraph).

## 2. Departure, breach, pressure

### 2.1 Departure (and the baseline)
**Owner:** Implied World theory (`implied-world-theory.md`), Key terms; principle 1 Minimal departure. The Gap theory's departure gap (reality against the world) is the same difference seen as a layer.
- **Own words:** “any way the fictional world differs from the baseline” (Implied World, Key terms, Departure)
- **In plain words:** a standing difference from the baseline, which is “what the audience assumes by default” (Implied World, Key terms, Baseline): a magic system, a political order, a ruined climate, a slang. It is true on an ordinary day before the story starts.
- **Not to be confused with:** the breach (2.2); pressure (2.3). The Gap theory calls the breach a second level of departure (2.2); on its own, *departure* means the first level, the world against reality.
- **Telling test:** Is it true on an ordinary day before anything happens (a departure), or does it happen against that day's normal (a breach)? *Dune*'s shortage of water is a departure; a rich single man moving into Netherfield is a breach. A dragon attack is a breach in our world; in a world full of dragons it might be weather. A water shortage or overpopulation does not become the breach by being dramatic (H16).
- **Open:** settled by the theory. That a scene of the world at its own business "changes the departure gap" is the workshop's reading, Q1 (b); never state it as the theory's (H27).
- **Owning skill:** story-world (Words: *baseline*, *departure*); genre (Words).

### 2.2 Breach (and the world's own normal)
**Owner:** Gap theory, principle 2 Breach; the five gaps table; Part 2 section 2.
- **Own words:** “A story is a departure from the world's *own* normal.” (Gap, principle 2)
- **In plain words:** what happens that breaks the normal of the fictional world, departures included. The normal is the world's (“what's possible, normal and valued” (Gap, Terms, World)), not the audience's baseline. The breach produces stakes and a reason to tell the story.
- **Not to be confused with:** a departure; a broken guarantee (the Anticipation theory's broken contract); a broken promise.
- **Telling test:** Measured against what? Against the audience's real world plus the genre's habits: a departure. Against the fictional world's own everyday: a breach.
- **Open:** settled by the theory.
- **Owning skill:** plot (Words: the five gaps).

### 2.3 Pressure
**Owner:** Gap theory, principle 3 Pressure; Part 2 section 2.
- **Own words:** “A world shows itself best through what it does to people under strain.” (Gap, principle 3)
- **In plain words:** a way of showing the world, through what it does to someone under strain rather than through explanation. The theory's case: we learn what Gilead is from what it does to Offred, not from a history lesson. It is not a kind of gap.
- **Not to be confused with:** the breach (an event); a departure (a difference); tension (10.1); the everyday phrase "under pressure".
- **Telling test:** Replace the scene where a rule bears down on a character with a paragraph explaining the rule. The departure and the breach are unchanged; the pressure is gone.
- **Open:** settled by the theory. Q1 (b), open, lets a world-business scene stand if it shows the world "under some pressure".
- **Owning skill:** no Words paragraph defines it. story-world cites it in its Built on paragraph and uses it in Building.

## 3. The senses of "gap" (Q2)

**Owners:** Implied World theory (Key terms; principle 7 Productive gaps); Gap theory (central claim; the five gaps; Part 2 sections 3 and 4); Anticipation theory (the fear ladder; Part 2 section 8).
- **Own words:**
  - Something left open on purpose: “what nobody fills in, left open on purpose for the audience.” (Implied World, Key terms, Gap)
  - One of five layers: “a narrative is a stack of gaps” (Gap, central claim): departure, breach, withholding, perspective, knowledge.
  - One open question: “a gap about the future” (Gap, Part 2 section 3); “Each rung closes one gap” (Anticipation, the fear ladder)
  - The Gap theory joins the first and the third: “This is where worldbuilding's gaps and plot's gaps meet” (Gap, Part 2 section 4)
- **Not to be confused with:** each other; the reserve, which the creator knows (11.2).
- **Telling test:** Can you name the two things it lies between (the audience and the characters; story order and plot order)? Then it is a layer, and it is named in full: *the knowledge gap*. Is it one thing the audience wants to know, which a fact could settle? Then it is an open question. Is it something nobody fills in, left open so the audience's imagination works on it? Then it is the Implied World gap. A second test: fill it in. If the world shrinks (Bombadil explained, the Force turned into a blood test), it was left open on purpose; if the audience feels paid, it was an open question the story had pressed.
- **In use:** say which sense at first use, and never let *gap* slide from one sense to another inside one argument.
- **Open:** Q2 (now (a): keep both senses, and each skill says which it means). Whether a gap never mentioned at all counts is put to the owner under S13 (section 13, item 3; plot, Words).
- **Owning skill:** story-world (Words: the Implied World sense); plot (Words: the layers and the single open question).

## 4. The knowledge gap and its neighbours

### 4.1 The knowledge gap
**Owner:** Gap theory, the five gaps table; question 3 of "Five questions a writer can ask"; Part 2 section 5.
- **Own words:** “What the audience knows and what characters know” (Gap, the five gaps table, Knowledge row)
- **In plain words:** the audience is always one side and the characters the other. It produces dramatic irony and dread (for *dread*, see section 7).
- **Not to be confused with:** what one speaker knows against another (a difference between characters, not one of the five layers) (H7); the perspective gap; withholding; the knowledge position.
- **Telling test:** Is the audience one of the two sides? "Kate knows about the debts and Tom doesn't" is not yet the knowledge gap. "The audience knows about the debts and Tom doesn't" is.
- **Open:** settled by the theory.
- **Owning skill:** plot (Words); dialogue (Words: keeps the theory's meaning).

### 4.2 Its neighbours
- **Perspective gap:** “What happened and what the teller says” (Gap, the five gaps table, Perspective row). **Test:** does the gap close when a character learns the truth (knowledge), or only when the telling is corrected (perspective)? The theory sets them apart by medium: “Film's natural gap is between audience and character” (Gap, Part 2 section 5); “Prose's natural gap is between the telling and the truth” (Gap, Part 2 section 5).
- **Withholding gap:** “Story order and plot order” (Gap, the five gaps table, Withholding row). **Test:** is the fact missing because the plot hasn't told it yet (withholding), or because a character doesn't have it (knowledge)? One scene can hold both.
- **Knowledge position:** “This is the Gap theory's knowledge gap aimed at fear” (Anticipation, Part 2 section 6). Owner: Anticipation principle 6, which picks, scene by scene, whether the audience is ahead of, level with or behind the characters. **Credit:** the three positions are the Anticipation theory's; the gap is the Gap theory's.
- **Open:** settled by the theories. **Owning skill:** plot (Words; the positions in `references/suspense-and-fear.md` section 6); dialogue (Words).

## 5. Price and cost (Q4)

**Owners:** Implied World theory, principle 2 Attention budget and Part 2 section 1 (price as attention); Gap theory, principle 2 and Part 2 section 2 (what things cost the characters).
- **Own words:**
  - Price as attention: “Each one has a price, because every new rule, name or custom uses attention” (Implied World, Part 2 section 1)
  - Cost as stakes: “The world decides what counts as an event, what characters want and what things cost.” (Gap, principle 2)
  - The Gap theory also says *price* for stakes, once: “its stakes feel arbitrary because nothing sets the price” (Gap, Part 2 section 2)
- **Not to be confused with:** each other. Watch two more: the Bond theory's Agency lever breaks when a character “never pays a price” (Bond, the ten levers, Agency), the stakes sense; and the Implied World theory's one-line summary, “make every difference cost something” (Implied World, In one line), which can be read either way.
- **Telling test:** Who pays? The audience, in attention, to learn it: the *price*. A character, in what the world makes them give up: the *cost*. Cutting a paragraph of explanation lowers the price and leaves the cost as it was.
- **Credit:** that the world sets what things cost is the Gap theory's, not the Implied World theory's (H20).
- **Open:** Q4 (now (a): *price* for attention, *cost* for stakes, marked as the workshop's).
- **Owning skill:** story-world (Words: *price*, *cost*); plot (Words: *cost*).

## 6. Mystery, wonder, strangeness

### 6.1 Mystery and wonder (two kinds of open question)
**Owner:** Gap theory, principle 5 Promise; Part 2 section 4.
- **Own words:** “Every open question is either a *mystery*, which the narrative promises to answer” (Gap, principle 5); “A gap mentioned in passing and never pressed is a wonder.” (Gap, Part 2 section 4)
- **In plain words:** emphasis decides which it is. Lingered on, chased by characters, brought back: a mystery, owed an answer. Mentioned in passing and never pressed: a wonder, owed silence. Break a mystery's promise and you get betrayal (*Lost*); break a wonder's and you get what the theory calls *deflation* (the midi-chlorians).
- **Not to be confused with:** other uses of *mystery* in the theories (the Implied World theory says “the Force went from mystery to blood test” (Implied World, What it predicts), where the Gap theory would say wonder; the Bond theory's Interiority lever uses it in the everyday sense); a Detective story; the workshop's *expected mystery* (S8).
- **Telling test:** Does the story press it? If characters chase it and it keeps coming back, it is a mystery and must be answered; if not, it is a wonder and must stay open. Tom Bombadil stays a wonder because “the book never makes solving him anyone's goal” (Gap, Part 2 section 4). Or answer it: if the audience feels paid, it was a mystery; if the world shrinks, it was a wonder.
- **Open:** the distinction is settled by the theory. The word *wonder* is Q5 (6.2); *expected mystery* is S8.
- **Owning skill:** story-world (Words: *mystery*, *wonder*, for facts about the world); plot (Words); genre (Words: *expected mystery*).

### 6.2 Wonder the feeling, and strangeness (Q5)
**Owner:** Gap theory, the five gaps table (what the departure gap produces).
- **Own words:** “Wonder, strangeness” (Gap, the five gaps table, Departure row)
- **In plain words:** the table gives *wonder* as a feeling the departure gap produces. Principle 5 gives *wonder* as an open question left open. The workshop keeps *wonder* for the open question and says *strangeness* for the feeling.
- **Not to be confused with:** a wonder in principle 5's sense (6.1).
- **Telling test:** Can the audience ask it as a question? A question left open on purpose is a wonder; the feeling that a world is unlike ours is strangeness. Answering a wonder deflates it; strangeness has nothing to answer.
- **Open:** Q5 (now (a)).
- **Owning skill:** story-world (Words for *wonder*; *strangeness* in its Built on paragraph).

## 7. Dread, the suspense of helplessness, dread by proxy (Q6)

**Owners:** Anticipation theory, the fear ladder and principle 6 (Part 2 section 6); Gap theory, the five gaps table (Knowledge row).
- **Own words:**
  - The dread rung: “Something is wrong, but not what” (Anticipation, the fear ladder, Dread row)
  - What the knowledge gap produces: “Dramatic irony, dread” (Gap, the five gaps table, Knowledge row), with Hitchcock's bomb, where the audience is ahead.
  - Audience ahead: “suspense of helplessness. We fear *for* the character” (Anticipation, Part 2 section 6)
  - Audience behind: “(the character sees something we can't): dread by proxy” (Anticipation, Part 2 section 6)
  - Audience level: “shared terror. We fear *with* the character” (Anticipation, Part 2 section 6)
- **Not to be confused with:** each other; the terror rung (8.1); shock (11.4).
- **Telling test:** Who knows more? Nobody knows what the threat is: the dread rung. The audience knows and the character doesn't: the suspense of helplessness (the Gap table's *dread*). The character knows and the audience doesn't: dread by proxy. Both know the same: shared terror. Change one thing: show the audience the killer in the closet, and a dread-rung scene becomes the suspense of helplessness.
- **Nearby:** *residue*, the ladder's last row and principle 8: “Fear outlasts the story when the threat could exist in the audience's own world” (Anticipation, principle 8). Whether a monster meets this through what it stands for is S4.
- **Open:** Q6 (now (a): *dread* for the rung only; *the suspense of helplessness* for the audience ahead). Write *dread by proxy* in full for the audience behind.
- **Owning skill:** plot (`references/suspense-and-fear.md` sections 2 and 6; the plot Words paragraph does not define these).

## 8. Horror the genre, the horror rung, horror the word

### 8.1 The horror rung (and terror)
**Owner:** Anticipation theory, the fear ladder; principle 2; Part 2 section 2.
- **Own words:** “Ann Radcliffe split terror, which expands the mind, from horror, which freezes it.” (Anticipation, Part 2 section 2)
- **In plain words:** terror is “It's here, but unseen” (Anticipation, the fear ladder, Terror row); horror is “They see it” (Anticipation, the fear ladder, Horror row). Reaching the horror rung uses up imagination, so what is shown must be worse or stranger than what was imagined.
- **Not to be confused with:** Horror the genre (8.2); horror the everyday word (8.3); dread (section 7).
- **Telling test:** Is the threat seen? Seen: the horror rung. Present but unseen: terror.
- **Open:** settled by the theory.
- **Owning skill:** plot (`references/suspense-and-fear.md` section 2).

### 8.2 Horror, the genre
**Owner:** no theory defines the genre. The Anticipation theory writes it in lower case (Part 2 sections 3 and 4).
- **Own words:** “horror is a world whose rules turn against the people living in it” (Anticipation, Part 2 section 4, framed in Implied World terms)
- **In plain words:** the workshop's naming rule (genre, Words): a genre's name takes a capital (Horror), and the rungs are written *the horror rung* and *the suspense rung*. The workshop reads the line above as about the genre; the theory does not say.
- **Not to be confused with:** the horror rung; the everyday word.
- **Telling test:** Cut the scene where the creature is seen in full light. The story is still Horror (*The Haunting of Hill House* reaches pure terror with nothing seen), but it never reaches the horror rung.
- **Open:** the capital is the workshop's house rule, not an owner question (H10: lower-case genre names kept coming back).
- **Owning skill:** genre (Words, last item).

### 8.3 Horror, the everyday word
- **Own words:** “the horror he's after is their acceptance, not the reveal” (Gap, Part 2 section 3, on *Never Let Me Go*)
- **In plain words:** appalled recognition, with no threat shown. Neither the rung nor the genre.
- **Telling test:** Is any threat seen or sensed? If neither, it is the everyday word.
- **Open:** settled (ordinary use). **Owning skill:** none; say which sense is meant.

## 9. The Bond theory: relationship, lever, rung, care

### 9.1 The relationship (the bond) and a lever
**Owner:** Bond theory, central claim; principle 1; principle 4 and The ten levers.
- **Own words:** “They care because of the relationship the story builds with them” (Bond, central claim); “Traits make that relationship easier or harder to build.” (Bond, central claim)
- **In plain words:** the relationship is what the story builds between audience and character: time together, access to their mind, understanding what they want, fearing what they could lose. A lever is one of ten traits a writer turns up or down (Desire, Vulnerability, Agency, Competence, Decency, Flaw, Interiority, Specificity, Humour, Connection). Levers make the relationship easier or harder to build; care does not come from them (H8, pattern b).
- **Not to be confused with:** each other; the Connection lever (9.6).
- **Telling test:** Is it something the character *is* (a lever), or something the audience has *done* with them (time, access, shared history: the relationship)? Change one thing: keep the trait and cut the audience's time with the character. If care falls, it was coming from the relationship.
- **Open:** settled by the theory. Holmes's *warmth* is not one of the ten; which lever it is, Q8 (the workshop reads Connection).
- **Owning skill:** character (Words: *bond*, *lever*).

### 9.2 A rung of the ladder of care, and a lever
**Owner:** Bond theory, The ladder of care.
- **Own words:** “The **Worry** rung is the "care" term in the suspense equation” (Bond, under the ladder of care)
- **In plain words:** a rung is a step the audience reaches, named by what it thinks: Recognise, Understand, Side with, Worry, Miss. The ladder's "Built by" column overlaps with the levers (Interiority helps build Understand), but a lever belongs to the character and a rung to the audience. Worry (“I'm afraid for them” (Bond, the ladder of care)) is the equation's care term, not the whole ladder.
- **Not to be confused with:** a lever; care in general (9.5).
- **Telling test:** Is it said as what the audience thinks (“I see why they do that” (Bond, the ladder of care, Understand row): a rung), or as a quality of the character (Interiority: a lever)?
- **Miss (H5):** built by “Time, shared history, relationships” (Bond, the ladder of care, Miss row). The workshop's reading (section 13, item 16; put to the owner under S13): a loss cannot stand in for that time; in the *Up* montage the loss comes last, after a whole marriage has built the rung. Test: would the death hurt more after more time with them? If so, time built Miss, and the death is where the audience feels it.
- **Open:** settled by the theory, except Miss and loss: the workshop's reading, section 13 item 16, S13.
- **Owning skill:** character (Words: *rung*).

### 9.3 Side with (a rung), alignment and allegiance
**Owner:** Bond theory, the ladder of care; principle 2; Part 2 section 2.
- **Own words:** “I want them to get it” (Bond, the ladder of care, Side with row); “We tend to side with whoever we spend time with and understand” (Bond, principle 2)
- **In plain words:** *Side with* is the third rung, built by “A goal we share, sympathy, fairness” (Bond, the ladder of care, Side with row). Murray Smith's pair sits under it: “*alignment* (whose company we keep and whose mind we can see into)” (Bond, Part 2 section 2) and “*allegiance* (whose side we're on)” (Bond, Part 2 section 2). The claim is that “alignment tends to produce allegiance even when morality says it shouldn't” (Bond, Part 2 section 2).
- **Not to be confused with:** alignment (the cause) and allegiance (the rung); Worry; the workshop's turned rung, *Side against* (9.4).
- **Telling test:** Remove the threat. Side with stays (the audience still wants them to get it); Worry goes. Or keep the audience in a killer's company and inside his head: alignment is unchanged, and if the audience still wants the car to sink for Norman Bates, allegiance has followed.
- **In use:** within one passage, *Side with* means one thing (H30 put it in two senses a paragraph apart).
- **Open:** settled by the theory.
- **Owning skill:** character (Words: *rung*; Diagnosing, Step 3).

### 9.4 A villain's ladder, turned round (S6)
**Owner:** Bond theory, principle 6 and Part 2 section 6 for the facts; the turning is the workshop's.
- **Own words:** “that investment is care pointing the other way” (Bond, Part 2 section 6); “A villain works when we badly want something to happen to them.” (Bond, Part 2 section 6)
- **In plain words:** the theory says a villain needs care and that wanting his downfall *is* care. It does not say the rungs turn. The workshop reads the upper rungs turned for an opponent the audience should oppose: *Side against* (wanting him stopped), Worry as fearing he will get what he wants, Miss as wanting his end and the payoff when it comes.
- **Not to be confused with:** the ladder as the theory states it; indifference.
- **Telling test:** Would the audience cheer his fall (care pointing the other way) or shrug (indifference, the real failure)? Principle 6 asks only for the first; how each rung reads for him is the workshop's.
- **Open:** S6. (H28 was an earlier version given as an instruction, which clashed with the Worry rung.)
- **Owning skill:** character (Words: *Side against*; Diagnosing, Step 3; `references/opponents.md` section 8).

### 9.5 Care
**Owner:** Bond theory, central claim, principles 1 and 6; the care term of the Anticipation theory's suspense equation.
- **Own words:** “they can't stop caring what happens to them” (Bond, central claim); “Indifference is the enemy, not dislike.” (Bond, principle 6)
- **In plain words:** care is not liking or approving. It includes wanting bad things for a character (Joffrey). The theory says “Care is a relationship, not a verdict.” (Bond, principle 1). The workshop says *bond* for the relationship and *care* for what it produces (character, Words; the workshop's reading).
- **Not to be confused with:** liking, virtue, pity; the equation's care term (the Worry rung only, 9.2); *investment*, which both theories use loosely for care (the workshop keeps the one word *care*).
- **Credit (H6):** “Care has to be built *before* the threat arrives” (Anticipation, Part 2 section 1) is the Anticipation theory's, not the Bond theory's.
- **Telling test:** If it happened to him, would the audience feel anything (cheer, grieve, fear)? Then there is care, liked or not. If they would shrug, there is none.
- **Open:** care against liking, settled by the theory. S2 (whether the limit *care can't be ordered* covers other characters condemning someone) is open.
- **Owning skill:** character (Words: *bond*, *care*).

### 9.6 Connection (a lever) and the relationship
**Owner:** Bond theory, The ten levers; principle 5.
- **Own words:** “loves and is loved” (Bond, the ten levers, Connection)
- **In plain words:** Connection is a trait: the character's ties to other characters. The relationship (9.1) is between the audience and the character.
- **Not to be confused with:** the bond; the Miss rung's *relationships* (section 13).
- **Telling test:** Who are the two parties? A character and another character: Connection. The audience and a character: the bond.
- **Open:** Q8 (Holmes's warmth, read as low Connection). **Owning skill:** character (Words: *lever*).

## 10. Tension and suspense

### 10.1 Tension (S1)
**Owner:** Anticipation theory, principle 7 The ratchet; Part 2 section 7. The theory does not define it.
- **Own words:** “Tension should rise in steps with partial releases” (Anticipation, principle 7)
- **In plain words:** the workshop's reading: the felt level of suspense or fear at a moment, the thing the ratchet raises and eases.
- **Not to be confused with:** suspense in the equation (10.2); pressure (2.3); stakes (cost, 5).
- **Telling test:** Would a false alarm lower it? The Lewton bus eases tension for a moment and leaves the threat where it was; the real threat arrives in that release.
- **Open:** S1.
- **Owning skill:** plot (`references/suspense-and-fear.md` section 7; not in the Words paragraph).

### 10.2 Suspense: three uses
**Owners:** Gap theory, Part 2 section 3 (Sternberg), and the Withholding row of the five gaps table; Anticipation theory, the suspense equation and the fear ladder.
- **Own words:** “a gap about the future” (Gap, Part 2 section 3); “Suspense = Care × Threat × Uncertainty × Time” (Anticipation, the suspense equation); “What the threat is, but not how it ends” (Anticipation, the fear ladder, Suspense row)
- **In plain words:** Sternberg's suspense is any open question about the future; the equation's suspense needs all four terms above zero; the suspense rung is one step of the fear ladder.
- **Not to be confused with:** each other; curiosity (a gap about the past); surprise (11.4); tension (10.1).
- **Telling test (H12, H15):** Tell the audience the ending. Suspense does not fall to zero: “Uncertainty can also move from *whether* to *how*.” (Anticipation, Part 2 section 1). A claim that knowing the outcome removes uncertainty turns the theory round.
- **In use:** write *the suspense rung* when the rung is meant (genre, Words).
- **Open:** settled by the theories.
- **Owning skill:** plot (Words mention Sternberg's sense; `references/suspense-and-fear.md` sections 1 and 2).

## 11. Other pairs the theories keep apart

### 11.1 Story and plot
**Owner:** Gap theory, Terms; Part 2 section 1.
- **Own words:** “Here, cause belongs to story, because the grief is part of what happened.” (Gap, Part 2 section 1)
- **In plain words:** the story is what happens, in the order it happened, causes included. The plot is the order, selection and emphasis in which the audience learns it. Forster put cause in the plot; this theory does not.
- **Telling test:** Reorder the telling and change no event: only the plot changed (*Memento*). Change what happened, or why: the story changed.
- **Open:** settled. **Owning skill:** plot (Words).

### 11.2 Reserve, gap, implication, surplus
**Owner:** Implied World theory, Key terms; principles 5 and 6.
- **Own words:** “what the creator knows but never shows” (Implied World, Key terms, Reserve); “If there are border guards, someone pays them.” (Implied World, Key terms, Implication); “Details that serve no purpose make a world.” (Implied World, principle 5)
- **Telling test:** Known to the creator and not shown: reserve. Filled in by nobody, on purpose: gap. Required by what is shown: implication. Shown, doing no job but signalling that the world is real: surplus. A detail that also does a job (shows which sibling stayed) is doing plot or character work, not surplus.
- **Open:** settled. **Owning skill:** story-world (Words); dialogue (Words).

### 11.3 Indifference, twice
**Owners:** Implied World theory, principle 4; Bond theory, principle 6.
- **Own words:** “A world feels real when it has business of its own” (Implied World, principle 4); “Indifference is the enemy, not dislike.” (Bond, principle 6). The Bond theory joins them: “the world shouldn't care about the hero, but the audience must” (Bond, How it fits with the others)
- **Telling test:** Whose indifference? The world's, to the hero: wanted. The audience's, to a character: the enemy.
- **Open:** settled. **Owning skill:** story-world (Words: *business*); character.

### 11.4 Surprise, three times
**Owners:** Gap theory, Part 2 section 3 (Sternberg); Anticipation theory, the fear ladder (Shock); Bond theory, principle 7.
- **Own words:** “you thought you understood and you were wrong” (Gap, Part 2 section 3); “Nothing: it arrives without warning” (Anticipation, the fear ladder, Shock row); “Care grows when a character does something unexpected that still makes sense for them.” (Bond, principle 7)
- **Telling test:** Did the audience think it understood and turn out wrong (the Gap theory's surprise), get no warning at all (shock, the side rung), or see a character do something unexpected that still fits them (the Bond theory's surprise)? If the act does not fit and the story has not earned it, the audience calls it “out of character” (Bond, Part 2 section 7), and the bond breaks.
- **Open:** settled, but two uses overlap (section 13). **Owning skill:** plot (`references/reveals-and-withholding.md`; `references/suspense-and-fear.md`); character (`references/change-and-growth.md`).

### 11.5 An open question and the story's main question
**Owners:** Gap theory, principle 5 and Part 2 section 6; Implied World theory, principle 8 Thematic fit.
- **Own words:** “The world's main departure should embody the story's main question.” (Implied World, principle 8); “A theme is the story's answer to the question its world poses.” (Gap, Part 2 section 6)
- **Telling test:** Can one fact settle it? Then it is an open question (a mystery or a wonder). Is it answered only by how the whole story settles? Then it is the main question, and the answer is the theme. The genre skill splits the genre's *question* from its *expected mystery* the same way.
- **Open:** settled. The genre's question and *expected mystery* are the workshop's (S8).
- **Owning skill:** plot (`references/premise-and-theme.md`); story-world; genre (Words: *question*, *expected mystery*).

### 11.6 The threat's rules and the thing (Q3)
**Owner:** Anticipation theory, principle 3; Part 2 section 3.
- **Own words:** “Show the rules, hide the thing.” (Anticipation, principle 3); “The rules work like mysteries: stated, tested and kept to.” (Anticipation, Part 2 section 3)
- **In plain words:** the audience needs enough of the threat's rules to predict danger; the threat's nature and origin work better as wonders. A world's *rule* in the story-world skill is a departure about how something works, which is a different thing.
- **Telling test:** Does it tell the audience how the threat behaves, so they can predict danger (a rule, to show), or what it is and where it came from (the thing, to hide)?
- **Open:** Q3 (whether this applies to human villains; now (b)).
- **Owning skill:** plot (`references/suspense-and-fear.md` section 3); character, for human opponents.

### 11.7 The guide
**Owner:** Implied World theory, What it predicts.
- **Own words:** “Worlds with many departures need a guide.” (Implied World, What it predicts)
- **In plain words:** an outsider who learns alongside the audience, or an outside aid (an opening crawl, a glossary, maps). One invented thing taken for granted in passing, like Heinlein's dilating door, needs no guide; that is surplus doing its job (H17).
- **Telling test:** A world with many departures, or one invented thing mentioned in passing? Only the first needs a guide.
- **Open:** settled. **Owning skill:** story-world (Words: *guide*).

## 13. Where the theories themselves leave a term unclear

All of these are put to the owner together under S13 in the questions file; an item that is also a question of its own gives that number.

1. **Promise** has three homes: the Gap theory's open questions (principle 5), the Anticipation theory's contract (which it calls guarantees and protections, never promises) and the Bond theory's “The bond is a promise.” (Bond, The limits of care). The theories do not say whether the Bond promise is a kind of Gap promise.
2. **Departure** covers both levels in the Gap theory: “A story is a departure from the world's *own* normal.” (Gap, principle 2)
3. **Gap** has three senses (Q2), and no theory says whether a gap never mentioned at all counts.
4. **Price:** the Implied World theory uses it for attention; the Gap theory uses it once for stakes and otherwise says *cost*; the Bond theory's Agency lever, “never pays a price” (Bond, the ten levers, Agency), uses it for stakes too, which Q4 does not mention; and the Implied World theory's own summary, “make every difference cost something” (Implied World, In one line), can be read as attention or as consequence.
5. **Wonder** is both a feeling and an open question (Q5). Also, principle 5 says “Breaking either promise feels like betrayal.” (Gap, principle 5), while Part 2 section 4 names the wonder side *deflation*.
6. **Mystery** is used in other senses: “the Force went from mystery to blood test” (Implied World, What it predicts) is the Gap theory's wonder; “The rules work like mysteries: stated, tested and kept to.” (Anticipation, Part 2 section 3) is about rules kept, not questions answered; Forster's “a plot with a mystery in it” (Gap, Part 2 section 1) is a withheld fact; the Interiority lever's “removes mystery” (Bond, the ten levers, Interiority) is the everyday word.
7. **Dread** has three uses (Q6), and the Anticipation theory also uses it loosely: “After real dread it works as a release valve” (Anticipation, Part 2 section 7)
8. **Horror:** the Anticipation theory writes both the rung (principle 2) and the genre (Part 2 sections 3 and 4) in lower case; “horror is a world whose rules turn against the people living in it” (Anticipation, Part 2 section 4) does not say which it means; “the horror he's after is their acceptance, not the reveal” (Gap, Part 2 section 3) is a third, everyday sense.
9. **Surprise:** both theories call Hitchcock's bomb that simply goes off a surprise, “fifteen seconds of surprise” (Gap, Part 2 section 3) and “you get seconds of surprise” (Anticipation, Part 2 section 1), yet the Anticipation theory's side rung for a threat that “arrives without warning” (Anticipation, the fear ladder, Shock row) is Shock. The Bond theory uses *surprise* for a third thing (principle 7).
10. **Care:** “Care is a relationship, not a verdict.” (Bond, principle 1), but the central claim has care coming *from* the relationship. The workshop's split (*bond* for the relationship, *care* for what it produces) is its own reading; it has no question of its own and is put to the owner under S13.
11. **Relationships** in the Miss rung's "Built by" column could mean the audience's shared history with the character or the character's ties to others (the Connection lever).
12. **Tension** is used but not defined (S1).
13. **Warmth** (Holmes) is not one of the ten levers (Q8).
14. **A villain's ladder:** the theory gives the ladder for any character and says villains need care, but not how the rungs read for someone the audience opposes (S6).
15. **Suspense** has three uses (10.2). The equation needs a threat; Sternberg's suspense needs only an outcome at stake. The theories do not say how the equation applies on the dread rung, where the threat is not yet known.
16. **Miss and loss:** the ladder says time, shared history and relationships build Miss, but the *Up* montage “climbs every rung of the ladder in order” (Bond, Part 2 section 1), ending with the loss, which could be read as the loss completing the climb. The workshop reads the loss as where Miss is felt, not what builds it (H5); put to the owner under S13.
17. **Expect** means predicting in the Anticipation theory and the world's normal in the Gap theory's “what breaks from the expected” (Gap, Part 2 section 2). *Contract* names Anticipation principle 5 but is never defined apart from the guarantees. *Invested* stands in for care in both the Anticipation and Bond theories.
