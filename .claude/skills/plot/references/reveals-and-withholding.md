# Reveals and withholding

*Sources: the Gap theory (the withholding gap; principles 4 Scheduling, 5 Promise and 6 Perspective; section 4 on mystery and wonder; writer's question 4), with the Implied World theory's reserve and expansion paradox and the Anticipation theory's time term; Truby, The Anatomy of Story (the reveal that forces a decision, the revelations sequence, the opponent's hidden plan, the reversal, holding back the ghost), McKee, Dialogue (exposition: timing, ranking facts, narrative drive, exposition as ammunition, secrets and backstory), and Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing (foreshadowing, planting what the climax uses, the obligatory scene, jumps); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 Suspense, curiosity, surprise. 2 Mystery or wonder. 3 What a reveal must do. 4 The reveals in sequence. 5 Exposition as ammunition. 6 Promises: foreshadowing, set-ups and the awaited scene. 7 Fair and unfair withholding. 8 Rivals. 9 Traps.

Plot is a schedule of knowledge: it decides when the audience learns each fact about the story and the world, and with what emphasis. This module is about drawing up that schedule.

## 1. Suspense, curiosity, surprise

**Case.** *Columbo* shows the murder, and the murderer, in the first act. Nobody wonders who did it. The whole episode runs on suspense about how the detective will catch someone the audience watched commit the crime, and on the knowledge gap: we see him circling what the killer thinks is hidden. A whodunit with the same kind of crime hides the very facts *Columbo* gives away, runs on curiosity, and ends in surprise. Same kind of story, opposite schedule.

**Point.** For each key fact, pick the interest it should serve, on purpose.
- **Suspense**, a gap about the future: show what is at stake early and hold the outcome. It lasts.
- **Curiosity**, a gap about the past: withhold something that happened, and let the audience sense that it is being withheld. Truby's advice on the hero's old wound, his *ghost*, is a case *(built; the timing fitted)*: keep it out of the opening, plant signs that something is hidden, and explain it early but not at once (Truby puts it in the first third); held to the end, it drags the plot backwards.
- **Surprise**, a gap the audience did not know was there: plant, hide, reveal. It lasts seconds, unless it makes the audience reread everything before it.

A surprise can start new suspense, and a fact delivered that way hardly registers as exposition, because it arrives as a question about what comes next (McKee calls the pull *narrative drive* *(built)*). In *Speed*, the rule that the bomb goes off if the bus drops below fifty is taken in without effort, since the audience is at once asking how the bus can keep moving.

**World facts are scheduled too.** Sometimes the biggest reveal is about the world (the statue on the beach in *Planet of the Apes*). Sometimes the writer refuses to make it a twist: *Never Let Me Go* lets the truth about its students out early and quietly, because the horror it is after is their acceptance. For each world fact ask: if it came early and quietly, what would the audience watch for instead? If the answer is stronger than the twist, give it early.

## 2. Mystery or wonder

Every open question carries a promise, and emphasis sets which one. A question the telling lingers on, that characters chase and that keeps coming back, is a **mystery**: the narrative promises to answer it. One mentioned in passing and never pressed is a **wonder**: it promises to leave it open. Break the first promise and the audience feels betrayed; break the second and the thing deflates.

**Case.** *Citizen Kane* presses one question, what "Rosebud" means, through a reporter whose whole job is to answer it. The film does answer it, for the audience, with the sled. What the answer says about the man stays open. Truby argues that a dying word should deepen a question rather than settle it *(asserted)*; that view is recorded as one rival in section 8. The workshop's reading, a hypothesis and not the theory's: one question can be pressed at one level (what is Rosebud?) and left unpressed at another (what did the life come to?), and each level keeps its own promise.

**Point.** Measure emphasis on the page, not in your intentions. For each open question, first ask: does a character make answering it a goal? If so, it is a mystery, whatever you meant; the theory's own test case is Tom Bombadil, who stays a wonder because nobody's goal is to solve him. If not, ask two more: does it come back in several scenes, and does the telling linger on it (a held shot, a chapter ending)? The more it returns and is dwelt on, the more the audience expects an answer, so a question you mean as a wonder should stay light. (This weighting is the workshop's reading of the theory, a starting guess, not a rule.)

Mark each fact a later turn depends on, and check that it appears at least twice, in different scenes, before that turn (the idea of ranking facts by weight is McKee's *(built)*). The theory adds the caution: emphasis is a promise, so give it only to what pays off.

The reserve is the world the writer knows but never shows (Implied World theory). Turning reserve and wonders into official answers makes a world feel smaller as it gets bigger (the theory's expansion paradox). Most of the reserve should stay down.

## 3. What a reveal must do

**Case.** In *Star Wars*, Luke gets home to find the farm burned and his aunt and uncle dead. He decides to go with Obi-Wan after all. His want bends: he still wants off the planet, but now to join the fight, not just to see the galaxy. And the reason changes: nothing holds him here any more, and the Empire has made it personal. All four slots below are filled, so the reveal turns the story.

**Point.** For a reveal that reaches the hero, use Truby's four slots *(built)*: what is learned; what the hero decides; how the want shifts; how the reason for it shifts. Empty slots mean a weak turn. The want should bend, not be replaced; a wholly new want starts a new story.

A reveal to the audience alone is judged differently, since by design no character decides anything. The Gap theory's own example of plot is one: the bomb shown to the audience and not to the people at the table. Judge it by what it does to the gaps: the suspense it buys, the knowledge position it sets up, the curiosity it closes. Move it later and ask how much suspense is lost (`suspense-and-fear.md`, sections 1 and 6). Either way, a reveal that changes neither a character's course nor what the audience expects is information, not a turn: cut it or give it more weight.

**Where reveals come from.** Truby again *(built)*: mostly from the opposition's hidden moves. Write the opponent's plan as fully as the hero's, mark which attacks are hidden, and note when each is sprung. His *iceberg opponent* is an opposition mostly under the surface: draw it as a hierarchy, mark what the hero knows at the start, and release the hidden layers at a quickening pace. His *fake-ally opponent* is an enemy posing as a friend, whose unmasking is a ready-made reveal. Whether to hide these moves from the audience too, or only from the hero, is a knowledge-position choice; see `suspense-and-fear.md`, section 11.

**What forces a secret out.** For each major secret, write what the holder loses by telling and what they lose by keeping it. The secret comes out when the cost of silence overtakes the cost of speaking; if keeping it costs nothing, the reveal will feel convenient (the idea is McKee's, on secrets *(built)*).

## 4. The reveals in sequence

**Case.** *Chinatown*. The detective thinks he is on an adultery case; the woman who hired him was an impostor; the dead man was killed over water being dumped in a drought; the land is being bought up under false names; the dead man's father-in-law is behind it; the girl everyone is hiding is his granddaughter and his daughter. Each reveal is found in an order the detective could plausibly find it, each is heavier than the last, and each lands closer to one family, which Truby notes as a detective pattern *(fitted)*: later reveals come nearer the hero's own life.

**Point.** Truby calls the reveals, pulled out and read on their own, the *revelations sequence*. List only the reveals, in order, and check three things *(built)*:
- **Logic.** Could this teller have found them in this order? This is also the fair-play check (section 7).
- **Weight.** Does the line rise? Mark any reveal lighter than the one before it and say what it is there for.
- **Pace.** Does the interval between reveals shrink towards the end?

Truby's count of seven to ten major reveals for a feature film is asserted, with no data shown; use it as a prompt to count, not a quota. His steady rise also rivals the Anticipation theory's ratchet (see `suspense-and-fear.md`, section 11).

**The reversal.** Truby's name for a reveal that makes the audience reread everything before it (*Fight Club*, *Atonement*, the midpoint of *Gone Girl*). He warns of its cost *(fitted)*; see the trap in section 9. Run two checks. Remove the reversal: does the story still work on character and theme? And could a second viewing find the signs that were there all along? A reversal that fails the first check is a gimmick; one that fails the second is a cheat.

**Reveals that mean something.** Ask of each reveal whether it also moves the theme, not only the plot (Truby on Borges, whose stories are built of reveals *(fitted)*). A reveal that only surprises is loose: swap it for any other surprise and nothing changes.

## 5. Exposition as ammunition

*Exposition* (McKee's term) is any invented fact the audience must hold to follow the plot. It is on the schedule of knowledge like any other fact. The jobs below regroup his rules in the workshop's terms:

- **Time it** *(fitted and built)*. McKee holds each fact back until the audience needs it. The working rule: if a turn is guessed, look for a fact released too early. (For threats, the Anticipation theory says the opposite: show them early. See the rivals.)
- **Slip it in while attention is elsewhere** *(built)*. A fact noticed as a fact interrupts; a fact taken in while the audience watches a conflict or chases a question does not. Two routes: narrative drive (section 1), and **exposition as ammunition**, McKee's term: a character who already knows a fact fires it at another, at a pivotal moment, to win something. In *The Empire Strikes Back* the villain reveals that he is the hero's father as a move in their duel. The weakest delivery is minor characters gossiping the fact to each other; the strongest is a character firing it at a climax.
- **Spend backstory at the big turns** *(built)*. In this module *backstory* (McKee's term) is the part of the reserve spent at a turn; he rates spending it there the strongest way to handle exposition *(asserted)*. In *Rebecca*, what really happened to Maxim's first wife is held until her boat is found, then spent in one confession that turns the book. Keep the biography as reserve; surface only what turns something.
- **Pass the honesty test** *(built)*. If two characters tell each other what both already know, their visible activity is a cover for the author's (McKee; the Implied World theory calls it dumping the reserve onto the surface). The line-level fix belongs to `dialogue`.

Egri adds *(built)* that revealing a character never stops: what the audience learns about a person is scheduled across the whole plot, mostly through what they do under pressure. Facts about people are on the schedule too.

## 6. Promises: foreshadowing, set-ups and the awaited scene

Every open question carries a promise (Gap principle 5). Three kinds matter most here.

- **Foreshadowing.** Egri's term for an early signal that a clash is coming *(built)*. It buys the audience's patience through stretches with no open conflict. A clash foreshadowed and never delivered is a broken promise.
- **Set-up and payoff.** Whatever the climax uses (an object, a skill, a fact) must be known earlier, and the way the character came by it must follow from who they are, not from chance. Egri's case *(built)*: if a woman will kill with a nail file, the audience must already know she had it and that it was sharp. Chekhov's gun makes a plot; Flaubert's barometer makes a world (Implied World theory). **Test:** list everything the climax uses; find where each is first planted, and whether it comes from the character's habits or situation.
- **The awaited scene.** Older textbooks call it the *obligatory scene*: the confrontation the story has promised all along. Egri rejects the idea *(built, by analogy)*: every scene is a causal link, so all are required, and what the audience really waits for is the proof of the premise. See the rivals.

## 7. Fair and unfair withholding

The teller sets the limits on what can be known, and so on what the plot can fairly hold back (Gap principle 6). A fact may fairly be withheld when:
- **the teller cannot know it yet**: the audience learns with a character who is learning;
- **the teller will not say it, and not saying is part of who they are**: Stevens in *The Remains of the Day*; the narrator of *The Usual Suspects*, who invents as he goes;
- **it is a wonder**: nothing was promised, and the Promise principle asks for it to stay open.

A character who keeps a secret and later fires it as a tactic (exposition as ammunition) does not add a ground of its own. It is fair through the first two: the teller had no access to it (a camera outside the secret-holder's mind, a close third on someone else), or not saying it is part of who the teller is. An all-knowing narrator, or a close third inside the secret-holder, who keeps it back until it is fired is the author withholding. (This routing is the workshop's application of the teller test.)

It is unfair when it is held back only because the writer wants a twist, or when a teller who knows it hides it for no reason in character. That is why the narrator reveal in *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd* is still argued over.

**Three fair-play checks.** Logic: the reveals come in an order this teller could have found them. Planting: on a second reading, the signs are there. The teller test: could this teller have known it at this point, and would they have said it? The theory's question 4 is the short form of the teller test: "Is anything held back only because I want a twist, rather than because the teller couldn't or wouldn't say?" Logic and planting come from the books and the workshop, not from the theory.

**Surprise without a jump.** Egri calls any change of feeling or decision that skips its steps a *jump*: it startles without convincing *(built)*. His case is Nora's decision to leave in *A Doll's House*, built up in visible steps rather than sprung in one speech. Later he concedes that a hint of a step is enough if it shows how the mind is working *(built)*. That concession is the bridge to fair surprise: plant the steps so that they read correctly only afterwards.

## 8. Rivals

**When to release a fact.** McKee: release each fact when it is needed and no sooner *(fitted, partly built)*. The Anticipation theory: suspense needs the threat shown well before it strikes. The test: reveal one threat at the moment it strikes in version A, and several scenes earlier in version B. Both sides accept that B gives suspense across the interval and A an unguessed turn; measure which is worth more by asking readers which version grips them more across the whole sequence, from B's early reveal to the strike. The theory predicts B; McKee predicts A, since B's guessed turn costs more than its lead-up gains. B rated higher counts against McKee's timing for threats; A rated higher counts against the theory's time term here. The workshop's line: McKee's timing for facts about the past (curiosity), the theory's for threats still to come (suspense).

**The awaited scene.** Egri: there is no obligatory scene; every scene is equally required, and the audience waits only for the premise to be proved *(built, by an analogy that mixes up causal need with dramatic weight)*. The Gap theory: emphasis promises an answer, which is close to what the textbooks meant. The test: take a story that sets two rivals circling each other for most of its length, and cut their confrontation, letting the outcome be shown some other way. The theory predicts the audience feels cheated; Egri predicts it plays, as long as the premise is proved.

**Jumps and surprise.** Egri: a change the audience did not see built is a fault. The Gap theory counts surprise as a proper tool, and the Bond theory values an unexpected act that turns out to have been in the character all along. The test: a decision prepared by planted steps that can only be read in hindsight. Egri's strict rule predicts it fails to convince; the theories predict it deepens the bond, provided the steps were there.

**The question never settled.** Truby praises *Heart of Darkness* for a central crime whose nature it never settles, and holds that a dying word should deepen a question rather than settle it *(asserted)*. The Gap theory: emphasis sets the promise, so a question characters chase is a mystery, and leaving it open is betrayal; the theory names no exception for a teller's disclaimer. The workshop adds a hypothesis the theory does not make: a teller who signals early that the answer cannot be known may turn the question into a wonder. The test holds emphasis constant: a character chases the question to the end, and the teller says from early on that it cannot be known. The theory predicts readers feel betrayed; the Truby and workshop line predicts a wonder. Until tested, follow the theory.

## 9. Traps

- **A reveal that changes nothing.** If it changes neither a character's course nor what the audience expects, it is information, not a turn.
- **An emphasised fact that never pays off.** The emphasis made a promise.
- **A twist the teller could not have hidden.** The second reading shows the author's hand.
- **Spending backstory early,** in idle talk, where it turns nothing.
- **A story with nothing left once the reversal is removed.** If character and theme do not hold without it, the story was only the reversal's delivery system.
- **Answering every question.** Mysteries close; wonders stay open, and the reserve stays mostly down.
- **Taking a book's count or order as a law.** Seven to ten reveals, a ghost in the first third: patterns fitted to past films, to question.
