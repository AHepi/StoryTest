# Suspense and fear

*Sources: the Anticipation theory (`sources/anticipation-theory.md`) throughout, with the Gap theory's knowledge gap and principle 5, and the Bond theory's Worry rung; Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing (foreshadowing, crisis, rising conflict, the crisis-climax-resolution cycle), Truby, The Anatomy of Story (the failed first plan, the audience revelation, the last reveal inside the refuge, image against sound, the problem present at the opening), and McKee, Dialogue (beats that each top the last, as a rival), used only where they add; in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 The four terms. 2 The fear ladder, and when to climb. 3 Show the rules, hide the thing. 4 Lost refuge. 5 Broken contract. 6 Knowledge positions. 7 The ratchet. 8 Residue. 9 Prose and film. 10 Questions for a scene. 11 Rivals. 12 Traps.

Keep the theory's central claim in view throughout: suspense and fear do not come from what happens but from what the audience expects *might* happen. The audience does the frightening. The writer makes them care, gives them enough to predict with, and never lets them feel sure.

## 1. The four terms

**Suspense = Care × Threat × Uncertainty × Time.** The terms multiply, so if any one is zero the suspense is zero, however large the others.

**Case.** *Titanic*. Everyone knows the ship sinks, and the frame shows old Rose alive, so the question is not whether the ship goes down or whether she survives, but how, and whether Jack does. The film spends well over an hour on care (Jack and Rose, the class wall between them) before the iceberg arrives, and then a long stretch of time as the ship fills. Swap in two strangers and the same sinking is spectacle, not suspense.

**Point.** For each scene with a threat, rate each term low, middle or high. Raise the lowest first: under multiplication the weakest term limits the whole.

- **Care.** Build it before the threat arrives. In Gap terms, show the normal before the breach, so the audience has seen what could be lost. This is the Bond theory's Worry rung, reached through desire and vulnerability; how to build it is the `character` skill's job. Test: cut the scenes before the threat. If the threat scene plays the same, care was not doing any work there.
- **Threat.** Make it specific: name what could be lost. Egri adds two useful checks *(built)*. Tension peaks when *both* sides stand to lose everything and neither can back down, so the clash cannot settle; write what each side loses if it loses. And no knockout before the end: after a heavy blow, the loser must still have something to fight with, or the fight is over and so is the suspense.
- **Uncertainty.** Aim for bad odds, not no odds. Suspense climbs as the hero's chances fall and collapses when hope is gone. Egri's *crisis* is a good working test *(built)*: a moment when a decisive change could still go either way, but with the odds against the hero; if the odds are even, worsen them. Name the force pulling each way; in *A Doll's House* the husband reading the letter is poised between his principles and his love for her. If one outcome is already certain, it is not yet a crisis. Truby adds *(built)*: the hero's first plan should fail against an opponent stronger than expected, because a plan that simply works removes uncertainty. When the outcome is announced (a title that names the killing), move uncertainty from *whether* to *how*.
- **Time.** Show the threat early; Hitchcock's bomb shown first buys minutes where a surprise buys seconds. Clocks, deadlines and a known date all lengthen it. Egri's *foreshadowing* *(built)* is a promise of a coming clash (Gap principle 5), and an audience will sit through long stretches with no open conflict if a clash has been clearly promised: *Thirty Seconds over Tokyo* spends most of its length training for a raid announced at the start. Cutting between two lines stretches time too, by leaving each one hanging (see `scenes.md`).

## 2. The fear ladder, and when to climb

Each rung up closes one gap. The fear gets sharper, but the audience's imagination, which was filling the gap with the worst thing it could build, has less to do.

| Rung | The audience knows | Typical tools |
|---|---|---|
| **Dread** | something is wrong, not what | the normal slightly off: a silence, a routine broken, a character's unease |
| **Suspense** | what the threat is, not how it ends | the threat shown, the outcome held |
| **Terror** | it is here, but unseen | sound, touch, darkness, offscreen space |
| **Horror** | they see it | the full reveal |
| *Shock* (a side rung) | nothing: it arrives with no warning | the jump scare |
| **Residue** | what stays afterwards | section 8 |

**Case.** Truby's reading of *M* *(built)*: after a killer buys a girl a balloon, the film cuts to her mother calling her name over empty places, an empty chair, then the balloon drifting away. The death is never shown. The sequence stays below the horror rung and the audience builds the worst itself.

**Point.** Climb slowly and linger below the top rung. A working rule, the workshop's reading of the theory: stay on a rung while it still produces new predictions, and climb when the audience has imagined all it can there. When you do reach the horror rung, what is shown must be worse or stranger than what was imagined. Monsters that break categories (dead yet alive, human yet animal, inside the body yet coming out) do this; so does the familiar made strange. Shock works as a release valve after real dread. Without dread it is a flinch, and audiences soon stop reacting.

**Test** (`hard-to-vary`, swap): show the thing one rung earlier. If nothing is lost, the lower rung was not doing work.

## 3. Show the rules, hide the thing

The audience needs enough of the threat's rules to predict danger, but not enough to understand it. The rules work as mysteries: stated, tested and kept. The monster's nature and origin work better as wonders, which is why franchises that explain their monsters get less frightening.

**Case.** Egri calls this the easiest foreshadowing there is *(built)*: put a character with an unbending rule on stage early, then let the audience learn that someone close to him has broken it. In *A Doll's House* the husband cannot bear the smallest dishonesty, and we know his wife forged a signature. His rule is the threat's rule, shown early and kept: it lets the audience predict a collision. How he will react when he finds out is the uncertainty term, and the play does show it; that is the outcome, not the hidden thing. Carrying the principle over to a human threat is the workshop's move, not the theory's. For a person, the hidden list holds what the theory hides for a monster: what they really are and where they came from. Explaining it shrinks a human threat as it shrinks a monster (the usual complaint about *Hannibal Rising*, which gave Lecter a childhood). His present reasons are not on the hidden list: a human opponent still gets a case (`.claude/skills/character/references/opponents.md`, section 6). How far the principle reaches for human villains is a question put to the owner.

**Point.** Write the threat's rules as a short list. For each, mark where it is first shown, where it is tested, and where it is kept. Then list what stays hidden (origin, nature, full appearance), and check that nothing on the hidden list gets explained by accident.

## 4. Lost refuge

Fear is strongest when the threat enters somewhere meant to be safe (the home, the bed, a child, the body, broad daylight) or makes an everyday necessity dangerous (sleep, sound, looking). You cannot opt out of a need, so no safe action is left. Stripping away help does the same work: the line is cut, the car will not start, nobody believes her. In Implied World terms, the world's rules turn against the people living in it.

Truby's reading of *Alien* *(fitted, one film)* gives a placement: the last reveal is that the creature is aboard the escape craft. Where the hero flees to is where the danger is greatest.

**Point.** List the refuges your characters rely on: places, people, needs, help. Take them away one at a time. A useful default, the workshop's own and not the theory's: take the most intimate last, so that the final refuge is the one the ending invades.

## 5. Broken contract

Genres carry unspoken guarantees: the star survives, the child is safe, the dog lives. While the audience trusts them, part of it relaxes. Break one early and convincingly, as *Psycho* does with its apparent lead, and every later threat is real. Break all of them and care collapses, and suspense with it.

**Point.** The theory says only: one, early and convincingly, not all. Which one is the workshop's reading: the one whose loss would shake this audience most, and among those, one the story's question is about. List the guarantees your audience leans on (the genre profiles in `.claude/skills/genre/references/genres/` discuss them genre by genre), choose one that way, and break it early. Then check that the audience still cares about someone afterwards. If not, too much was broken. A late break, which gives the ending weight instead of making later threats real: `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`, section 2.

## 6. Knowledge positions

Choose, scene by scene, whether the audience knows more than, the same as, or less than the characters.

- **Audience ahead** (we see the bomb, the killer in the closet): suspense of helplessness. We fear *for* the character.
- **Audience level** (a tight point of view, found footage): shared terror. We fear *with* the character.
- **Audience behind** (the character sees something we cannot): dread by proxy. A reaction to something offscreen frightens us before we know why.

**Case.** Egri notices a scene in *Tartuffe* where nobody fights at all, yet tension rises *(fitted, one example)*: a man happily signs his estate over to a pious fraud. The audience knows what the fraud is; the dupe does not. Egri calls it preparing for conflict; the theory explains why it works. It is the audience-ahead position: we watch a weapon change hands. Egri also points to lines that strike no blow but still raise tension, such as a naive reply that misreads the danger *(fitted)*: Nora offering to take all the blame, sure her husband will shield her, when we know him better than she does.

**Point.** For each major scene, write one line: ahead, level or behind, and about what. Look for long runs of the same position and check that each was chosen. A shift near a peak is a strong tool, not a requirement. The strongest moment is often the instant the audience realises the character is not alone, just before the character does. How a teller sets the position (point of view, cutting away, a narrator who knows the end) is in `telling-and-perspective.md`, section 5.

Truby's *audience revelation* is the point where the audience first learns something the hero does not, often who the enemy posing as a friend really is *(fitted)*. He places it once, late, and treats it as distancing; the theory treats audience-ahead as a main source of suspense. See the rivals.

## 7. The ratchet

*Tension* here means the felt level of suspense or fear at a given moment, the thing the ratchet raises and eases. Tension that only rises goes numb; tension that fully releases resets to zero. The ratchet rises, eases a little, then rises higher. False alarms are a tool: in *Cat People* a stalking peaks, a bus hisses into frame, the audience jumps and laughs and lowers its guard, and the real threat can arrive in that release. Audiences learn a rhythm fast (the music swells, then the scare), so break it before they learn it: a scare with no warning, or a long wait where nothing comes.

**Case.** Egri's crisis (section 1), *climax* (the moment it tips) and *resolution* (what follows) repeat in small cycles *(fitted, from A Doll's House, Act Two)*. The blackmailer's threat is a crisis; the husband refusing to keep him on is the climax; her terror is the resolution. Later cycles end higher (the dismissal is sent; then the blackmailer's letter drops into the letterbox), and each resolution leaves her a live option, so the next attempt can start. Each resolution is a partial release; each cycle is one click of the ratchet.

**Point.** Chart tension scene by scene on a rough scale of one to ten. Look for three faults: a straight climb (numbness), a drop to zero (a reset), and the same spacing between peaks three times running (a rhythm the audience can learn). Fixes: add a partial release before a peak, vary the spacing, and let the real threat arrive inside a release.

## 8. Residue

Fear follows the audience home when two things hold: the threat is something they cannot rule out in their own world, and the ending leaves something open, because the threat survives or the rule is never broken. The deepest residue comes from theme: a monster that gives a body to a fear the audience already carries (grief in *The Babadook*, racism in *Get Out*).

**Point.** Check both conditions. First, could the threat exist in the audience's own world? A fantastic threat that survives the ending leaves an end open but is not residue in the theory's sense unless the audience can find it in their own lives, often through what it stands for (the theme route above). Second, what stays open, and was it promised (Gap principle 5)? The theory names two ways to leave the gap open: the threat survives (the story can answer whether they escaped and still leave it at large), or its rule is never broken. The workshop adds a third, consistent with the Promise principle: a wonder, something the plot never promised to close. A question the plot pressed hard and then left dangling is betrayal, not residue. The theme's part is in `premise-and-theme.md`.

## 9. Prose and film

Each medium makes different things cheap.

| | Prose | Film |
|---|---|---|
| Who controls time | the reader; the writer steers with sentence length, white space and chapter breaks | the filmmaker, who can hold a shot until it is unbearable |
| Strongest rungs | dread and terror: the reader builds the monster | suspense and horror: the camera shows what characters cannot see |
| A threat in the background | naming it points straight at it | it can sit unnoticed in the frame |
| Jump scares | nearly impossible | easy, and easy to overuse |
| Signature tool | the narrator who knows the ending | offscreen sound, the held shot, the reaction shot |

Two book notes. Egri observes that as tension mounts, sentences tend to get shorter and simpler *(fitted, a tendency, not a law)*; it holds for spoken lines and serves prose pacing. Truby shows that picture set against sound can mean what neither says alone *(built)*, as in *M*; a prose writer can set narration against the scene it covers for a similar effect.

**Point.** When adapting, swap tools rather than copying them. A film's unnoticed background threat becomes, in prose, a passing detail the reader will only understand on rereading; a prose chapter break becomes a held shot or a cut to black.

## 10. Questions for a scene

The theory's six questions, as a working check:

1. Do we care before the threat arrives, and what exactly could be lost?
2. Which rung is this scene on, and is it climbing too fast?
3. What are the threat's rules, and what about it stays hidden?
4. Which refuge has been taken away?
5. Is the audience ahead of, level with, or behind the characters?
6. Has the audience learned the rhythm yet, and where does it break?

Then one `hard-to-vary` check: remove the scene where the threat is first shown. If the tense scene plays just as well, the showing was not buying time, and either the showing or the tense scene is loose.

## 11. Rivals

This section is the workshop's home for these rivals; other skills keep only a test of their own and point here.

**Start with the conflict, or show the normal first.** Egri: the first line should start the conflict, with no background or atmosphere before it *(asserted)*. Truby asks something close of the hero: a *problem*, a crisis already present at the start that shows the flaw *(fitted)*; since that trouble can itself be part of the normal, his view fits version B below as well as A. The theories: care must be built before the threat, which means showing the normal before the breach. A change that tells them apart: write two openings of one story. A opens on the clash; B opens on a short stretch of the normal that already carries small friction and shows what could be lost (Egri's own *Hedda Gabler* opening, a squabble over a hat, does both). Give both to readers and measure one thing for both sides: which version they rate more gripping across the whole opening, up to and including the moment the real threat lands, and whether any stop reading before it. The theories predict B wins over that span, because care built first multiplies the threat; Egri predicts A wins, because B loses readers before the clash. B rated higher, with no one lost early, counts against Egri's rule; A rated higher, or readers dropping out of B's first pages, counts against the claim that the normal must come first.

**Hide the attack, or show it.** Truby: hide the opponent's attacks so each lands as a surprise, and keep the audience level with the hero for most of the story, with one audience revelation that creates distance *(built and fitted)*. The theories: audience-ahead is a main source of suspense; choose the position scene by scene. The test: take one hidden attack. In version A, show the audience the attack being prepared a few scenes earlier; in B, keep it hidden. Both sides accept that A gives suspense and B a jolt; they disagree over which is worth more, so measure that: which version readers rate more gripping across the whole sequence, from the first lead-up scene to the attack. The Anticipation theory predicts A (minutes of suspense outweigh seconds of surprise); Truby predicts B (the jolt and the closer tie to the hero outweigh the lead-up). A rated higher counts against hiding the attack; B rated higher counts against showing it.

**A steady climb, or a ratchet.** Truby asks for reveals that each hit harder and come faster *(built)*; Egri asks for each scene to hit harder than the last, and McKee for each beat to top the last *(both asserted)*. The theory: rise in steps with partial releases, and break the pattern before it is learned. The test: run the same sequence with and without one partial release (a false alarm, a resolution that leaves hope) just before the peak. The theory predicts the peak lands harder after the release and the steady version goes numb; the books predict the release loses momentum. Egri's own reading of *Ghosts*, whose last act starts lower and climbs again, is closer to the theory.

**How low is the low point.** Truby: at the apparent defeat the audience must believe the hero is finished *(fitted)*. The theory: suspense collapses once hope is entirely gone. The test: lengthen the despair, against keeping it brief and following it at once with a new reveal that restores a chance. The theory predicts suspense drops during a long stretch of total despair. The two agree when the despair is brief.

**What boredom means.** Egri: boredom always means missing conflict *(asserted; a catch-all)*. The workshop's reading of the theories: any term at zero (care, uncertainty, time), or too many new things to take in at once. The test: in a stretch readers call boring, add conflict in one version and build care, or clear up confusion, in another. Whichever cures it names the missing term.

## 12. Traps

- **Raising the threat when care is zero.** A bigger bomb under strangers is still no suspense.
- **Climbing to the horror rung too early,** or showing the thing just to prove it exists.
- **Explaining the monster.** Its nature was a wonder; answering it spends the fear.
- **One knowledge position by default, not by choice.** Great sequences move between positions, and the strongest moment is often a shift; one position throughout gives up those peaks. A single position chosen on purpose (found footage; *Alien* keeping the audience level with its crew) is legitimate.
- **A rhythm the audience learns.** Then the scare arrives on schedule and stops working.
- **Jump scares without dread.**
- **Breaking every guarantee.** Everyone dies, nobody matters.
- **Calling a dangling question residue.** Residue needs a threat the audience cannot rule out in their own world and an open end: the threat left alive, its rule never broken, or a wonder left open. Never a broken promise.
