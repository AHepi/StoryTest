# Symbols

*Sources: the Implied World theory (principle 8 Thematic fit; principles 2 Attention budget and 5 Surplus; the baseline), the Gap theory (principles 1 Nested inference, 4 Scheduling, 5 Promise and 7 Settlement; mystery, wonder and deflation) and the Anticipation theory's residue (a monster that gives a body to a fear); Truby, The Anatomy of Story (the symbol web, refer and repeat, the story symbol, symbols for characters, for change, for theme and for a world, ordering symbols by structure, genre symbol sets and turning them inside out, the cost of too many; and, from his dialogue chapter, key words and taglines); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 How a symbol gathers meaning. 2 Symbols come in webs. 3 Where to attach one. 4 Scheduling symbols. 5 Ready-made sets. 6 Costs. 7 Rivals. 8 Traps.

A **symbol** here is an image, object, act or word that comes back and stands for more than itself. The owner theories touch symbols only in passing: a monster that gives a body to a fear the audience already carries (the Anticipation theory's residue), and a main departure that embodies the story's question (the Implied World theory's thematic fit). This module ties Truby's method to those two points and to the Gap theory's settlement, and is kept short on purpose.

## 1. How a symbol gathers meaning

**Case.** *The Great Gatsby*: the green light across the bay, on Daisy's dock. Nick first sees Gatsby reaching towards it across the water at night. Later, with Daisy beside him at last, Gatsby feels its enchantment drain away. On the last page it becomes the thing everyone reaches for and never holds. Each return is changed, and the last one settles what the book meant.

**Point.** Truby's rule, *refer and repeat* *(half built: an image met again and again alongside a feeling becomes tied to it; half asserted: that it works below awareness)*. Start from the feeling you want, choose an image that stirs it, and bring the image back, altered each time, so the feeling grows. Tests: list every appearance. An image that appears once is not yet a symbol. If two appearances are identical, change one. If the changes do not strengthen the feeling, reorder them. In owner terms the audience infers the meaning (Gap principle 1), and, as the Implied World theory says of gaps, that work turns into attachment; saying the meaning aloud does the audience's work for them.

## 2. Symbols come in webs

Truby *(built, by analogy with a cast whose members are defined against each other)*: a lone symbol points nowhere, and symbols fix each other's meaning by contrast. *The Lord of the Rings* sets the elven forest, a community that tends living things, against the mountain of fire, power that consumes. Checks:
- Name each symbol's partner. No partner: find one or cut it.
- The hero's symbols and the opponent's should oppose.
- One character can carry two opposed symbols, which gives depth without adding more.
- Hold the web against the question the world poses and the designing principle (`premise-and-theme.md`, sections 4 and 5). A symbol off that line is decoration or clutter.

## 3. Where to attach one

| Attach to | Move | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The whole story | one image for the premise, the story's shape or its main twist, often in the title; its meaning should change over the story | the letter in *The Scarlet Letter*, a badge of shame that comes to stand for a truer morality |
| A character | one symbol for one core quality, returned with small changes; use sparingly, since each extra one weakens the rest | an ironic name: Steerforth in *David Copperfield* |
| The hero's change | plant it, unnamed, in an opening scene, ideally the one that shows the weakness; bring it back altered at the moment of change | *The Godfather*: the Don's favour-for-a-favour bargain at the wedding, a pact with the devil that nobody names; it returns at the christening, where Michael renounces Satan while his men kill for him |
| The theme | an object that stands for a run of acts with moral effects, or for two opposed moralities at once | the scarlet letter again, first the town's verdict on Hester, then a charge against the town |
| A world in change | one object or place whose fate tracks a society's | the cinema in *Cinema Paradiso*, which ends as a car park |
| An unseen force | give fate, luck or a curse a body, and show it acting on many people, not only the leads; the Anticipation theory's residue works the same way, a monster giving a body to a fear | the moon in *Moonstruck* |
| Words | a word or line repeated in changing situations until it carries the theme (Truby's *tagline*) | the heroes' puzzled question about their pursuers in *Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid*, idle at first, life or death by the end |

Truby's shorthand types, linking a character to a god, an animal or a machine, work fastest in forms that run on metaphor *(asserted)*. Say which side of the type you mean, and consider letting the machine-like figure prove the most humane one present *(fitted)*.

## 4. Scheduling symbols

The plot decides when a symbol appears, how often, and with what emphasis, as it does for every fact (Gap principle 4).
- **Order them by force, not by chronology** (Truby *(fitted from one book, with a built mechanism)*). *Gatsby* holds its image of the continent as the first sailors saw it, the earliest thing in history, for the final page, after Nick's understanding is complete; set against what was made of the place, it lands hardest there.
- **Plant them inside events** *(built)*. A symbol introduced inside an event that matters gathers meaning as later events recast it; one merely placed in view stays fixed.
- **Emphasis is a promise.** A symbol the telling lingers on, that characters chase and that keeps coming back, reads as a mystery (Rosebud) and must be answered at the level at which it was pressed (`reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 2). Recurrence is one of the theory's marks of a mystery, so a returning symbol stays a wonder only if the telling never lingers on it as a question and no one makes solving it a goal (the Bombadil test).
- **The last return is part of the settlement.** Its meaning should have moved between first appearance and last.

## 5. Ready-made sets

Genres come with symbol sets the audience reads at once: the Western's hat, badge, gun and fence; Horror's light against dark (Truby *(fitted)*). They cost little attention because they ride on the baseline (Implied World theory), and for the same reason they add nothing the audience did not bring: used straight, the set tells the audience about the genre, not about this story. Truby's move *(built on expectation and its breach)*: keep the pieces and turn them, each turn drawn from this story's theme rather than from a wish to be different. *McCabe and Mrs. Miller* stages its showdown in a snowstorm while the town is busy elsewhere, so the Western's public act of order goes unwitnessed. *Carrie* turns Horror's holy symbols against religious bigotry. Genre by genre: `.claude/skills/genre/references/genres/`.

## 6. Costs

- **Too many symbols turn feeling into puzzle-solving** (Truby *(built)*). Each symbol is one more inference the reader must make (Gap principle 1); past a point, reading becomes a hunt for codes and the feeling drains away. Symbols spend the same attention the plot needs (Implied World principle 2); count what a reader must track in a chapter.
- **A heavy-handed symbol can be rescued by its speaker** *(fitted from one film)*. Test: give the plain statement to a clever character. If it now preaches, the speaker was what saved it; *Forrest Gump*'s saying about a box of chocolates passes this test.
- **A symbolic act halts the flow** (Truby *(built)*). Tie an act to a meaning and the audience stops to read it. Choose few, and keep them out of stretches that need momentum.
- **Leave most objects free.** Not everything needs a meaning. Surplus detail, like the Flaubert barometer in the Implied World theory, says "this is real" precisely because it serves no purpose.

## 7. Rivals

**Every element given a job, or some left free.** Truby tends to file every element under the theme; in his reading of *The Lord of the Rings*, even Tom Bombadil stands for self-mastery. The owner theories hold Bombadil up as a wonder, powerful because the book never explains him or makes him anyone's goal, and they value detail that serves no purpose. The test: cut his chapters, as the 2001 film did. Truby's filing predicts a lost thematic strand (self-mastery set against the Ring's craving); the owner theories predict a world that feels smaller, with no loss of theme. Ask readers of both versions what went missing. (Adding a passage that explains him would not tell the two apart: Truby too holds that a symbol spelled out goes flat, and the Gap theory calls explaining a wonder deflation.) The two can live together if the symbol web is a small, deliberate set and everything else stays free.

## 8. Traps

- **A lone symbol with no partner.** It points nowhere.
- **A symbol that never changes.** Repetition without variation goes numb.
- **Explaining the symbol.** The audience's inference was the point.
- **A ready-made set used straight by accident.** Decide: play it straight or turn it.
- **A symbol pressed like a mystery and never paid.** Emphasis made a promise.
- **Making everything mean something.** A world with no surplus feels like a diagram.
