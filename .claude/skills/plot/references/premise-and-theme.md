# Premise and theme

*Sources: the Gap theory (principle 7 Settlement and Part 2 section 6, where a theme is the story's answer to the question its world poses; principle 5 Promise; Part 2 section 2, a realist world's "one rule"; question 5), the Implied World theory (principle 8 Thematic fit; principle 5 Surplus), the Bond theory's point that care cannot be ordered, and the Anticipation theory (principle 8 Residue and its two conditions; the multiplication); Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing (the premise as a claim the action proves: its three parts, active and vague premises, narrowing, the chain of whys, one premise per work, never stated), Truby, The Anatomy of Story (the premise line and its problems, the designing principle, moral argument, the final moral choice, variations on a theme, beliefs in conflict, the opponent's best case, theme widening as the conflict tightens, the thematic revelation, some of his variants of moral argument, false endings), and McKee, Dialogue (story values and the core value); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 Four words kept apart. 2 The premise line. 3 From idea to claim. 4 The question the world poses. 5 The designing principle. 6 Theme argued through action. 7 When the meaning shows. 8 The settlement. 9 Rivals. 10 Traps.

The owner theories put meaning at the end: what a narrative means is how its gaps are settled, and a theme is "the story's answer to the question its world poses". The books put a sentence at the start. Both are useful if each is kept in its place. A sentence at the start steers the writing; the ending is what the audience actually takes home.

## 1. Four words kept apart

**Case.** *Children of Men*. The story in a line: in a world where no child has been born for eighteen years, a burned-out official agrees to get a pregnant refugee to safety. The question the world poses (the Implied World theory's own reading): can hope exist without a future? The theme is the film's answer, settled in a small boat in fog, with a child alive and the man who got her there dying. A claim in Egri's sense might be that grief walls a man off until another's need breaks through (section 3 climbs to it); the film never argues it, it shows it.

**Point.** Four things, four words:

| Word | What it is | From | When to write it |
|---|---|---|---|
| premise line | the whole story in one sentence: what sets it off, who it is about, a hint of how it ends | Truby, who calls it the premise | first, and revise it often |
| claim | a proposition the action is built to prove: a trait or force, the struggle it drives, where it ends ("grief walls a man off until another's need breaks through") | Egri, who also calls it the premise | early, as a private steering tool |
| question | what the world's main departure, or in realist fiction its main rule, asks | Implied World principle 8; Gap theory | while building the world |
| theme | the story's answer to that question, as the ending settles it | Gap theory | known for sure only once the ending works |

Truby's *theme line*, the story's moral view in one sentence, is a draft of the answer. Keep it private.

## 2. The premise line

- **Three parts** (Truby *(asserted, clear)*): the event that sets the story going (in Gap terms, the breach), who it is about, and a hint of the outcome. A missing outcome usually means the ending has not been decided. The Gap theory adds a fourth thing the line should imply: the normal being broken.
- **A hook is a pitch, not a story** (Truby *(built)*). Test it: list the scenes the twist itself produces. Set that short list against the scene count the story needs (`scenes.md`, section 6); every scene not on it has to come from character, opponent and theme, and the shortfall is the work still to do. The Implied World theory's ripple says the same of a departure: it convinces through its knock-on effects.
- **Name the idea's own problems early** *(asserted; the examples fitted with hindsight, but a strong move)*. Under the premise line write the one thing this story must pull off, then the specific problems it brings: a hero who starts out good, a hero who only reacts, a narrator who is only a helper, jumps in time, a small story that must stand for a large one. At one line they are cheap to solve.
- **One spine** *(built)*. Boil the premise line down to the hero's single most important action. If it takes "and" to join two unrelated actions (a romance and a quarrel over a vineyard), the premise is split: make one cause or serve the other now, while it costs one sentence.

## 3. From idea to claim

**Case.** Egri follows a writer who sees boys rob a passer-by and wants to write about it. A single robbery will not fill a play, so the writer keeps asking why. Neglect at home? Why? Parents without money or authority. Why? No work to be had. The chain stops at a cause that explains thousands of boys like them, and that is the claim (the play he has in mind is *Dead End*). Notice where the chain ended: at a condition of the world, which is where the Gap theory says wants and costs come from.

**Point.** Egri's claim has three parts *(built from the grammar of a proposition)*: a character or force, a verb of struggle, and an end. Ways to reach one:
- **Ask why, again and again,** from an incident, until the answer explains many cases, not only this one.
- **Climb from a feeling** (Egri's method). A feeling alone sets no goal: "grief". A verb of struggle gives it a direction: "grief walls a man off from the world". An end gives the story somewhere to stop: "until another's need breaks through, at the price of his life". Each rung is a different story; the last is roughly Theo's in *Children of Men*.
- **Narrow with "which?" and "how far?"** *(built)*. "Grief isolates" leaves both open. Which grief (a child's death) and how far (he no longer cares whether the species ends) tell you who the man must be and how much it will take to reach him.
- **Root it in the trait, not the event** *(fitted)*. In Maupassant's *The Necklace* the loss is a consequence; the claim lives in what made her borrow the necklace and then hide its loss.
- **Test it against counter-cases** *(built)*. Find a case where the cause is present and the effect absent, and dig until the claim survives. Egri adds *(asserted)* that the claim only has to hold inside this story: the story's world must make it hold (close the exits: `structure-and-conflict.md`, section 5).
- **One claim per work** *(asserted, by analogy)*. Two claims pulling different ways blur the story; make one serve the other, or cut it. Egri allows sub-claims but never says where a sub-claim ends and a second claim begins.
- **Never state it.** Egri and Truby agree: by the end the audience should be able to put it into words, and no character should have done it for them.

Symptoms of a missing or muddled claim *(fitted)*: a stretch that stalls, or an event with no bearing on the central concern; a claim that switches near the end, so the central character suddenly becomes someone else; incident piled on incident to fill space; characters doing things only because the plot needs them to. Stalls and forced acts have other causes too, so separate them: write the claim in one line. If the stalled stretch bears on it and still stalls, look elsewhere (`structure-and-conflict.md`, section 9). If nothing in the stretch bears on it, either the claim is missing or the stretch is.

## 4. The question the world poses

**Case.** *The Handmaid's Tale* builds a state that controls fertility because the book asks who owns a body. The world's main departure and the story's question are one thing (Implied World principle 8). Offred's story is that question worked out under pressure, and the ending, her voice recovered and picked over by scholars long afterwards, settles some gaps and leaves her fate open.

**Point.** Where Egri and Truby start from a trait or from the writer's moral view, the owner theories start from the world:
1. Write the world's main departure, or in realist fiction its main rule (the entail in *Pride and Prejudice*, which the Gap theory calls "one rule of its world").
2. Write the question it asks, as a question.
3. Check that the breach puts that question under strain. A world shows itself best through what it does to people under strain (Gap principle 3).
4. Write the answer the ending gives. That answer is the theme.

Egri's chain of whys often arrives at the same place from the other end: keep asking why a character acts as they do, and the answer turns out to be a rule of their world. McKee's *core value* (a pair such as hope and despair, freedom and bondage) names the axis the question runs on *(built)*. If the claim, the question and the core value do not meet, test the question first: does the breach put it under strain (step 3)? If it does, rewrite the claim and the core value to fit it. If it does not, the world's question is wrong: go back to the departure or rule (`story-world`).

## 5. The designing principle

Truby's *designing principle* is the strategy for telling this particular story, in one line: the deeper process the story traces, plus the fresh form it takes *(asserted; loose, since almost any successful story can be given one afterwards)*. Take *Children of Men*, whose premise line is in section 1. One designing principle: carry the future on foot through a country that has stopped believing in one, so that each stage of the escort meets a different way of giving up.

Where such a form tends to come from *(fitted)*, grouped four ways:
- **In space:** a road whose stages carry the change (the escort above); one bounded place that holds the whole society (a ship, an island, a single house).
- **In time:** a fixed span or a countdown that shapes every scene.
- **In an image:** one object or place, or two in tension, that the whole story turns around (`symbols.md`, section 3).
- **In the telling:** a teller whose slant is the story (Stevens in *The Remains of the Day*); an order of events that is itself the point (*Memento* told backwards, so the audience shares the hero's lost memory).

Tests. Could the line describe dozens of other stories in the genre? Then it is a genre label, not a principle. Draft three for one premise line and keep the one whose problems you most want to solve (Truby's exercise). When the principle lives in the telling, check the teller test for anything held back (`telling-and-perspective.md`, section 4).

## 6. Theme argued through action

**Case.** *It's a Wonderful Life*. George and Potter want the same thing, the town, and prize it for opposite reasons: the people in it against the money in it. An argument between them early on tells the audience what the fight is about; everything after argues it through deeds; the alternative town at the end makes the stakes visible.

**Point.** Truby's *moral argument* is theme argued through the means characters use to reach their goals, not through speeches *(built: audiences resist being told and believe what they see done, as the Bond theory says of care)*. Jobs:
- **Split the answer into positions,** and give each to a character. Every major character meets the same central problem and handles it their own way (Truby's *variations on a theme*). Write the problem as a question and each character's answer in one line; look for duplicates and blanks.
- **Give the sides beliefs, not only goals:** each side's view of the good life, colliding over one place or object that each side prizes differently (the town; the cherry orchard). (This skill keeps *value* for McKee's pair of opposed conditions, section 4.)
- **Use more than two sides** *(asserted)*. Good against evil comes out simple; several positions on one question give the feel of real moral difficulty.
- **Give the opponent the strongest case** (Truby *(built)*). If nothing in it tempts a reader, strengthen it; if you cannot name its flaw, the theme is muddled. The opponent as a person: `.claude/skills/character/references/opponents.md`.
- **End on a real choice when the moral line is meant to carry suspense** *(built)*. The final decision is then between two goods (rarely, two evils), close in weight and impossible to undo. Good against bad is a fake choice of that kind: nobody would take the other side, so it reveals nothing and holds no moral suspense. Write it as "A over B" and ask whether B is something the hero, and the audience, truly value. Rick puts the cause before Ilsa. In a story built on good against bad (section 7), the final choice may be good over bad; then all the suspense has to come from the threat, since the terms multiply (`suspense-and-fear.md`, section 1).
- **Test with verbs** *(built)*. Summarise the moral argument using only action verbs. If you need to quote a speech, the argument lives in talk.

Talk carries theme where people with something riding on the outcome argue what is worth having, as George and Potter do in the Case above; the same argument with nothing at stake is a lecture. How those lines are worded belongs to `dialogue`.

## 7. When the meaning shows

When the audience can see what is at stake morally is a scheduling choice too (Gap principle 4). Truby's pattern *(built in outline)*: the theme is mostly out of sight early, while hero and opponent are only mildly opposed; it grows as the conflict tightens and the beliefs meet; it opens out at the battle (whose view of the good life holds), the self-revelation and the final choice.
- **Hold the moral turn late** *(built)*. In *The Verdict* the lawyer decides to fight for justice early, so from then on he is in no moral danger: the audience wonders whether he will win, not whether he will do right. Once a moral question is answered, its uncertainty is zero. Move the turn later, or set a second, harder test after it (the *Iliad* runs Achilles' fall and rise twice).
- **Enough plot to carry the moral line** *(asserted, with a hinted mechanism)*. Moral beats with no surprise between them read as a sermon. Count the reveals and reversals between moral beats.
- **The audience's own insight** (Truby's *thematic revelation*): an understanding of how anyone might live, larger than the hero's lesson, carried by one concrete act or image near the end rather than said *(fitted from one film; the move from specific to general is built)*. When the hero cannot see, give the recognition to a witness whose view the audience shares (Kay at the closing door in *The Godfather*), or to the audience alone.

Deciding who learns, and when, is a knowledge-position choice for the ending. Some rows below follow Truby's variants of moral argument *(fitted)*, among them tragedy and the simple good-against-bad form:

| Who learns | When | What the plot must supply |
|---|---|---|
| The hero, in time | before the final choice | a lesson that can still change the act, so the moral question stays open until then |
| The hero, too late | after the act that cannot be undone | the cost arriving, often watched from above once the audience sees it first |
| The hero, in part | a glimpse at the end that does not take hold | an ending that undercuts the insight |
| A witness | at the end, while the hero stays blind | a character whose view the audience shares (Kay) |
| The audience only | at the end | a clear view of what the hero cannot see; cut between the two (`scenes.md`, section 5) |
| Nobody needs to | never: the hero is good from the start and wins by being good | no moral suspense, so all the suspense must come from the threat |

## 8. The settlement

The Gap theory's fifth question tests every ending: what has changed from the opening normal, and what did it cost? Check four things: which mysteries closed (each one the story pressed), which wonders stay open (and were never pressed), how the normal differs, and the cost. Then the books' checks:
- **The end comes from the characters' own acts:** `structure-and-conflict.md`, section 7.
- **Three false endings** (Truby's *premature*, *arbitrary* and *closed* endings *(built for the first two, asserted for the third)*), read in Gap terms. Premature: the question that drove the story settles while pages remain, so what follows coasts or starts a second story. Arbitrary: the events stop without bringing the pressed gaps to a settlement. Closed: the settlement shuts the wonders along with the mysteries. A want that goes on past the last page, a fragile equilibrium or an unfinished insight can stay open, provided the story never pressed it as a question (it is a wonder). Whether the goal was won, and every other mystery the story pressed, must close. What the ending means is still how those gaps settle.
- **The ending should change the opening** *(asserted)*. Reread the first scene after writing the last. If nothing in it now reads differently, plant something the ending will recast.
- **Residue** needs a threat the audience cannot rule out in its own world and something left unresolved: a threat still alive or a wonder left open, never a broken promise. The deepest residue comes from theme, when the threat gives a body to a fear the audience already carries (`suspense-and-fear.md`, section 8).

## 9. Rivals

**Prove a claim, or settle the gaps.** Egri: fix one claim before writing, take a side, and make the action prove it; without a clear claim the audience has nothing definite to wait for *(built as a steering tool; "prove" is loose, since the author controls every event)*. The Gap theory: meaning is how the gaps are settled, and a story may leave a question open on purpose as a wonder. The two agree about steering and part at the ending. The test: write the ending twice. In A, every thread closes to prove the claim. In B, every mystery the story pressed closes, but one question it never pressed (whether the hero was right, say, when no character ever made that their goal) is left open. Egri predicts B reads as muddled; the Gap theory predicts B lands and A deflates, since A explains a wonder, and that B turns to betrayal only if the open question had been pressed. The deciding change is how hard the telling pressed that question. (Egri himself admits that a writer may show a condition and leave the audience to judge it.)

**Is theme always moral?** Truby: theme is the writer's view of right action, argued through what characters do to each other. The owner theories: theme answers whatever question the world poses, which need not be about how to treat people (*Children of Men* asks about hope). The test: add a Truby-style final choice between two goods to a story whose world asks a non-moral question. Truby's view predicts the theme sharpens whatever the choice is about; the owner theories predict it sharpens only if the choice answers the world's question, and splits the meaning if it answers something else.

**Must every line serve the claim?** Egri: every line and move must further the proof; the Implied World theory: details that serve no purpose make a world feel real. The claim-level test: strip a draft of every detail that serves no claim. Egri predicts a tighter story; the owner theory predicts a world that feels like set dressing. The workshop's line: a scene earns its place by what it changes, and the world's own business counts (`scenes.md`, section 1); texture rides in passing. General form: `.claude/skills/story-world/references/world-materials.md`, section 9.

## 10. Traps

- **A character announcing the theme,** unless saying it plainly is natural for that character (see `symbols.md`, section 6).
- **A mood in place of a claim.** "Life is wonderful" has no verb of struggle and no end.
- **A fake final choice where the moral line was meant to carry suspense.** Good against bad reveals nothing; if that is the form, load the suspense onto the threat.
- **A straw opponent.** If his case tempts nobody, the argument is rigged.
- **A claim or question that switches midway.** The character built for the first cannot carry the second.
- **Answering the moral question early.** The moral line then has no suspense left.
- **Leaving a pressed mystery open and calling it depth.** That is betrayal, not a wonder.
- **Letting the claim strip out the world.** Surplus is not waste.
