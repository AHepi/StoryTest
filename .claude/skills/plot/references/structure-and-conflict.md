# Structure and conflict

*Sources: the Gap theory (the story and plot terms; the breach gap; principles 2 Breach and 7 Settlement; Part 2 section 2, a character chasing something the world resists; questions 1 and 5), with the Implied World theory's point that the world sets the price of things, and the Anticipation theory (the uncertainty and time terms, the ratchet, and its rule that care comes before the threat, read in Gap terms as the normal before the breach); Truby, The Anatomy of Story (the seven key steps, his longer step list regrouped here as jobs, plot strategies and shapes, kinds of opening, the plan that fails, the single low point, the battle as the place where everything converges), Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing (rising, foreshadowed, static and jumping conflict, transition, attack and counterattack, the tie between opponents, point of attack, crisis, climax and resolution), and McKee, Dialogue (inciting incident, story values, the spine of action, forces of antagonism, turning points and progression); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 What structure is for. 2 The seven jobs. 3 More jobs for a longer plot. 4 Where to start. 5 How conflict moves. 6 Values, the spine and four kinds of opposition. 7 Crisis, climax and resolution. 8 Shapes of plot. 9 When the middle sags. 10 Rivals. 11 Traps.

This module is about the story in the Gap theory's sense (what happens, in the order it happened: people with desires causing and suffering change, inner change included) and about the breach gap: how the break from the world's normal escalates until the ending settles it. When the audience learns each fact is a matter for the plot's schedule, in `reveals-and-withholding.md`. The books' step lists are fitted from past plays and films. Use them to find a job that is missing, not as a mould.

## 1. What structure is for

**Case.** *Die Hard*. The normal: a New York cop, estranged from his wife, flies to her firm's Christmas party in Los Angeles. The breach: armed men take the building. Everything after is that breach escalating, each step caused by the last. He is alone, then unarmed, then barefoot on broken glass; the police outside misread the situation; the thieves learn who his wife is. The settlement: the thieves are beaten, the marriage is mended, and the price has been paid in plain view. Remove any one escalation and the next loses its cause. That is what makes it a structure rather than a list.

**Point.** Structure is the chain by which each consequence of the breach makes the next one unavoidable, until the ending settles it. Check two things at every link:
- **Cause.** Each event grows out of the one before, and could not be removed without breaking something later. Truby's name for this is an *organic* plot *(built: an event imposed from outside shows the author's hand and shrinks the characters to game pieces)*. He adds a check worth keeping: the amount of scheming and incident should match how much this hero wants and what they are able to do.
- **Cost.** The price of each step is set by the world, not by the author's need for trouble (Gap principle 2). A breach that could happen anywhere has arbitrary stakes.

## 2. The seven jobs

Truby finds seven steps under every working story and argues they are the stages anyone goes through in solving a problem *(fitted from successful stories; the problem-solving reason is plausible but would fit many other lists)*. Read them as jobs. The names are his; the third column says where each job sits in the Gap theory.

| Job (Truby's name) | What it must get done | In Gap theory terms | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weakness and need | the hero is stuck in a way that costs something now; the need is the inner change that would free them | the hero's state at the opening, shaped by the world's normal (a desire the world has shaped); the inner change the settlement will measure | what is it costing, and whom, on the first pages? |
| Desire | a specific goal, provoked by the breach, that the audience can ride along with | the story: an agent with a desire; the breach turned into a want | will the audience know the moment it is won or lost? |
| Opponent | a resistance that keeps colliding with the want and answers each move harder; Truby's strongest version is one rival after the same prize *(fitted)*, not the only one | what resists the want; often the world's resistance given a face (Gap theory, Part 2 section 2) | name the face the resistance wears in each act: does it keep colliding with the want? Where it is society or the physical world (section 6), give it a face in each scene |
| Plan | how the hero means to win | a scheduling choice: a plan the audience sees lets it predict and fear; a hidden one buys surprise | does it aim at this opponent and this goal? |
| Battle | the final clash that decides who gets the goal | the breach at its height; plan who knows what when the blow falls (the knowledge gap) | is the main resistance there in person, or in its strongest face, not a stand-in? |
| Self-revelation | what the hero learns about themselves, or fails to | inner change in the story, read at the settlement; if the audience saw the need before the hero did, it also closes a knowledge gap (dramatic irony); given to a witness instead, it becomes a choice of knowledge position | does it answer the need point for point? |
| New equilibrium | the new stable state | the settlement: how the normal differs at the end, and what it cost (question 5) | set the last scene beside the first |

Three notes the table cannot hold:
- **Need and desire are separate jobs** *(built)*. The need is inner and hidden; the desire is outer and visible. The desire should push the hero into places that expose the need. If the two come out as one sentence, pull them apart. Building both in a person: `.claude/skills/character/references/building-a-character.md`, sections 4 and 5.
- **Work from the end** *(built)*. Fix the self-revelation first, then the need as its mirror, then the desire that would force the lesson. The Gap theory supplies the reason: meaning is read backwards from the ending, so the ending should aim every step.
- **The settlement is wider than the hero.** Truby mostly measures an ending by whether the hero now stands higher or lower. The Gap theory also asks how the world's normal has changed. A hero can stay the same while the world around them changes (see Rivals).

## 3. More jobs for a longer plot

For a feature film or a novel, Truby expands the seven into a list more than three times as long *(fitted; he says each story bends the order)*. Regrouped here as six jobs. A short piece may need none of them.

1. **The past that presses.** Something before the first page still hurts the hero and pulls against the desire; Truby calls it the *ghost*. Its explanation is a curiosity gap, and when to release it is in `reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 1. Egri's rule belongs here too *(built)*: the urgency of the opening must grow out of events that have already happened.
2. **A push from outside.** An event knocks the stuck hero into wanting. Truby's best version looks to the hero like a way out and is really the deepest trap *(built: the false relief is ironic and drops the hero into the main trouble)*. If the audience senses the trap before the hero does, it becomes suspense.
3. **Opposition in layers.** More than one opponent; part of the opposition hidden; perhaps an enemy posing as a friend (Truby's *fake-ally opponent*); the opponent's own plan and attacks written as fully as the hero's. Each hidden attack is a reveal waiting to happen, each shown one is suspense waiting to happen, and choosing between them is the knowledge position, scene by scene (`suspense-and-fear.md`, sections 6 and 11).
4. **Turns by discovery and by failure.** New facts force decisions and bend the want (`reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 3). The first plan fails against an opponent stronger than expected *(built: a plan that simply works removes uncertainty)*. Through the middle the hero's actions must develop, not repeat: four dates in four places are one beat played four times *(built)*.
5. **One low point, and a restart.** A moment when all seems lost, usually well past the middle (Truby places it around the two-thirds mark or a little after), followed by a discovery that makes winning possible again *(the placement fitted; "only one" asserted)*. A story that ends badly mirrors it with a false peak from which everything runs downhill. How low to go: `suspense-and-fear.md`, section 11.
6. **The narrowing and the proof.** Options and space shrink, often with a brush with death; the strands meet at the battle (section 7); a final choice proves what was learned (`premise-and-theme.md`, section 6).

One more job runs through all six: **the desire needs room to grow** *(built)*. Raise the stakes of the same want rather than swapping in a new one, which splits the spine. A want that starts at full pitch can only repeat. *Casablanca* breaks Truby's advice to start low and still works, because Rick's want can grow into a cause bigger than himself; so the real rule is room to grow.

A subplot is a whole second story and needs its own core jobs. How to weave it: `scenes.md`, section 7.

## 4. Where to start

**Case.** *Hamlet* opens after the murder and the hurried marriage; *A Doll's House* opens after the forgery and years of secret repayment. Egri's point *(built)*: good plays begin late, once something before the first page has made waiting impossible. Yet *Hamlet* also opens on frightened guards on a cold night and a court going about its business: the normal, already cracked, before the ghost gives the order.

**Point.** The owner theories decide where the story turns: at the breach, **measured against the world's own normal**, not only against the hero's balance (Gap principle 2). *Pride and Prejudice*'s entail is what makes a new tenant at Netherfield an event. The books then help to place and stage it:
- **Name the breach in the hero's life** with McKee's *inciting incident*, the first event that knocks the hero's life off balance *(a definition)*. If that event breaks nothing in the world's normal, check the stakes (section 10, Rivals).
- **Check that the moment is ripe** with Egri's *point of attack* *(built)*: what has already happened before page one that makes waiting impossible now? If nothing, start later.
- **Stage the stretch before the breach.** Show enough of the normal for the audience to price the breach (Gap principle 2), and **build care before the threat**, so the opening shows what could be lost (the Anticipation theory, Part 2 section 1). How much normal to show, and whether trouble is already under way on page one, is the staging choice; Truby sorts openings along much the same line *(fitted)*. A long, settled normal buys care and a clear price but needs a visible crack to hold attention. An opening with the hero already in trouble holds attention at once but must show the normal through the trouble. An opening with no want in sight has nothing to drive it until one appears.

Both owner checks pull against Egri's rule that the first line should start the conflict; the reconciling move is an opening whose friction is itself part of the normal (the rival is in `suspense-and-fear.md`, section 11).

Egri also warns *(built)* that a passive hero stalls a story: start at the moment pressure has built far enough that even they must act. His objection to opening on a struggle that is only inside the hero's head is a stage rule. In prose, where the mind is on the page, such an opening can be fully visible.

## 5. How conflict moves

Egri sorts conflict into four kinds *(a classification with built mechanisms)*:
- **Rising:** each clash caused by the last and harder than it, carried by small steps he calls *transitions*.
- **Foreshadowed:** the clash promised before it comes. An audience will sit through a long stretch without open conflict if a clash has been clearly promised (the time term: `suspense-and-fear.md`, section 1).
- **Static:** nothing rises, because the people want nothing, cannot decide, are too alike, or only talk.
- **Jumping:** a character leaps from one state to another without the steps between, which startles without convincing.

Three moves keep it rising:
- **Attack and counterattack** *(built)*. Every blow draws a harder answering blow, and nobody is knocked out before the end: after a heavy blow the loser must still have something to fight with.
- **Close the exits** *(built)*. List every way the hero or the opponent could walk away or split the difference. Close each one through character (they would never), through a rule of the world (the law or custom forbids it), or by removing it (the helper is dead or gone), and make each closing believable inside the story. Egri's *unity of opposites* is the tie that makes leaving impossible; in Anticipation terms it strips away help and ways out, as a lost refuge does. The Gap theory names the source: the world's rules set what leaving costs, as the law against a wife borrowing on her own signature does in *A Doll's House*.
- **Steps between the poles** *(built)*. Name where each main character starts and ends, and check that every scene moves them at least one step. A skipped step is a jump; a step held too long is static. At character level: `.claude/skills/character/references/change-and-growth.md`, section 4.

The owner theories add what Egri's list leaves out: conflict is not the only engine. A stretch full of conflict can still carry no suspense if care, uncertainty or time is at zero, and a quiet stretch can hold if a clash has been promised.

## 6. Values, the spine and four kinds of opposition

**Case.** *Casablanca* swings on one axis, looking after yourself against committing to others. Early on Rick refuses to take a risk for anyone; in the last scene he commits to a cause. Almost every scene moves that value one way or the other, and the ending settles it.

**Point.** McKee's tools *(built as definitions)*, each tied to a Gap term:
- A **value** is a pair of opposed conditions: life and death, loyalty and betrayal, hope and despair. A scene is an event only if the charge of at least one value changes. The moment it changes is McKee's *turning point*, which this skill calls a *turn* (scene by scene: `scenes.md`, section 1). One **core value** runs under the whole story.
- The **spine of action** is the one want the whole story serves (in Gap terms, the desire the breach provoked). However far a scene seems to wander, what people do in it should be a move in that pursuit; a scene whose action serves some other want has left the spine.
- **Opposition comes from four places** (McKee's *forces of antagonism*): inside the hero, close relationships, society, and the physical world. These are the faces the world's resistance can wear (Gap theory, Part 2 section 2). For each act, list what resists from each place, and let it press harder and closer as the story goes on; a plot that draws on only one tends to repeat itself.
- **Turns come from a world that answers back** *(built)*. A character acts expecting one response and gets another, harder or stranger. That difference between what was expected and what came back is where each turn comes from.

In Gap terms the core value is the axis on which the settlement lands. The Implied World theory's thematic fit points the same way: the world's main departure should embody the story's main question, and the core value names the poles of that question. See `premise-and-theme.md`, section 4.

## 7. Crisis, climax and resolution

Egri's three words *(built definitions, applied loosely in his own examples)*: a **crisis** is a moment when a decisive change could still go either way; the **climax** is where it tips; the **resolution** is what follows. They repeat in every act, each cycle ending higher and each resolution a partial release. That cycle as the ratchet: `suspense-and-fear.md`, section 7.

For the story's last cycle:
- **The final crisis is a choice with one option left.** In *A Doll's House* the husband throws every argument he has at Nora and each is answered, until leaving is the only road still open (Egri). So close every other route before the last choice, and the choice shows who the hero has become (McKee makes the same point *(fitted)*).
- **The battle is where the strands meet** (Truby *(built)*). In Gap terms it is the breach at its height, with every strand's question on the table at once. Bring the main characters and strands into one place, and let the clash settle whose view of the good life holds, not only who is stronger (`premise-and-theme.md`, section 7).
- **The climax comes from the characters' own acts** (Egri *(built)*). An accident, an illness or a rescue from outside proves nothing and shows the author's hand. Nobody softens out of character to make the ending work.
- **The resolution can be short or long** (Egri *(fitted)*). Shakespeare's tragedies close fast; Ibsen can spend most of an act. A long one needs conflict still alive in it.

The resolution is where the settlement is shown: which mysteries closed, which wonders stay open, how the normal differs and what it cost. What that means: `premise-and-theme.md`, section 8.

## 8. Shapes of plot

Truby's plot strategies and shapes *(fitted from literary history)* are most useful for naming a draft's shape and its usual failure:

| Shape | Good for | Usual failure | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Journey | change made physical along a road | episodic: every encounter the same beat, and early characters cannot come back | a small change at every stop; send others to the same destination with something hidden about them |
| One day, one place, one action | pressure | too little left to discover | plant more hidden agendas before the start, or loosen the frame |
| Few opponents with much hidden (Truby's *reveals plot*) | surprise after surprise | surprise where suspense would last longer | choose the knowledge position for each reveal |
| Circling one event, deeper each time | curiosity closed in stages | a loop that repeats what we already knew | every return adds a layer |
| Many strands in one group | comparison; the group as the unit of change | unrelated strands; care spread thin | every strand varies one question; keep a small inner circle |
| A genre's preset plot | smooth, large turns | generic: nothing that only this hero could meet | bend each beat until it fits this hero (`genre`) |

A related move *(fitted from one book, with a built mechanism)*: a first half heavy with world and cast can be narrowed by a goal with a clear finish line, often a mystery. In the first Harry Potter book the guarded trapdoor turns a loose school year into a detective story.

## 9. When the middle sags

Pin down the symptom first (SKILL.md, Diagnosing, step 1). Then run the checks. Several will often say yes at once. For each yes, write the one change that would fix that cause and not the others (add a deadline in one draft, a new fact in another), try them on paper, and let whichever cures the stall name the cause.
1. **Is a term of the suspense equation at zero?** No care for the people in it, an outcome nobody doubts, or no time to anticipate (the threat not shown ahead): `suspense-and-fear.md`, section 1. The terms multiply, so this is the first thing to check, and more conflict will not fix it.
2. **Is the desire still rising?** Same goal, higher stakes. A replaced goal splits the spine; a goal at full pitch from the start has nowhere to go.
3. **Does each scene turn?** Mark each scene's value at its start and end. A run where nothing changes is what McKee calls a *nonevent*, padded with explanation.
4. **Do the hero's actions develop?** Name each action's beat. Two with the same name are one beat done twice.
5. **Are new facts arriving?** Truby says a story moves by doing and by finding out *(asserted, but a useful count)*. A middle with no reveals has only the doing.
6. **Is the plan still working?** Then uncertainty is at zero.
7. **Can anyone walk away?** An open exit lets conflict go static.
8. **Is a slow stretch grouped in one block?** Split it and interleave it with a faster strand (`scenes.md`, section 7).
9. **Are there too many new things to learn at once?** The Implied World theory's attention budget: cut or delay facts, not conflict.

## 10. Rivals

**Where the breach is measured.** McKee: the inciting incident upsets the hero's personal balance. The Gap theory: the breach is a departure from the world's own normal, and the world sets its price. The change that tells them apart is to keep the opening event and remove the world rule that prices it. Take *Pride and Prejudice* without the entail: a rich tenant's arrival still disturbs the Bennet household. McKee's model predicts the story still runs, since a balance was upset; the Gap theory predicts the stakes go arbitrary, because nothing now makes a good marriage urgent. Readers' sense of what is at stake is the measure.

**Whose change the ending measures.** Truby: every story shows the hero change, or makes clear why they did not *(asserted; the "or why not" clause means it can hardly fail)*. The Gap theory: meaning is how all the gaps settle, including where the world's normal ends up. On which ending lands, the two do not separate: Truby's own *traveling angel* pattern (*Shane*, *Mary Poppins*) already measures those endings by the community that changes, and his "or why not" clause covers either ending. They part on the fix. Take a draft whose flawed hero ends unchanged and whose ending readers call flat. Truby's fix is to add a self-revelation, or to make the refusal to change visible and meaningful; the Gap theory's is to show the world's new normal and what it cost. Write both. Truby's view predicts the first cures the flatness; the Gap theory predicts the second does, provided the opening normal was shown, and that the first helps only where the hero's change was the question the story pressed.

**Covered in other modules.** Start with conflict or show the normal first; how low the low point should go; a steady climb or a ratchet; what boredom means: all in `suspense-and-fear.md`, section 11. Jumps against fair surprise: `reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 8.

## 11. Traps

- **Filling in step names instead of doing jobs.** A step with a label and no work in it is still missing.
- **A turn with no cause.** Remove the cause (`hard-to-vary`): if the turn would happen anyway, it was placed, not caused.
- **Escalating events but not the want.** Bigger explosions, same stakes.
- **Opening on the explosion before the normal.** The audience cannot yet see what could be lost, so the threat buys no care (the Anticipation theory).
- **Ending by rescue from outside.** It proves nothing and settles nothing.
- **One kind of opposition throughout.** The plot repeats its one note.
- **Treating conflict as the only engine.** Care, uncertainty, time and a manageable load of new facts matter as much.
