# Telling and perspective

*Sources: the Gap theory (the telling term; the perspective and knowledge gaps; principle 6; section 5 on the teller and the two media), with the Anticipation theory's knowledge positions and its prose-and-film table, the Bond theory's interiority lever and books-and-film table, and the Implied World theory's medium corollary; Truby, The Anatomy of Story (the storyteller device and its rules, scrambled and backward telling, the audience revelation), and McKee, Dialogue (talk aimed at the audience, first, second and third person, a narrator's settings, reported talk, direct telling and counterpoint); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 Every narrative has a teller. 2 Kinds of teller, and their settings. 3 The character-narrator, and when it earns its place. 4 Unreliable telling and fair play. 5 Setting the knowledge position. 6 What each medium makes cheap. 7 Choosing a teller. 8 Rivals. 9 Traps.

The teller is whoever or whatever delivers the plot: a narrator, a point-of-view character, a camera. A *narrator* is a teller who speaks in words. A *point-of-view character* is one whose knowledge the telling is held to, whether or not they narrate; holding the telling inside one mind like this is a *tight point of view*. The teller sets the limits on what can be known, and so on what the plot can fairly hold back. Two gaps live here: *perspective* (what happened against what the teller says) and *knowledge* (what the audience knows against what the characters know).

## 1. Every narrative has a teller

**Case.** *The Great Gatsby*. Nick Carraway tells it, after the events are over, from back home in the Midwest. He knows the ending. Early on he assures us that he holds back his judgements, and later that he is among the most honest people he knows, and both claims invite us to watch him. Gatsby's past reaches us in pieces as Nick learns it, which keeps us level with Nick and curious. One piece, the story of Gatsby's youth, Nick tells us he heard much later and has set down early on purpose: the teller acting as scheduler. The perspective gap runs underneath: what Nick says about his own part (the affair he helped arrange, the carelessness he blames on others) against what we come to suspect. And the telling changes him: he goes home.

**Point.** Answer four questions for any teller before fixing the reveal plan.
1. **Who or what delivers the plot?**
2. **What do they know, and when?** At the time of the events, or after the end?
3. **What is their bias?** For a teller who is a character, McKee's question is the sharpest *(built)*: what do they want from the audience? To inform, confess, justify, seduce, recruit. The bias follows the want.
4. **So what may they fairly hold back?** (Section 4, and `reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 7.)

Truby puts the choice of teller first in his order of work, because it controls how every fact reaches the audience *(asserted)*. The Gap theory does not order the steps, but its principle 6 agrees on the stake: the teller limits what the plot can fairly hold back. So settle the teller before the reveal plan is locked. `SKILL.md` drafts the schedule of knowledge with a working teller in mind and checks it once the teller is chosen. Truby's "no storyteller", the unseen all-knowing voice of most popular work, is still a teller in the theory's sense. It is simply not a character. A camera is a teller too: what it shows is its knowledge, and where it points and lingers is its bias.

## 2. Kinds of teller, and their settings

| Teller | Knows | The gap it makes cheap | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unseen and all-knowing | any mind, any place | knowledge: can put the audience ahead at will | hiding what it knows looks like the author's hand |
| Third person, inside one mind (*close third*) | what that character perceives and thinks | knowledge: level with that character | slipping into things the character could not know |
| Third person, outside every mind | behaviour only | knowledge: inner life stays hidden and is read from behaviour | reads cold; inner change must show in acts |
| First person, the hero | what she lived, and what she knows now | perspective: what she says against what happened | knowing the end and hiding it for no reason in character |
| First person, a witness or confidant (Watson, Nick) | what they saw and were told | perspective, plus curiosity about the hero | reporting minds they could not see into |
| First person plural, "we" | a group's knowledge and rumour | perspective: a shared bias | losing track of who "we" is |
| A camera | what is framed and heard | knowledge: shows what characters cannot see | forgetting that framing is a choice |

**A narrator's settings.** For a narrator who is not a character, the Gap theory's telling term gives two settings: its *knowledge* (every mind, one mind, none) and its *bias* (from even-handed to openly judging). The workshop adds two, taking the idea of such scales from McKee *(fitted)*: *honesty* (a narrator outside the story who deceives is rare and has to be set up) and *visibility* (does the reader feel someone telling, or forget anyone is?). Set each at the start and keep to it. A change mid-book is a change of teller and should be chosen, not drifted into.

**Second person.** McKee *(fitted)*: "you" always hides an I or a he. Swap the pronoun to find which, and write it knowing which it is.

## 3. The character-narrator, and when it earns its place

Truby calls a visible narrator who is a character in the story *the storyteller*, and gives a set of techniques for using one; this skill says **character-narrator**, and regroups what matters under the Gap theory's terms. Using one is a structural choice, not decoration *(asserted, with a built core)*.

**What it buys and what it costs.** A second channel beside the events, for comment. A bias the audience can read past, which is the perspective gap made visible. Freedom of order, since memory need not run in sequence and a remembering mind can hold a long or wandering story together. Hindsight, with its sense of loss, since the events are already over. The costs are speed, and some distance: the audience knows it is being told.

**What a frame can do.** The *frame* is the present-day situation in which the character-narrator tells the story. The jobs below are candidates, fitted from past cases *(fitted)*, not a checklist; a frame earns its place if it does at least one of them for real, and the empty-bookend test below is the gauge.
1. **Bias: the perspective gap.** The teller wants something from the audience, or cannot see what the audience can. Humbert in *Lolita* argues his own case and the reader reads past him; Stevens in *The Remains of the Day* cannot say what the reader sees. This is the one job the Gap theory itself backs (principle 6).
2. **Reordering: the withholding gap.** The frame lets the plot release facts out of story order. *Atonement*'s last pages change what the whole book was; the narrator of *The Usual Suspects* builds the past as he goes.
3. **Present stakes.** The telling happens under pressure and will make things better or worse: Salieri confessing to a priest after trying to kill himself in *Amadeus*; Jamal answering a police interrogation in *Slumdog Millionaire*.
4. **Change in the teller: the settlement.** The telling moves the teller. Give them something they do not yet understand, let the recounted past reach the present with room left after it, and let an insight show in a choice: Nick goes home. Truby places the catch-up near the three-quarter mark *(fitted and loose: one of his own examples stops earlier)*. The ironic version withholds the insight on purpose, so that the reader sees what the teller will not (Truby's reading of *Goodfellas*).

**Tests.** *Empty bookend:* remove the frame and reread; if nothing is lost (no bias, no reordering, no present stakes, no change in the teller), cut it or give it one of the jobs above. *Layers:* for each extra frame, name what it adds that outweighs the distance it costs (a rival with the Bond theory, section 8). *Last words:* a dying word that seems to unlock a whole life is a pressed question; decide whether it is a mystery or a wonder, and see the rival in `reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 8.

**Scrambled and backward telling.** Truby's term *antiplot* covers rotating narrators, broken time and branching structure used to show a character in finer detail *(built, but loose: almost any structure can be defended this way afterwards)*. Test each fragment: what does it show about the character that a straight line could not? Cut the fragments that answer "nothing". Backward telling, as in Pinter's *Betrayal* or *Memento*, makes the audience work out the causes consciously *(built)*. As a diagnostic, list your scenes last to first: where you cannot say how a scene grew out of the one before, the causal link is weak.

## 4. Unreliable telling and fair play

A teller who hides what they know is cheating, unless the hiding is part of who they are (Gap principle 6). Stevens' evasions in *The Remains of the Day* are the story. The narrator of *The Usual Suspects* lies by nature and by trade, so his lie is fair. *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd* is still argued over, a century on, on exactly this point: whether its narrator's silence is part of who he is.

Ways to make unreliability a design choice:
- **Name the want.** What the teller wants from the audience sets the slant. McKee *(built)*: talk aimed at the audience is still an act done to a listener.
- **Plant one blind spot** the audience can see and the teller cannot *(Truby, built)*. That is where irony lives.
- **Read "trust me" as a cue.** A teller who vouches for their own honesty invites doubt *(Truby, built)*. Decide what they are hiding, and let the audience catch it.
- **Use reported talk for its slant.** When a character retells what someone else said, the audience learns her reading of that person, not the words *(McKee, built)*.
- **Try counterpoint** (McKee's term, *fitted*). A narrating voice that pulls against the drama makes meaning from the distance between them: Thackeray's narrator in *Vanity Fair* keeps puncturing what his characters believe about themselves.

**Test.** For each thing the teller withholds, mark one: *could not know*, *would not say because of who they are*, *a wonder* (never promised an answer), or *neither*. A secret a character keeps and fires later counts under the first two only (`reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 7). Every *neither* is a twist held back by the author, which is the theory's question 4.

## 5. Setting the knowledge position

The teller sets the knowledge position scene by scene (`suspense-and-fear.md`, section 6).
- **Level**: a tight point of view (close third, or first person told as it happens), found footage, a narrator who learns as we do. Truby notes that *Alien* never lets the audience get ahead of its crew, and the fear rises for it *(fitted)*.
- **Ahead**: an all-knowing teller; a cut to the opponent at work; a chapter from the villain's side; a narrator who knows the ending ("had I known then"); a prologue that announces the end, as in *Romeo and Juliet*.
- **Behind**: an outside teller showing a character react to what we cannot see; a narrator who knows and withholds, fair only under section 4.

**Point.** In close third or first person there are three ways to put the audience ahead: switch teller, use a teller who already knows the end, or let the audience read past a teller who misreads what she sees (the perspective gap doing the knowledge gap's work). Decide early which of these the book allows, because adding one late reads as a change of rules.

## 6. What each medium makes cheap

The Gap theory: film's natural gap is between audience and character (the camera can show a bomb the characters cannot see; editing can cut between people who do not know about each other), and prose's natural gap is between the telling and the truth (voice, inner life, slippage). So film tends to excel at suspense and dramatic irony, and prose at the irony of voice.

| | Prose | Stage | Screen |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leans on | the reader's imagination | the ear | the eye |
| Inner life | built in: first person, close third | borrowed: soliloquy and aside | borrowed: voice-over, looks to camera, confidants |
| Cheap for the teller | direct telling: fast, compressed | telling the audience what other characters do not know | showing what characters cannot see |
| Typical failure | the info-dump; "and then" narration | speeches that repeat what scenes show | exposition dialogue, title crawls; voice-over that recites background |

The first row is McKee's *(fitted)*. In the last row the info-dump, exposition dialogue and title crawls are the Implied World theory's, and the rest are McKee's cases; the middle rows join the owner theories' books-and-film tables with his.

Two questions to ask of any voice-over, the workshop's distillation of McKee's survey of screen narration *(fitted)*: does the audience trust it, and does it agree with the pictures or pull against them? Holly's flat, dreamy voice-over in *Badlands* pulls against the killings it narrates, and the distance is the point. A look to camera recruits the audience as a confidant, which in Bond theory terms buys alignment: *Fleabag* builds that alliance on purpose, then turns it into a question about her. Direct telling in prose is fast *(built)*: *Slaughterhouse-Five* runs through Billy Pilgrim's whole life in a few pages before the story proper starts. Its danger is a flat list of events; McKee's cure *(built)* is to break the summary with something dramatised, such as a remembered exchange or the narrator quarrelling with themselves.

**Adapting.** Prose builds by adding and film by taking away (Implied World theory), so filming a book forces a decision on everything the author left unnamed (the Implied World theory's gaps). Prose techniques (inner talk, reported talk, a narrator's comment) need replacements on screen: voice-over, a confidant, a reaction shot, picture set against sound.

## 7. Choosing a teller

1. **Which gap should the audience live in most?** Perspective: a character-narrator, or close third with a visible bias. Knowledge: an unseen teller or a camera free to cut away. Withholding about the past (curiosity): a teller who learns it as we do.
2. **Answer the four questions** of section 1.
3. **Check the reveal plan against the teller, both ways.** Every withheld fact must pass the test in section 4; everything the plan puts the audience ahead on must be something this teller can deliver, or use one of the routes in section 5.
4. **For a character-narrator, check that the frame does at least one job** of section 3 for real.
5. **Run `hard-to-vary`.** Swap the teller for its nearest neighbour (first person for close third; the confidant for the hero). What breaks? Name it: that is what holds the choice in place. If nothing breaks, the choice is free; say so plainly rather than dressing it up as necessary.

## 8. Rivals

**Frames and feeling.** Truby: every narrator's frame puts distance between story and audience, and more frames drain more feeling *(built)*. The Bond theory: access to a mind (interiority) is the most powerful lever of care. The two may meet if the cost falls on the characters inside the told story while the teller gains. That gives a test: tell one chapter in the hero's own first person, and again as recalled by a later narrator. If the frame lowers care for the hero but raises it for the narrator, both hold; if it lowers care for everyone, Truby's cost is real and the frame needs a job that repays it.

**Does a third-person narrator have a voice?** McKee: no; a narrator who is not a character has only the author's style, and draws neither sympathy nor curiosity *(built, but by definition)*. The Gap theory: every teller carries knowledge and bias that shape what can be known, and voice and irony live in the gap between teller and truth. The test: a third-person narrator with a steady, marked judgement, such as the ironic narrator of *Pride and Prejudice*. If readers find irony at the characters' expense in the narrator's framing, the teller's bias is doing work, as the theory says. The workshop keeps third-person bias as something to design, with the four settings of section 2.

**One audience revelation, or a choice in every scene.** Truby's single, late, distancing audience revelation against the theory's position chosen scene by scene: see `suspense-and-fear.md`, section 11.

## 9. Traps

- **Fixing the teller after the reveals are locked.** Every reveal depends on it; changing it late unpicks the schedule. Draft with a working teller and settle it before the reveal plan is fixed.
- **A frame with no job.** Remove it; if nothing is lost, it was an empty bookend.
- **A point of view that leaks.** The close-third character suddenly knows what she could not.
- **The teller who knows and hides for no reason.** A twist made of cheating.
- **Voice as decoration.** Quirks of phrasing that reveal nothing about what the teller wants or cannot see.
- **Forgetting the camera's bias.** What is framed, and for how long, is a telling choice like any narrator's.
