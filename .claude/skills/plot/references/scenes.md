# Scenes

*Sources: the Gap theory (the plot term: order, selection and emphasis; principles 1 Nested inference, 3 Pressure and 4 Scheduling; the five gaps), the Anticipation theory (care built before the threat; knowledge positions, the time term, the ratchet), the Implied World theory (attention budget; indifference and surplus; the guide for a world with many departures) and the Bond theory (care cannot be ordered; a small inner circle); Truby, The Anatomy of Story (scene weave and scene list, ordering by development, placing scenes side by side, crosscutting, interleaving slow and fast strands, subplots and many-strand weaves; scene construction: the scene as a small story, its design questions, the key line placed last, open and covert tactics, entering late, subtext as a choice, opening and closing scenes), McKee, Dialogue (scene intention, value change and turning points, beats named by action, repeated tactics, background desires, nonevents), and Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing (every scene a whole play in small, attack and counterattack, kinds of resistance, entrances and exits, exposition through conflict, the strongest fact last); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 What a scene must change. 2 Building one scene. 3 Beats. 4 Getting in and out. 5 Opening and closing scenes. 6 The scene list. 7 Weaving strands. 8 Rivals. 9 Traps.

A **scene** is one action in one place and stretch of time (Truby's definition). A **strand** is one line of action followed across scenes: the main plot, a subplot, one hero's line in an ensemble. This module decides what each scene does and where it goes. How the talk inside it is worded, line by line, belongs to the `dialogue` skill (`.claude/skills/dialogue/SKILL.md`).

## 1. What a scene must change

**Case.** *Jaws*, the crowded beach. The audience knows a shark may be in the water; Brody watches every swimmer, and so do we. A scream turns out to be a game, the false alarm eases us, and then a boy on a raft is killed. The scene changes two gaps at once. The breach escalates: a child is dead, the cost of keeping the beaches open is now plain, and the town can no longer pretend. The knowledge position shifts: Brody and the audience were ahead of the bathers, and now everyone knows.

**Point.** A scene earns its place when it changes the state of at least one gap. Name which:

| Gap | The scene... |
|---|---|
| Breach | moves the story on: a value's charge changes (McKee's *value*, a pair such as hope and despair: `structure-and-conflict.md`, section 6); the want, the odds or the cost changes |
| Withholding | opens a question, answers one, or makes a promise (a set-up to be paid off) |
| Knowledge | shifts who knows what: audience ahead, level or behind |
| Perspective | exposes the teller's slant, or the distance between what is said and what happened |
| Departure | lets the audience learn a departure (a way the world differs from the baseline), shown under pressure, not in a lecture (Gap principle 3) |

If none of them moves, the scene is what McKee calls a *nonevent*: cut it, merge it, or give it a turn. McKee gives two ways to turn a scene (his *turning point*; this skill says *turn*) *(built)*: an action, or a revelation (a secret told, a fact found out). Actions mostly move the breach; revelations mostly move withholding and knowledge.

**The world's own business.** A scene that shows the world going about business of its own, with nothing to do with the hero (a strike, a market day, a festival), is not a nonevent, in the workshop's reading. Where the business shows a departure, it changes the departure gap. Where it is baseline business (a market day in a realist town; *Alien*'s crew grumbling about bonus shares), it changes no gap, but it does the Implied World theory's work: a world with business of its own, and detail that serves no plot, is what makes a world feel real (principles 4 and 5). It is the one kind of scene the workshop lets stay without changing a gap. The attention budget (Implied World principle 2) limits how long it may run, and it does most when it also does a plot job, as *Alien*'s opening does (section 8). Whether a whole scene of world business with no other job may stay is a question put to the owner; the workshop's present reading is that it may, if it is short and shows the world under some pressure (Gap principle 3).

## 2. Building one scene

Truby treats a scene as a small, complete story that must also push the whole story forward, and says the whole wins when the two pull apart *(asserted)*. Egri says every scene holds a whole play in small: each person's aim, a first friction, steps, a crisis, a climax, and what is settled *(asserted by analogy; a demanding standard that quiet scenes may not meet)*. Joined with the owner theories, that gives a scene card. Fill it in before drafting; a blank field is a diagnosis.

1. **Job and gap.** Which job of the plot the scene serves (`structure-and-conflict.md`, sections 2 and 3) and which gap it changes (section 1 above); the same two columns as the scene list in section 6. Work back from where the arc or relationship ends: the first scene between Rick and Renault in *Casablanca* only reads as crucial once you know they walk off as allies *(Truby, built)*.
2. **Driver.** Whose want powers the scene; not always the hero's. McKee's test for the want of the moment *(built)*: grant it, and the scene should be over.
3. **Resistance.** Who or what opposes, and over what. A place can resist.
4. **Tactic.** Open or covert: does the driver name the want, or hide it behind another? Truby notes the trade *(built)*: an open tactic spends the conflict now, a covert one banks it for the moment it is found out. A covert tactic is a reveal waiting to happen, so schedule that moment (`reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 3).
5. **Knowledge position.** Ahead, level or behind, and about what (`suspense-and-fear.md`, section 6). For a covert tactic, decide who knows the hidden want: the other character, the audience, or neither.
6. **Care and time.** What could be lost here, whether the audience already cares, and how long it sees the threat coming before it lands (a clock or deadline is one way to stretch that). Truby's list has no field for these; the Anticipation theory does.
7. **Turn.** Action or revelation, and the moment the value flips.
8. **Landing.** The scene ends where the driver's want is won, lost or blocked, and its key line goes there too, since what comes last carries the most weight *(Truby, built on emphasis by position)*. Write the last line first.

**Subtext is a choice, not a virtue** (Truby *(built)*). A character hiding a want sets up a gap. If the audience does not know the hidden want either, it is withheld, a question to be paid when it comes out (withholding). If the audience knows and the other character does not, the audience is ahead (knowledge). Hiding costs open conflict, so choose it when these people, here, would not say it. McKee's *background desires* say what each person would risk by speaking openly (a job, a marriage, their standing, their picture of themselves): the more such ties, the more restraint *(built)*.

## 3. Beats

McKee's *beat* is one action and the reaction it draws *(built)*. Name each with a pair of "-ing" words: accusing and mocking; pleading and refusing. For a scene carried by talk, the beat table and its tests are in `.claude/skills/dialogue/references/line-and-scene-design.md`, section 2. The names are the tool:
- **Find the stall.** One tactic repeated in fresh words is one beat, however many speeches it fills. A scene is stuck until someone changes tactic, and a character pressing the same tactic speech after speech is the commonest sign of a dead scene. Fix the beats before touching the words.
- **Make the reply strike back** (Egri *(built)*). A charge that is accepted, dodged or merely rebuked kills the exchange. A counter-charge, best of all one that turns the accusation on the accuser, lifts it.
- **Vary the resistance** (Egri *(fitted from one scene, with a clear mechanism)*). Attack and counterattack alone tire an audience. A cold refusal to explain, a silence, a concession made for hidden reasons (Nora agreeing with Helmer because she has already decided to leave) let the audience watch resistance gather that the attacker cannot see.
- **Put the strongest fact last** (Egri *(built)*). A speech or scene that opens with its bombshell can only fall away. Move it to the end and give the listener a reaction to each step before it.
- **Climb, with releases.** Egri and McKee want each beat to top the last *(asserted)*; the Anticipation theory rivals that with partial releases and a pattern broken before it is learned (`suspense-and-fear.md`, section 11). An unaware character's light remark can ease the room and raise the stakes at once, as when, in *Shadow of a Doubt*, the killer's sister cheerfully brings the talk back to her women's club and a widow she means him to meet.

## 4. Getting in and out

- **Enter late** (Truby *(asserted)*). Open the scene as near its turn as it can stand. The owner theories say what it cannot lose: the care and the shown threat that suspense multiplies. Cut the first half page; if the scene still plays but the worry drops, restore only what built the worry.
- **Every entrance and exit caused** (Egri *(built)*). A character comes on or goes off for a reason of their own, and the move changes the conflict. Someone who remembers an errand just as two others need to be alone is the author's hand showing. A character talked about before they appear arrives with weight (Egri; Truby on Laszlo and Ilsa in *Casablanca*), but the Bond theory's limit applies: the build-up works only if they then earn it in front of us.
- **Leave on a push.** The last line should open a question the next scene answers or deepens: a small promise (Gap principle 5).
- **Background through conflict** (Egri *(built)*). Let the past come out while people fight. Someone with a practical reason to dig, such as a policeman questioning a suspect or a suspicious relative, can draw a history out without a lecture *(Truby, built)*. Exposition fired as a weapon: `reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 5.

## 5. Opening and closing scenes

- **The opening sets out the ground and plays as a scene** (Truby *(built)*). It has two jobs at once: show, in small, the ground the whole story will cover, and turn on its own like any other scene. One way is to open on the central figure's typical activity inside a gathering that brings the world together: *The Godfather* opens at a wedding with the Don hearing requests, which is the normal before the breach, in Gap terms, with a world's rules shown through behaviour. Let an outsider's want drive such a scene, so the audience learns the rules with them (the Implied World theory's guide).
- **Act out the story's movement in small** *(fitted)*. *Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid* opens on an outlaw sizing up a bank that has been fitted with bars and is locking up for the night, in a West that is closing in on men like him. Put the story's process into a verb and let the first scene's setting perform it.
- **A line wrong now and right later** *(built)*: an early remark that reads as a joke or an error and turns out true by the end. This is what the Gap theory, after Brooks, calls reading forwards in order to look back.
- **The closing scene echoes the opening** *(fitted)*: the same kind of place, the same kind of crisis, and the key word or line last. If the hero cannot learn, cut between their blindness and what the audience can see, so the audience learns instead (the last scene of *Butch Cassidy*).
- **Pack the final turns** *(fitted)*. In a climactic exchange, aim for a turn every one to three lines, as in the closing scene of *Casablanca*.

## 6. The scene list

Truby's last planning step is a list of every scene, one line each, naming its single core action *(built: detail hides structure)*. Add the columns this skill needs:

| # | One action | Strand | Job it serves | Gap it changes | Knowledge position | Tension, 1-10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Then work the list:
- **Reorder** the large blocks first, then check each pair of neighbours.
- **Merge.** A scene that exists to house one good line should hand the line to a neighbour. Keep one core action per scene.
- **Cut, then add.** Pace comes from which scenes are chosen, not only from their length. After cutting, read for jumps and add a scene where development skips a step.
- **Order by development, not by the clock** (Truby *(built)*). Choose each next scene for what it changes or sets up, not for what happens next in time. The Gap theory adds that the plot's order may leave the story's order on purpose.
- **Read neighbours as a pair** *(built)*. What does the second scene say about the first? On screen the cut is instant and can mean more than either scene.
- **Read each strand alone** *(built)*. Filter the list by strand: does it rise, turn and settle?
- **Chart the tension column.** A straight climb, a drop to zero, or three equal gaps between peaks in a row are the faults in `suspense-and-fear.md`, section 7.

Truby's counts are a sanity check only *(asserted)*: forty to seventy scenes in a feature film, roughly twice that in a novel.

## 7. Weaving strands

- **A crosscut does one of two jobs** (Truby *(built)*): suspense, because each cut leaves a strand hanging and faster cuts near a climax stretch time; or comparison, because two strands side by side invite the audience to compare them, which widens the theme. Decide which each crosscut is for. Cutting between strands often puts the audience ahead of the characters in each. *The Godfather*'s christening, cut against the killings Michael has ordered, does both at once.
- **Split a slow block and interleave it** *(built from Truby's before-and-after comparison of two drafts of The Godfather)*. Grouping a slow strand's scenes together makes one long stall; alternating them with a faster strand keeps the drive, and lets both arrive together at a low point that a big reveal then turns into momentum. *The Empire Strikes Back* runs Luke's training against the others' flight.
- **Stay near the main strand** *(built)*. Scenes that prepare it are fine; a long stretch away from it drains the drive.
- **A subplot must touch the main plot** (Truby *(built)*). It shows another character handling much the same problem, collides with the main plot (often near the end), begins as soon as the story can carry it, and does its own core jobs. Otherwise it is a separate story competing for time, and the main one drags. It always slows the drive, so spend it where the gain in theme is worth the cost.
- **Many strands** *(fitted from television, with built mechanisms)*. Every strand varies one question; each does its own core jobs or it reads as a distraction; one strand's hero can serve as another's opponent, which holds the cast together; strands can converge like a funnel (*L.A. Confidential*'s three detectives). The Bond theory's warning stands: keep a small inner circle, or care thins out.

## 8. Rivals

**Must every scene serve the hero?** Truby: cut any scene that neither advances nor sets up the hero's development *(built, but narrow: it forgets scenes that serve the opponent, the world or suspense)*; the Implied World theory: a world with business of its own feels real. The scene-level test: cut *Alien*'s opening, the crew grumbling about their bonus shares before anything alien appears. Truby's view predicts a faster start and no loss; the owner theory predicts the Company's betrayal lands lighter and the ship feels like a set. The workshop's rule is in section 1. General form: `.claude/skills/story-world/references/world-from-story.md`, section 11.

**Every beat tops the last, or a ratchet.** See `suspense-and-fear.md`, section 11.

## 9. Traps

- **A scene that changes no gap.** It spends attention and buys nothing. The exception is a short scene of the world at its own business, which does the Implied World theory's work instead (section 1; the workshop's reading, pending the owner).
- **A blank driver or resistance on the card.** No want, no scene; no resistance, no conflict.
- **Rewriting the words when the beats are wrong.** Fix the actions underneath first.
- **The same tactic in new words.** One beat, repeated.
- **A slow strand grouped in one block.** Interleave it.
- **A crosscut with no job.** It neither stretches time nor compares.
- **Entrances and exits for the author's convenience.**
- **Cutting the world's own business as padding.** Keep it short, and let it ride inside scenes that do a plot job where it can (section 1).
- **Characters announcing what they feel.** Move the feeling under an action; the line-level fix is in `dialogue`.
