# Building a cast

*Sources: the Bond theory's principle 5 (care is social), principle 4 (balance the levers, with its Holmes and Watson case), the limits "too many characters dilute care" and "the bond is a promise", and the Connection and Interiority levers; the Implied World theory's baseline and principles 4 (indifference) and 5 (surplus), applied to people; the Anticipation theory's lost refuge; the Gap theory, Part 2 section 6 (theme as the answer to the world's question); Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing (the chapters on the pivotal character, orchestration and unity of opposites, and his later notes on stakes, dependence and stock types); Truby, The Anatomy of Story (the character chapter: the character web, story functions, archetypes, variations on a theme, love and buddy stories, many heroes, cutting; the moral-argument chapter: one problem across the cast; the plot chapter: allies, subplots, attack by ally); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 A cast is built together. 2 Jobs in the story. 3 Who drives, and whom we follow. 4 Contrast. 5 One question, many answers. 6 Types, gifts and shadows. 7 The tie that keeps them in. 8 Care through the others. 9 How many, and who to cut. 10 Two leads, or many. 11 When the sidekick outshines the hero. 12 Rivals. 13 Traps.

Words used in one sense only in this file: the **cast** is every character the story gives scenes to; a **job** is what the story needs a character to do (Truby's *story function*); the **driver** is the character who starts the conflict and keeps it going (Egri's *pivotal character*; `change-and-growth.md` uses the same word); a **tie** is whatever stops two characters walking away from each other (Egri's *unity of opposites*; never called a bond here, because *bond* in this skill always means the audience's relationship with a character); the **inner circle** is the few characters the audience is meant to bond with deeply. *Hero*, *lead*, *opponent*, *villain* and *desire* are used as `SKILL.md` defines them. Provenance marks: *built*, *fitted* or *asserted*, as in `hard-to-vary`.

## 1. A cast is built together

**Case.** Truby's reading of *Tootsie*: people credit the star in a dress, but the film works because each figure in it is another answer to how men treat women, and the hero is measured against all of them. Take away the dress and the cast still argues the question; take away the cast and the dress is a sketch.

**Point.** A character is defined largely by who they are not *(Truby, built on contrast: a quality shows only against a different one nearby)*. So do not build the hero alone and fill in the others later: the hero comes out thin and the others thinner. Build the cast as one design, Truby's *character web*, and compare every character with every other. Each comparison forces out something that sets the hero apart, and turns a minor figure into a person. Bond principle 5 is the audience's side of the same fact: we read characters as we read people, partly through how others treat them and how they treat others. A cast built together gives the audience that evidence.

- *Test:* for each pair of main characters, finish the sentence "unlike X, this one...". Where you cannot, the two blur (section 4).
- *Swap test* (`hard-to-vary`): exchange two characters in a key scene. If nothing anyone does changes, they are one character in two coats.
- *Dependence* (Egri, *asserted*, with a usable test): nobody in a story is self-sufficient. List whom each main character depends on for money, love, standing and safety. A character who needs no one has nowhere to be pressed.

## 2. Jobs in the story

Every character does a job for the story as a whole *(Truby, asserted)*. His jobs, grouped here by where each stands towards the hero:

- **Against.** The *main opponent* (Truby's term), the one most set on stopping the hero, ideally after the same prize, and any lesser opponents. Built in `opponents.md`.
- **With.** The *ally* helps, and is someone the hero talks to, so the audience hears what the hero values. In film, where Interiority has to be borrowed, a confidant is one of the ways to borrow it (the Bond theory's books-and-film table). The fastest way to make an ally a person is a desire of their own *(Truby, built)*: the Scarecrow wants a brain.
- **Hidden.** The *fake-ally opponent* seems to be on the hero's side and works against him. Truby finds him among the richest figures in a cast, because in playing the friend he half becomes one *(fitted)*. Renault in *Casablanca* works with the Germans, fairly openly, and crosses to Rick's side at the end: a turn that surprises and turns out to have been in him (Bond principle 7). The *fake-opponent ally* seems to fight the hero and in fact helps: the harsh teacher. Lecter tests Clarice harder than her instructors do and trains her better.
- **Beside.** The *subplot character*, in Truby's narrow sense: someone facing the hero's problem who handles it differently and ends differently *(a definition, and a productive one)*. Hamlet's father was murdered by design, Laertes' father killed in Hamlet's rash blunder; Laertes rushes to revenge where Hamlet delays. Truby's condition: the parallel line must hit the main one or be cut *(built)*. Laertes ends up fighting Hamlet at the climax.

Moves:

- Fill the jobs for your cast. Empty ones are normal; ask whether filling one would help.
- Chart each character's stance towards the hero (against, with, mixed) scene by scene. Truby: most of a story's turns come from loyalties rising and falling *(asserted)*. A long stretch where no stance moves is likely to feel flat.
- When the audience learns of a hidden loyalty (before the hero, or with him) is a `plot` choice: `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`.
- A job is not a person. Truby's list says what the story needs; the Bond theory warns that a character who is only a job is a function. Give each working character a desire of their own and one thing they would not do (the workshop's reading).

## 3. Who drives, and whom we follow

**Case.** In *Othello* the man who starts the trouble and keeps it going is Iago, passed over for promotion. In *A Doll's House* it is Krogstad, fighting to win back his standing for his children's sake (Egri's readings). Neither is the character the audience spends most time with.

**Point.** Egri's *pivotal character*, here the **driver**: the one who starts the conflict, keeps pushing it, knows what they want, and without whom nothing happens *(asserted and fitted; loose, since Egri moves the label between characters of the same play, so use it as a question, not a finding)*. Two parts of it rest on a mechanism *(built)*:

- **Necessity, not whim.** A driver pushed by whim can stop at any moment, and the story stops with them. One pushed by something they cannot afford to lose, such as their name or a child's safety, often for someone else's sake, cannot. A storm or a plague cannot drive; the person who refuses to accept it can (the plague in *Oedipus* forces the hunt for the killer).
- **The driver has already decided.** So the driver covers the shortest distance of change, and the job is to force others to change (`change-and-growth.md`, section 5).

The Bond theory adds a split Egri does not make: the character who drives the conflict and the character the audience bonds with need not be the same. Alignment (whose company we keep, whose mind we see into) decides whom we follow; the driver decides what happens. *Test:* name the driver, and name the character with the most time in the audience's company and the most access to their mind. If there is no driver, the draft has no engine. If the driver is the opponent, that is common; build him as a person (`opponents.md`).

## 4. Contrast

**Case.** *The Odd Couple*: one man anxious and tidy, the other easy and a slob, sharing a flat. Neither can do the smallest thing the other's way, so every ordinary act becomes a clash (the workshop's example).

**Point.** Egri's *orchestration* (his term): choose the cast so they differ *(built: people alike in temper and outlook have nothing to fight about)*. Two characters can share a type or a trade as long as they would choose differently in the same room.

- **Swap the central pair** *(Egri, built)*. Replace one of them with another character already in the cast and rerun the key scene. Egri's own swap puts the sensible Mrs Linde beside Helmer: no forgery, so no play. If the conflict survives the swap, the pairing was not doing enough.
- **Fit the contrast to the size of the change** *(Egri, built)*. A pair built for murderous hatred swamps a quiet story of friction; a pair built for small frictions vanishes in a tragedy. Place each key trait on a scale and check that the two sit far enough apart for the clash you want, and no further. A trait left vague is a cosmetic one.
- **Pair tempers on purpose** *(Egri, built)*. Two hot-heads flare and burn out in a line or two. Set a volatile character against a patient one (Molnár's Liliom and Julie) and the change slows to a pace the audience can follow.
- **Contrast must move.** At the midpoint and the end, check the key pairs: has the contrast shifted, reversed or deepened? If it looks as it did in the first scene, the pair has stopped changing *(Egri, built, though a stall can have other causes too)*.
- **Voices.** When voices sound alike, widen the gap between the people before rewriting lines; Egri expects distinct speech to follow from distinct people *(built, and overstated)*. The lines themselves belong to `.claude/skills/dialogue/references/voice.md`.

**The side-by-side table** *(Truby's comparison, a working method)*: a row for each main character; columns for desire, flaw and need, values, power and standing, and answer to the story's central question (section 5). Empty cells, or two rows that read alike, show where the cast is weak. Start with hero against main opponent, then the other opponents, then the allies.

## 5. One question, many answers

**Case.** *Pride and Prejudice* asks what a woman in that world should marry for, and the cast answers it several ways: Charlotte Lucas takes security, Lydia takes a thrill, Jane waits for affection, and Elizabeth turns down two offers before accepting one she respects (the workshop's reading). Each answer makes the others clearer. Truby reads *A Streetcar Named Desire* the same way, as a cast arguing one question about lies and love.

**Point.** Once the jobs are set, make the characters individual by giving each their own answer to one central question *(Truby's variations on a theme; built: if everyone faces one problem, every difference in how they meet it means something)*. Write the question in one line, then each main character's answer in one line. Two identical answers are two characters doing one job; a blank is a character with nothing to say to the story.

- **Give the opponent an answer at least as damaging as the hero's,** so the story does not simply punish the hero's side (Truby's lesson from Stanley in *Streetcar*, *fitted to one example*).
- **A second pair with the same kind of tie.** In *Great Expectations* Magwitch and Pip, and Miss Havisham and Estella, are two guardians shaping a child for their own ends, and each pair comments on the other *(Truby, fitted to one example)*.
- **The cast as a whole says something.** In *Vanity Fair*, subtitled a novel without a hero, the choice of cast is itself the book's view of people (Truby's example, *fitted*). Ask what your cast, taken together, says about people, and whether you mean it.
- **A hook needs a cast.** A high-concept premise alone gives a handful of scenes around the hook; a question every character answers gives scenes in every relationship *(Truby, fitted to Tootsie)*.

The owner theories frame this more widely. The Implied World theory puts the story's question into the world's main departure (thematic fit); this move puts it into the cast as well. The Gap theory treats theme as the story's answer to the question its world poses, and does not limit that question to conduct; Truby's central problem is always about how to treat others. Use his tool with the wider question when the story's question is not a moral one. Theme itself belongs to `plot`: `.claude/skills/plot/references/premise-and-theme.md`.

## 6. Types, gifts and shadows

**Case.** Gandalf and Saruman fill the same role, the wise teacher, and show its two faces: one passes on what he knows so that others can grow, the other bends it to rule them.

**Point.** Truby's *archetypes*, after Jung: roles found in every culture (he names nine, among them king, mentor, warrior, trickster, lover and rebel), each with a gift and a *shadow*, the trap the role tends to fall into *(asserted; the categories overlap and stretch to fit almost anyone)*. A type gives quick weight, because the audience recognises the pattern at once. It is also crude: without specifics it slides into a stereotype.

- In Implied World terms a type works like a baseline: the audience furnishes it from a hundred earlier examples, cheaply, and it feels generic because it is (the workshop's reading). Egri notes the same from the other side *(built)*: a stock figure can pass for a real person, because the audience fills it in from what it already knows. Decide whether that is a shortcut you want or a thinness you need to fix.
- For each typed character, write the gift, the shadow, and two details no other version of that type would have (the Specificity lever; the Recognise rung).
- The shadow is a ready-made real flaw, rooted in a strength rather than bolted on. For values and their dark faces, see `building-a-character.md`, section 7.
- Across the cast, pick types that contrast. Label a character only where a type really fits; forcing one does harm *(Truby)*.

## 7. The tie that keeps them in

**Case.** Nora and Helmer cannot simply walk away from each other: in their world a marriage is close to a cage, for her above all. The tie breaks only when something in one of them dies, here the part of Nora that always gave way (Egri's reading). Set beside them two strangers who scuffle in a crowd: their pride is hurt, but nothing stops them apologising and walking off.

**Point.** Egri's *unity of opposites* (his term), here the **tie**: something binding two characters so strongly that neither can leave or settle, so the conflict has to be fought out *(built: if either side can walk away or split the difference, the story can stop before its climax)*. Truby makes the same point from the plot's side *(built)*: people who dislike each other drift apart, so give them a believable reason to stay in one place, like the cramped shared flat in *Streetcar*.

- **Ask the cheap question.** "Why don't they just leave, or meet halfway?" If there is a cheap answer, the tie is too weak. Ask it as soon as the central pair exists, before outlining.
- **Everyday ties** *(Egri, fitted)*: a secret someone else knows; money owed or hoped for; dependence; a suspicion that has to be proved first. Hamlet stays near an uncle he loathes because he must prove the murder. Lear, having given everything away, depends on the daughters who mistreat him. Pick the tie that also touches the story's central question.
- **A stake for everyone** *(Egri, built)*. For each character write what they stand to lose and why they cannot simply leave. A blank in either column is a weak point. The first column is the Vulnerability lever; the second is the tie.
- **Motive makes the tie.** A killer who picks strangers at random has no tie to them, which is why Egri finds such crimes poor drama *(built for character-driven drama; stories that keep evil unexplained are another matter)*. Show the chain of need that makes the act necessary for this person against this victim.
- **Name what must die.** Which trait in one of them has to die, not always literally, for the stand-off to end? If none has to, the ending is a truce, not a resolution. Then write what the trait turns into, so the ending shows a new balance and not only a loss.
- **A tie can grow during the story.** Egri's sketch: a stray dog follows a man home day after day until, by sheer persistence, it is inside, and it has set him against his wife *(drawn from his own invented case)*. Persistence that costs something makes a tie where there was none.

In the owner theories' terms, the tie is the Anticipation theory's lost refuge applied to a relationship: no exit, so no safe move. Love is part of the tie (the Connection lever), which is why the break costs so much. Both links are the workshop's reading.

## 8. Care through the others

**Case.** Sam's devotion makes Frodo matter more, and makes us love Sam. Watson's admiring point of view is how readers learn to care about a Holmes who is low on warmth (both are the Bond theory's cases).

**Point.** Bond principle 5: care is social. We read characters by who loves them and by how they treat people who cannot help them, and characters' reactions cue the audience's. The cast is the writer's main tool for this, so use it on purpose:

- **Someone who vouches.** A hero low on warmth can borrow it from a character the audience already trusts. The balancing rule works across a pair as well as inside one person: Holmes runs on Competence and Specificity, and Watson supplies the Connection.
- **Someone who cannot help them.** Plan a scene with a character who can do nothing for the hero: a child, a servant, a stranger. Emma mocking Miss Bates at the picnic, and Mr Knightley's rebuke, make such a test, and it hurts to watch (Truby's reading of *Emma*).
- **Someone who objects.** Truby's *attack by ally*: a friend who wants what the hero wants objects to how he is going about it, and the hero defends himself *(fitted; the conscience job is built)*. How the hero's own side reacts tells the audience how to read him, without the narration ordering anything.
- **Someone who sees.** When the hero stays blind, give the insight to someone near him: the witness, `change-and-growth.md`, section 7.

Limits, from the theory:

- Love claimed and never shown does not count, and loved ones who exist only to be threatened are where Connection breaks.
- A cast that praises the hero is ordering care, and audiences resist it. Let the others react to what the hero does, not describe what he is.
- The bond is a promise. A character killed only to move another breaks it ("fridging"). Before cutting down a member of the inner circle, check that the death means something for *them*.

## 9. How many, and who to cut

**Case.** A Fantasy draft opens with a council of twelve, each named and each given a speech. Readers remember three. At the midpoint one of the twelve dies to shock the hero, and nobody feels it.

**Point.** The Bond theory: too many characters dilute care. Deep bonds are expensive, so ensembles need a small inner circle. Truby's cutting rule *(built on time cost)*: ask of each character whether they do something important for the story as a whole. One who only adds colour costs more time than they repay; fold them into someone with a job, or cut them.

A working sort (the workshop's reading, not the theory's rule):

| Ring | Who | What they need | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inner circle | the few the audience should miss | time, access to the mind, something to lose | would losing them reach the Miss rung? |
| Working cast | characters with scenes and a job | a job, and a desire of their own | what does the story lose if they go? |
| Passing figures | faces in a clause or at the back of the frame | business of their own, shown cheaply | do they take scenes, or only lines? |

The third ring is where Truby's rule meets the Implied World theory. The crew's grumbling about bonus shares at the start of *Alien* shows a world with business of its own; a passing figure can do the same in a line, cheaply, and make the world feel real (surplus, indifference). Truby's target is the character who uses up scenes and does no job. Cut the second; keep the first. See Rivals.

Truby's limit for leads *(asserted)*: a character counted as a lead needs at least the core of a story of their own: a desire, an opponent, a struggle and some change or insight. One who lacks them is a supporting character, whatever the draft calls them. A very large cast also trades depth for breadth, which leaves little room for any one character's self-revelation (Truby's example is *Tom Jones*; *fitted*).

## 10. Two leads, or many

Truby treats stories with two main characters, and with many heroes, as special cases of the web (his sections on two main characters and on multiple heroes). In the workshop's terms each shape causes one problem, and one or two moves answer it.

**Two leads split the desire line and the audience's alignment.** A story rides one spine at a time *(built)*: Truby's *desire line* (his term) is the one desire that runs through a story as that spine. So decide whose wanting drives it. That lead is the hero; in a love story the other is, structurally, the main opponent, with a need of their own from the start. Alignment can still be shared: give both leads time and access to their minds, but give the desire line to one. In a pair who travel together, keep one lasting friction between them, since it is the only opposition that goes the whole way with them *(Truby, fitted)*.

**Many leads cost set-up time and spread care.** Every extra lead takes time from the story's forward pull *(Truby, built)*, and the Bond theory names the price in care: deep bonds are expensive, so an ensemble needs a small inner circle. Two moves do most of the work. Let one or two characters rise above the rest as the story goes, so the inner circle forms in the audience's sight. And make each strand a different answer to the story's one question (section 5), so a cut between strands carries the question on instead of starting again. Truby's *multistrand* stories make the group the thing that changes; the Bond theory still asks who the audience would miss (see Rivals).

## 11. When the sidekick outshines the hero

A **sidekick** here is an ally who is with the hero most of the time. Pin the symptom first: which readers, in which scenes, compared with what. Then:

1. **Work the ladder on the hero.** A sidekick often runs levers that are cheap to show from outside (Humour, Specificity, Decency), while the hero's desire is vague, or we are never inside the hero's head, or the hero is so competent that there is nothing to fear for. Find the hero's lowest missing rung (`SKILL.md`, diagnosing).
2. **Check whether it is the design.** Some stories split the jobs on purpose: the figure the audience finds most fascinating is seen through a steadier teller, who holds the Interiority while the figure keeps the mystery (Holmes and Watson; Gatsby and Nick Carraway). If the draft is built that way, the teller being outshone may be the point. Then check that the teller still wants something and has something to lose, or the audience has no one to ride with.
3. **Truby's rule, run as a test.** Never let an ally be more interesting than the hero; if it happens, rebuild the story with the ally as hero *(asserted, from his advice to tell the story of your most interesting character)*. Before swapping, give the sidekick the point of view for one chapter or scene. If readers' pleasure rises, consider the swap. If the sidekick goes flat once we are inside, the appeal was the mystery of being seen from outside (the Interiority lever breaks when narrating every thought removes the mystery), and the fix belongs on the hero.

## 12. Rivals

- **Cut the colour, or keep the surplus.** Truby: cut characters who only add texture *(built on time cost)*. The Implied World theory: details that serve no purpose make a world, and a world feels real when it has business of its own. *Test:* in one version, cut a character who takes three scenes and does no job; in another, cut three passing figures who each take a line and have business of their own. The theory predicts the first cut tightens the story at no cost to the world and the second makes the world feel thinner; Truby's rule, read strictly, predicts both cuts help. They agree about characters who use up scenes.
- **A cast built round the hero, or a world with its own business.** Truby builds every character as a version of the hero's problem, and his four-corner opposition (`opponents.md`, section 10) by how each opponent attacks the hero. The Implied World theory: the world should not care about the hero, and its test is what the world would be doing next week if the hero died in chapter one. *Test:* ask that question of each main character. Where the answer is "nothing", give them a desire that predates the hero (Iago was passed over for promotion before he chose Othello's jealousy as his weapon) and see whether they gain weight. The theory predicts they do; Truby's method does not ask. His own rule that the opponents also fight each other goes part of the way.
- **The group, or the inner circle.** Truby's multistrand story makes the group the thing that changes; the Bond theory says deep bonds are expensive and ensembles need a small inner circle. *Test:* in an ensemble of eight equal strands, kill one lead at the midpoint; do the same in a version where three leads get most of the time and the access to their minds. The theory predicts the death lands only in the second version, and only for those three; Truby's account predicts the group's change carries the story in both. His claim is about meaning and the theory's about care: the group can carry the story's meaning while three people carry the care.
- **The most interesting character, or the one we are aligned with.** Truby: tell the story of the most interesting character. The Bond theory: we side with whoever we spend time with and understand, and interest is not care. *Test:* the point-of-view experiment in section 11. Truby predicts the swap helps; the theory predicts it helps only if the sidekick's appeal rests on levers that survive being seen from inside.

## 13. Traps

- **Building the hero alone.** The rest of the cast arrives as furniture.
- **Two characters doing one job.** Merge them, or give one a different answer to the central question.
- **Contrast in costume only.** Different looks, the same choices. Run the swap.
- **A tie with an exit.** A cheap answer to "why don't they just leave?" lets the conflict stop halfway.
- **Types left as types.** The audience fills a stock figure in from other stories; that is a shortcut only when chosen.
- **A cast that exists only for the hero.** Everyone a mirror, nobody with business of their own.
- **The council of twelve.** Care spread so thin that no death lands.
- **Praise as characterisation.** A cast that admires the hero orders care; let them react to what he does.
- **A death to move someone else.** The bond is a promise: the death has to mean something for the one who dies.
