# Opponents

*Sources: the Bond theory's principle 6 (indifference is the enemy; villains use the same levers; a villain fails by being a function rather than a person), principles 2 (alignment beats virtue) and 5 (care is social), the ten levers and where each breaks, and the limits "care can't be ordered" and "some acts end allegiance"; the Anticipation theory's suspense equation (the threat term, and odds that look bad but not hopeless) and principle 3 (show the rules, hide the thing); the Implied World theory's principle 4 (indifference); Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing (the antagonist, attack and counter-attack, opponents who cannot understand each other or be bought off, the institution's man, forces of nature); Truby, The Anatomy of Story (the character chapter: main opponent, necessary opponent, the opponent as double, values and moral argument, four-corner opposition; the moral-argument chapter: no straw man, balancing hero and opponent, good against bad; the plot chapter: the failed first plan, the hidden opponent, the fake-ally opponent, the opponent's plan, levels of desire); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 A person, not a function. 2 After the same prize. 3 Built from the hero's flaw. 4 Strong enough, and not too strong. 5 A double of the hero. 6 A case of his own. 7 Hidden layers. 8 The levers villains use. 9 When the threat is not a person. 10 More than one opponent. 11 The opponent who drives, and the one who learns. 12 Rivals. 13 Traps.

Words used in one sense only in this file, as in `SKILL.md`: an **opponent** is anyone the story sets against the hero; a **villain** is an opponent the story presents as doing harm. A villain works, in the theory's terms, when the audience cares what happens to him, often by badly wanting his downfall. The **main opponent** (Truby's term) is the one most set on stopping the hero. The **prize** is the thing hero and opponent are both after. The **threat** is the Anticipation theory's term: whatever could harm what the audience cares about. The hero's **flaw** is the Bond theory's lever; where Truby says *weakness*, this file says flaw, as `building-a-character.md` does. The **driver** and the **tie** are defined in `building-a-cast.md`. Provenance marks: *built*, *fitted* or *asserted*, as in `hard-to-vary`.

## 1. A person, not a function

**Case.** Two drafts of one Fantasy villain. In the first, the dark lord wants to rule the world because he is evil; he appears at the start and the end, and in between his soldiers turn up whenever the heroes need something to fight. In the second, he is the heroine's elder brother, passed over for the throne, sure the kingdom will starve under her gentle rule, and right about the harvest figures. Swap the first for a plague and nothing in the story changes. The second cannot be swapped for anything.

**Point.** Bond principle 6: indifference is the enemy, not dislike. Villains need care too: audiences cheered Joffrey's death in *Game of Thrones* because they badly wanted his downfall, which the theory calls care pointing the other way. A villain works when the audience badly wants something to happen to him. What makes one fail is being a function rather than a person.

- **The swap test** (`hard-to-vary`). Replace the opponent with a force: a storm, a locked door, a queue at an office. If the story still works, he was a function. If it breaks, name what broke; that is what holds him in place.
- **A motive, not "evil".** What ties an opponent to the person he attacks is a motive: a need pressing on this person against that one (Egri, *built*). Write the chain of need that makes the attack necessary for him. Truby calls the opponent who is simply bad by nature mechanical *(built on realism: people in real conflicts justify themselves)*.
- **Know what drives him even when he does not.** A shallow or empty character can still be written with full knowledge of the motive under the surface; let a moment in each scene leak it (Egri, *built*). That motive is the writer's reserve. How much of it reaches the page is the Interiority question in section 8.
- **He fights for himself.** Egri's failed opponent asks the woman both men want to send his rivals away, handing the fight to someone else *(built)*. An opponent who will not fight is no opponent.

## 2. After the same prize

**Case.** In *It's a Wonderful Life* George Bailey and Potter fight over one town: what it is for, and whose it is. Each can win it only if the other loses it (Truby's reading).

**Point.** Truby's main opponent is after the same prize as the hero, so the two collide head on, again and again, to the end *(built: a shared prize forces repeated direct contact, and an obstacle does not)*. A bare obstacle feels mechanical. The opponent need not be hated: he may be kinder than the hero, a lover or a friend. He is simply on the other side.

- **Go down a level.** If they seem to want different things, look for the prize underneath: the same person's loyalty, the same ground, the right to say what is true *(Truby; loose, because almost any pair can be redescribed as wanting one thing, so the real test is whether the shared prize forces them into contact scene after scene)*.
- **One prize, two valuations.** The fight is often over a place or thing each side values differently. The orchard in *The Cherry Orchard* is beauty and memory to the family and rentable land to Lopakhin (Truby, *fitted*). In *Streetcar* the ground they fight over is a person: Stella belongs to both of their worlds.
- **Nothing the hero can offer.** Egri's Krogstad wants more than his money: his name restored, and revenge on the man who sacked him. When Nora offers any sum, he refuses *(built)*. *Test:* what could the hero offer to end this? If anything would work, deepen the opponent's need.

## 3. Built from the hero's flaw

**Case.** Othello's weak spot is doubt about his wife, and the weak spot chooses Iago's weapon: suggestion, which a general used to open battle has no defence against (after Truby's reading).

**Point.** The job: make the main opponent the person in this world best placed to press on the hero's central flaw, and keep him pressing *(Truby's necessary opponent, his term; built: change needs pressure on exactly the point that must change)*. Lecter's first scene with Clarice shows it in small: she overreaches, and he answers by reading her origins, her ambition and the background she has tried to shed.

- **If the hero's flaw is missing** (a pitch often leaves it out), do not stall and do not guess silently. Propose one that fits what the draft shows, offer the opponent's design as conditional on it ("if her flaw is that she cannot let go, he should be the one who..."), and ask the writer whether that is the flaw. Sections 5 and 10 work from the same proposal.
- Name the hero's central flaw (`building-a-character.md`, section 4). Ask who in this world could hurt it most. If that is not your opponent, rebuild the opponent from the flaw outwards.
- Design his method as the opposite of the hero's strength, aimed at the flaw *(Truby, fitted from examples)*. If he fights the way the hero fights, ask whether that is really the most dangerous choice.
- Egri's version *(built)*: build the opponent's leverage out of what the target values most. Krogstad's leverage rests on Helmer's respectability, and Helmer would pay anything to keep his name.
- The theories' reason: an opponent aimed at the flaw puts the threat on exactly what the audience has been shown could be lost. What could be lost feeds the care term of the Anticipation theory's equation (the Worry rung, built by Vulnerability and stakes), and aiming the threat at it makes the care and threat terms large together.
- *Swap test:* replace him with an opponent who fights the way the hero does. If the story only gets louder, the flaw was what held the original in place.

## 4. Strong enough, and not too strong

**Case.** Egri: when a big man beats up a small one, we take the small one's side, but we do not wait anxiously for the result, because we already know it.

**Point.** The opponent must be a real danger to the hero, and the hero a real danger back *(Egri, built: a close fight keeps the outcome in doubt and makes both sides show everything they have)*. Truby's tennis picture *(built)*: a champion playing a weekend amateur is dull; against the world's number two, both have to play their best. A hero rises no higher than the opponent he faces.

The Anticipation theory sets the target more exactly: suspense climbs as the hero's chances fall, and collapses once hope is gone. So aim for odds that look bad but not hopeless, which usually means an opponent somewhat stronger than the hero until late (the workshop's reading; see Rivals).

- **Write the balance sheet.** What can each side do to the other, and what does each risk? If one side risks nothing or cannot hurt the other, raise it. Truby's first-plan rule is a sign the balance is right *(built)*: the hero's first plan should fail, against a strength of the opponent he did not allow for.
- **Too weak** gives the Superman problem from the other side: the Competence lever breaks when there is nothing to fear.
- **Too strong** turns the opponent into a spider and the hero into a fly *(Truby, built)*: a naive hero caught by an overwhelming schemer only suffers, and pure victimhood turns to pity, then fatigue (the Vulnerability lever). Give the hero enough power and awareness that his choices, and his wrongs, are his own.
- **Equals show each other.** In a close fight each side must use everything, so each shows its strategy, its fear and its reserves *(Egri, built)*. Check that each attack in a confrontation draws a counter-attack, and that the pressure makes each side show something it would rather hide.
- **He keeps coming.** An opponent must be ready to go to the end and, as the story goes on, to be as ruthless as the hero *(Egri, built)*. One who stands for an institution cannot soften, because the institution backs him; if he gives way halfway, the writer picked the wrong man for the job *(Egri, asserted)*. A climax fails if he softens only because the ending needs it (`change-and-growth.md`, section 8).
- **They must not simply understand each other** *(Egri, built)*. If Helmer saw that the forgery was done for his sake, there would be no play. What blocks understanding should be who they are (pride, principle, fear), not a missing fact one conversation could supply.
- **Keep them in the room.** A reason neither can walk away: the tie, `building-a-cast.md`, section 7.

## 5. A double of the hero

**Case.** In *Heat* the detective and the thief share a craft, a discipline and an empty or wrecked life at home, and differ over which side of the law the craft serves. Their one quiet meeting plays as two men recognising each other, which makes the final chase cost more (the workshop's example).

**Point.** Truby's opponent as *double* (his term): a real opponent has the depth and worth of the hero, and so mirrors him in some way *(built on comparison: a difference shows only against shared ground)*. Fill in his flaw, need and desire as for the hero (`building-a-character.md`, sections 4 and 5), with his desire ideally aimed at the prize, and give him the power to press the hero hard. The mirror needs the hero's flaw too; if it is not known, work from the flaw proposed in section 3 and say that the design depends on it.

A blank is a question, not a verdict. A missing flaw or need is a lever kept low (principle 4), which other levers must carry, as Lecter's Competence and Specificity do. A blank desire, or no power to hurt the hero, is the likelier sign of a function; the swap test in section 1 decides. Then list three things the two share: a trait, a skill, a past hurt, a loyalty *(Truby, built)*. Truby also wants opponents pushed as far apart as possible, which pulls against likeness. The workshop's reading joins the two: alike in situation, skill or past hurt; apart in values. The final clash is often where they look most alike, which makes the difference sharpest *(Truby, fitted)*.

## 6. A case of his own

**Case.** In *A Few Good Men* the colonel defends the order that killed a Marine by casting himself as the wall that keeps the barbarians out. In *Shadow of a Doubt* Uncle Charlie gives a chilling account of why the rich widows he kills are worthless (Truby's examples). Neither case is right; neither is easy to answer on the spot.

**Point.** Hero and opponent each act on a view of what a good life is, and the fight is worth most when both views have reasons behind them and each has a fault *(after Truby's values and moral argument, built: an opponent who never justifies himself rings false)*. In the workshop's words: his case should be one a fair reader could half accept, with one fault in it the story can expose.

- *Test:* write his defence as he would put it to a jury, and ask a reader whether any of it persuades them. If nothing does, strengthen it. If you cannot name its fault, the story's question is muddled.
- Write each side's idea of a good life in one sentence. If the two do not contradict each other, the conflict is only tactical.
- **Where he makes it** *(Truby, built)*: a scene where he defends his methods on moral grounds, and a clash of values with the hero, either at the final battle or early, so the audience knows what the fight is really about. Which view wins is shown by what happens, not by who argues better: `.claude/skills/plot/references/premise-and-theme.md`.
- **The price, in the theory's terms.** Hearing his reasons gives the audience access to his mind, and Interiority is morally neutral: it builds understanding, and can build allegiance, whether he deserves it or not. Decide how far you want that to go (section 8).
- **Not for every threat.** For a monster, the Anticipation theory's principle 3 applies: show the rules, hide the thing. Its nature and origin work better as wonders, and Horror franchises get less frightening as they explain their monsters. Give a human opponent a case; give a monster rules (see Rivals). For a human threat the workshop's present reading splits the two: his present case is shown, while his origin and full nature may stay hidden (`.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`, section 3). How far "hide the thing" reaches for human villains is a question put to the owner (Q3).
- **Plain good against plain bad.** Truby's simplest form keeps the hero good and the opponent bad from first scene to last, as Myth and Action often do *(fitted)*. It can work, but then the moral line holds no doubt, so all the suspense must come from threat and outcome, and the opponent still has to be a person or he fails as a function.

## 7. Hidden layers

**Case.** Othello does not see what Iago is after; Lear cannot tell which daughter loves him; Hamlet cannot trust a ghost's word about his uncle (Truby's cases). Each hero must first find out who and what the opposition is, and only then fight it.

**Point.** Truby ties opponent and mystery together *(built)*: opposition the hero cannot see must first be exposed and only then beaten, which doubles both the task and the win. His *iceberg opponent* (his term) names the shape, most of the opposition below the waterline; staging how it comes to light is `plot`'s work (below). His *fake-ally opponent* seems to be the hero's friend and works against him; often torn, he may half want the hero to win, and his unmasking is a ready-made turn.

- Write the opponent's plan as fully as the hero's: which attacks are hidden, and when each is sprung *(Truby, built: a hidden attack that lands becomes a reveal)*.
- Scheduling those reveals is the `plot` skill's work: `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`, sections 3 (what a reveal must do, and the opponent's plan) and 4 (the reveals in sequence).
- Whether the audience sees an attack coming before the hero does (suspense) or learns of it when it lands (surprise) is a choice the Anticipation theory makes scene by scene. Truby's default is to hide; that rival is recorded in `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`, section 11.

## 8. The levers villains use

**Case.** Hannibal Lecter has famously little screen time, and maximum Competence, Specificity and Humour, in the form of wit (the Bond theory's case).

**Point.** Villains use the same levers as heroes (Bond principle 6). Choose three or four to carry the opponent, and name the ones kept low, as for the hero (`SKILL.md`, building, step 3). One lever has to be settled first, because it sets which way the audience's care points.

**Interiority is the switch** (the workshop's reading of the theory). With little access to his mind, the audience watches the opponent from outside and can want something bad to happen to him: care pointing the other way, which is how a villain most often works (principle 6). Give him time in the audience's company and access to his thoughts, and alignment tends to produce allegiance, whatever he has done: readers root for Ripley to get away with murder, and audiences want the car Norman Bates is sinking in the swamp to go down. Some acts end allegiance, and where that line falls varies from reader to reader. Decide which way you want care to point, and ration Interiority to fit.

**The ladder, pointing the other way** (the workshop's reading of principle 6, owner question S6, where the audience's wish for Joffrey's downfall is, in the theory's words, care pointing the other way). For an opponent the audience is meant to oppose, Recognise and Understand are built as for anyone: a particular person, and a motive the audience can see. The upper rungs are turned. Side with turns round into wanting him stopped (this skill calls the turned rung *Side against*). Worry becomes fearing he will get what he wants, or get away with it; this lands hardest when what he wants would hurt someone the audience already cares about. Miss becomes wanting his end, and the payoff when it comes: the cheer at Joffrey's death. (A separate observation, the workshop's: a well-built opponent can also leave a hole when he goes, the loss of a force the story needed.) A villain readers call "just evil" is usually stuck below Understand. The turned rungs count only if they are built on him (his own want and reasons, shown), not borrowed from care for the hero: readers already want a flat villain stopped because he hurts the hero (the workshop's reading of principle 6). *Test:* take the hero out of one of his scenes; if readers no longer care what happens to him or what he gets, the rungs were borrowed. One who reaches the turned rungs on his own account is working.

**Understand without Side with.** The rung table in `SKILL.md` lists Interiority among the levers that build Understand, and for a hero it does. For an opponent meant to be opposed it is also the switch above, so ration it. The theory's ladder gives Understand two routes, access to the mind and a clear motive; lean on the second. Show his motive in scene rather than in his head: his case argued to someone's face (section 6), a reason the world gives him, a moment in each scene where the need leaks (section 1). Where you do give access to his mind, keep it short and tied to a decision (a look, one line of thought as he acts), not time in his private company. Let other characters' reactions to what he does point care against him (principle 5). If readers begin to wish him well, cut his time alone before you cut his reasons.

**Care is social for villains too** (principle 5, turned round). How he treats people who cannot help him tells the audience more than any description. A moment of casual cruelty to someone powerless points care against him faster than a speech about his crimes (the workshop's reading).

**Menace cannot be ordered.** Characters calling him the most dangerous man alive make the audience resist, as praise does for a hero; they believe what they see him do (the first limit of care, turned on a villain: the workshop's reading, owner question S2).

The other levers, for an opponent (the workshop's reading of the theory's table):

| Lever | For an opponent | Breaks when |
|---|---|---|
| Desire | a desire the audience can see, and fear | it is "evil" or "power" with no reason behind it |
| Competence | menace, and the pleasure of watching skill | he is so strong the hero has no chance, or so weak there is nothing to fear |
| Specificity | a particular person, not a type | quirks piled on as decoration |
| Humour | charm offsets cruelty and makes him good company | he jokes about everything, so nothing seems to matter enough to him to be dangerous |
| Vulnerability | something he could lose makes him a person, and hands the hero a lever | he is only wounded, and the audience pities him instead |
| Connection | someone who loves him (Stella and Stanley) vouches for him and raises what defeat costs | the love is claimed and never shown |
| Decency | real decency in an opponent (a code he keeps, fairness to his own, a kindness that costs him) pulls some allegiance towards him and makes his defeat cost more | it is put on for the audience's benefit, or it softens him until he stops being a threat |
| Agency | he acts, plans and adapts | he only waits for the hero to arrive |
| Flaw | the flaw that makes him a double (section 5) | it is cosmetic; or it is severe with nothing to balance it, when the audience is meant to side with him |

A kindness used as a weapon is the Decency lever's counterfeit, not the lever: the mother in *The Silver Cord* ruins her sons' lives with tenderness (Egri). It works through Specificity and Flaw, and it fails if the mask never slips, so the audience never sees what it hides.

## 9. When the threat is not a person

**Case.** In *Jaws* the shark is the threat, but the human conflict comes from people: the mayor who wants the beaches open for the summer trade, and the friction among the three men on the boat.

**Point.** The Bond theory's warning about functions is aimed at villains. A shark, a storm, a plague or a machine can carry the Anticipation theory's threat term without being a person: give it rules the audience can predict with, and keep its nature hidden (principle 3). Four moves keep such a story from going flat:

- **The driver is the one who refuses.** A force of nature cannot drive a story; the person who refuses to accept it can (Egri, *asserted*, and useful).
- **Put a person beside the force,** someone whose desire makes the threat worse or who stands for the system behind it. Or let the system itself be the opposition, as in Truby's pathos, where an ordinary hero fights a vast, uncaring order he cannot understand *(fitted)*. Then care rests wholly on the hero, and the Bond theory's limit applies: suffering without response numbs, so the hero must keep responding, by fighting back, adapting, or at least feeling it in a specific way.
- **Survival is a threat, not a desire.** Truby ranks bare survival as the weakest desire, because the only action it offers is running *(built)*. The Anticipation theory treats a threat to survival as a strong engine of fear. Both hold if the threat is to survival and the hero's desire aims higher: to protect someone, to prove something, to finish something.
- **The world as opposition.** In the Anticipation theory's reading, Horror is a world whose rules turn against the people living in it. Building that world is the `story-world` skill's job; the threat's rules and the fear ladder are in `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`.

## 10. More than one opponent

**Case.** In *Tootsie* several opponents press on the same flaw, the way Michael treats women, each from a different side (Truby's reading).

**Point.** Truby's *four-corner opposition* (his term) sets the hero against a main opponent and at least two more *(his claim that the pattern underlies every good story is fitted and overreaches)*. Framed by the theories, two moves carry most of its value:

- **A different pressure on the same flaw** *(built: variety without losing focus)*. Write one line per opponent on how he goes after the flaw. If two lines match, merge those opponents or change one method: two opponents who press the same way are one function in two coats.
- **Business of their own.** Let the opponents quarrel among themselves as well as with the hero *(Truby, built)*. The Implied World theory asks the same of any world, and an opponent with disputes of his own passes the next-week test (Rivals).
- **Pressure from the hero's own side.** The fake-ally opponent (section 7) and the ally who objects to the hero's methods (`building-a-cast.md`, section 8) add opposition from friends.

How tension rises over the story is a `plot` question: Truby wants a steady climb, the Anticipation theory a ratchet with partial releases, and that rival is recorded in `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`, section 11.

## 11. The opponent who drives, and the one who learns

- **Often the driver.** The opponent is often the one who starts the conflict and keeps pushing it (Iago, Krogstad): the driver, `building-a-cast.md`, section 3. A driver has usually decided before the story begins, so he changes least; his change shows as new tactics and a late sign of what drove him (`change-and-growth.md`, section 5).
- **Sometimes he learns.** Truby's *double reversal*: the opponent reaches a self-revelation too, taught by the hero as he teaches the hero *(built)*. It needs an opponent with a flaw and a need who is able to change; one who is simply evil cannot learn. *Test:* what could your opponent learn from the hero? If nothing, he cannot carry a double reversal; that limits the form, not the person (Lecter learns nothing and is no function). Detail: `change-and-growth.md`, section 7.
- **He loses to something real.** If he is beaten, let it be because his own flaw meets the hero's change, not because the writer's thumb is on the scale (the workshop's reading of the double).

## 12. Rivals

- **An even match, or bad odds.** Egri, and Truby's tennis picture: the opponent as strong as the hero. The Anticipation theory: suspense peaks when the odds look bad but not hopeless. *Test:* stage the final clash twice, once with the two level, once with the opponent clearly stronger and one narrow way through that the audience can see. The theory predicts more suspense in the second; an even-match rule predicts no gain. How low the low point may go before hope is gone is recorded in full in `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`, section 11; at the far end, an opponent with no way through at all, the theory and Truby's spider agree that suspense collapses into despair or pity.
- **A case for every opponent, or a monster left unexplained.** Truby: every opponent needs a strong, flawed moral case, and the purely evil one is mechanical. The Anticipation theory: a threat's nature and origin work better as wonders, and explaining a monster makes it less frightening. *Test:* in a Horror draft, add a scene in which the monster sets out its reasons. Truby's rule predicts more depth; the theory predicts less fear. For a human opponent in a drama between people they agree: the Bond theory asks for a person, and a person has reasons (his present reasons; what may stay hidden is in section 6).
- **Made to measure, or a life of his own.** Truby's necessary opponent, built to attack the hero's flaw, against the Implied World theory's world with business of its own (principle 4). The opponent's test: ask what he would be doing next week if the hero died in chapter one; if "nothing", give him a desire that predates the hero and see whether he gains weight (Iago was passed over for promotion before he chose Othello's jealousy as his weapon). General form: `.claude/skills/story-world/references/world-from-story.md`, section 11.

## 13. Traps

- **The obstacle with a face.** He turns up when the plot needs a fight and wants nothing of his own. Swap him for a storm and see.
- **"Because he's evil."** A label where a motive should be.
- **The straw man.** A case nobody could believe, so beating it is worth nothing.
- **Making him nicer.** Dislike is not the fault; indifference is. Give him a desire, a case and something to lose, not a staged kindness.
- **Menace announced.** Others call him terrifying; the audience believes only what it sees him do.
- **The accidental ally.** Long access to his mind, and the audience starts siding with him. Fine if planned.
- **No hope, or nothing to fear.** Too strong makes a victim of the hero; too weak makes a Superman of him.
- **The explained monster.** A backstory that turns a wonder into a file.
- **The opponent who softens for the ending.** He gives way because the story needs a finish, not because anything in him changed.
- **One opponent, one method, repeated.** The same attack again and again goes numb.
