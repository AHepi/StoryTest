# Building a character

*Sources: the Bond theory's ten levers, principles 3, 4 and 7, and the limits of care; the Gap theory, Part 2 section 2 (a character is a desire the world has shaped); the Implied World theory's reserve and iceberg ratio, applied here to a person; Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing (the chapters on character: the three dimensions, environment, contradiction, the substitution argument); Truby, The Anatomy of Story (the premise and seven-steps chapter: weakness and need, desire, the opening problem; the character chapter: the hero's requirements and values; the plot chapter: the ghost); McKee, Dialogue (chapter two: characterisation and true character); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 From the inside out. 2 Three dimensions: body, place, mind. 3 Know it all, show it through behaviour. 4 Flaw and need. 5 Desire. 6 Ghost, backstory and biography. 7 Values. 8 Shaped by conflict, shown by choice. 9 What each part feeds. 10 Rivals. 11 Traps.

Provenance marks, as in `hard-to-vary`: *built* (argued from a mechanism), *fitted* (generalised from past stories) or *asserted* (simply said). A fitted rule is a pattern to question, not a law.

## 1. From the inside out

**Case.** Two drafts of one hero, a hospital porter who steals medicine for his sister. In the first the writer lists traits: quiet, loyal, good with his hands, does crosswords. In the second the writer knows causes: he left school at fifteen when their mother fell ill, the hospital is the only place that has ever trusted him, and he believes rules are for people who can afford them. Ask either porter what he does when a nurse catches him. The second can answer; the first cannot.

**Point.** A list of traits describes a person; it does not tell the writer what they will do. Build from what causes behaviour: what shaped them (section 2), what is wrong inside them (section 4), what they are after in this story (section 5), what past event still drives them (section 6), what they think a good life is (section 7). Every part then faces the same test from `hard-to-vary`: does it change a choice at a named moment? The Bond theory sets the limit on all of this: a well-built person is not yet a person the audience cares about. Traits make the relationship easier or harder to build; time together, access to the mind, understanding the want and fearing the loss build it. This file builds the person and the want; the levers and the ladder in `SKILL.md` decide how the audience meets them.

## 2. Three dimensions: body, place, mind

Egri's *three dimensions* are the body, the person's place in society, and the mind, which he treats as what the first two produce between them *(built on the idea that behaviour has causes; loose as an explanation, because "the mind is their product" can absorb any behaviour after the fact, so use it as a working discipline, not a proof)*. His *bone structure* (his term) is the full checklist of what to know under each; go to the book for it. What matters here is how each dimension turns into behaviour.

- **Body.** A bodily fact acts through the stance the person has taken towards it: a man who has always been the smallest in the room and never lets anyone stand over him. A fact the character does not know (an illness nobody has found) still bends what they do, and gives the story something to reveal later. *Test:* name one bodily fact, the stance towards it, and one choice it drives.
- **Place in society.** Where they stand in money, family, work and belief sets what they can afford to want and what wanting it costs. Egri's strongest point here *(built from social cause and effect)*: large forces reach small lives whether or not the person takes part. A crash, a strike or a war comes home. This is the Gap theory's claim in practice: a character is a desire the world has shaped. The Bennet entail in *Pride and Prejudice* shapes every want in the book. *Test:* name one large force in the world that has nothing to do with the hero's goal and show how it reaches their household. The same move gives the world business of its own, as the Implied World theory asks.
- **Mind.** What Egri calls a *personal premise* (his term; since the workshop's word for Egri's premise is *claim*, `.claude/skills/plot/references/premise-and-theme.md`, section 1, this file says **personal claim**): the belief or aim that governs their life. The porter in section 1 has one: rules are for people who can afford them. *Test:* name the personal claim and one choice it makes at a named moment.

**The generic character.** Egri traces complaints that a character is stock, unbelievable or seen before to a missing dimension *(asserted, and fitted with hindsight to plays that lasted)*. The usable move: when a draft feels generic, do not change the events first. Find the thin dimension, deepen it, and rerun the key scene to see whether the character now does something less predictable. In Implied World terms a stock character is one running wholly on the audience's defaults for that kind of person, with nothing that departs from them (the workshop's reading, using the theory's *departure* of a person by analogy; the theory defines it for worlds).

**Two quick probes.** *Take one condition away* (Egri, *built by analogy*): remove one ordinary condition of a life, such as sleep, hearing, a limb or an income, and trace how the whole day changes. It is the ripple principle turned on a person, and it shakes a character harder than a pile of disasters. *Same pressure, different reasons:* when several people face one pressure, give each a different reason for the same act (Egri's gang of boys in *Dead End*); the differences reveal the people. For the cast as a whole, see `building-a-cast.md`.

## 3. Know it all, show it through behaviour

**Case.** Nora's first minutes in *A Doll's House*: she tips a porter lavishly while hoarding coins to pay a secret debt, and eats sweets her husband has forbidden and lies about it like a child. Her secrecy, her carelessness with money and her contempt for small rules arrive without a word of description (Egri's reading).

**Point.** The writer should know the whole profile; the page shows behaviour *(built: behaviour is the only channel the audience has)*. In Implied World terms the part of the profile never shown is reserve, and behaviour is the surface; how deep a person feels depends on how much the audience senses beneath (the iceberg ratio, applied to people). Egri's image for the proportion is a plant with far more root than stem.

- *Test both ways.* For every line that describes a trait, ask whether a behaviour could carry it instead. For every item in the profile that matters, point to the behaviour that shows it. An item that never touches behaviour is either reserve (keep it offstage) or dead weight (cut it).
- *Never order.* The Bond theory's first limit of care: telling the audience a character is brilliant or kind, or having others praise them, makes audiences resist. They believe what they see.
- *Keep a little that serves no plot.* Egri's own sample profile keeps details that serve no event. The Specificity lever sets the rule for them: a detail may serve no plot purpose, but it must reveal something about the person. One that reveals nothing is a quirk, and quirks piled up are where Specificity breaks.

## 4. Flaw and need

The Bond theory's **Flaw** lever: a real weakness or wrong belief, which makes a character human and makes change possible. It breaks two ways: cosmetic flaws ("clumsy", "cares too much") do not count, and severe flaws alienate unless other levers compensate.

Truby's *weakness and need* (his term; this file says *flaw* for the first half, as the theory does). The flaw is serious enough to be damaging the hero's life now; the **need** is the inner change that would let them live better, usually by overcoming it *(fitted)*. He splits both in two *(built)*:

- **Psychological:** the flaw hurts only the hero.
- **Moral:** the flaw hurts other people. Truby's test: at the start, can we see the hero harming someone? If not, the need is only psychological. Two ways to find a moral flaw: grow it out of the inner one ("because he is afraid of being left, he keeps his daughter from every friend she makes"), or push a strength too far, until a virtue oppresses the people near it.

**The price.** A hero shown harming someone at the start has Decency turned down. The balancing rule says turn another lever up in the same stretch: Competence, Humour, Interiority or Connection. Truby does not count this cost; the theory does (see Rivals).

**Blind to the need.** Truby: the hero must not see the need at the start, or the story would be over *(built, but stronger than it needs to be)*. The rule that holds is that the hero cannot yet meet the need. An addict can know exactly what is wrong and still be unable to act on it.

**A wrong belief breeds choices.** Egri's Orgon takes a fraud for a saint, and each later blunder follows from the first *(built from examples)*. A flaw written as a wrong belief that looks reasonable from inside generates the middle of a story. How that plays out over time is in `change-and-growth.md`, section 4.

**Judge them by their own standards.** When a choice seems implausible, ask whether it is implausible for you or for them (Egri, *asserted*). Rewrite the reasoning in their terms. This is Bond's alignment from the writer's side: the audience follows a character from inside that character's standards, not the author's.

**The opening problem.** Truby puts the hero in some trouble on page one, as the outward sign of the flaw *(built: a crisis defines a character fast)*. The Anticipation theory wants care built before the threat arrives. The two fit when the trouble is the hero's everyday mess, a symptom of the flaw, and not the story's main breach (see Rivals).

## 5. Desire

The Bond theory's **Desire** lever gives the audience a goal to share, and principle 3 says wanting drives attention. It breaks when there is no reason behind the want.

**Specific, and in this story.** Truby's desire is the goal the hero chases in this story, not their hopes for their life *(asserted, sensible)*. The captain in *Saving Private Ryan* longs to go home; the desire that drives the story is bringing Ryan back. His test *(built)*: is there a moment when the audience will know whether the hero got it? "Finding peace" fails; that is a need.

**One line, rising, settled late.** Once the desire has a verdict, give it the story: one main line, pressed harder as the story goes, decided near the end, since a goal won at the midpoint means a second story has to start *(after Truby's working rules; built: drive comes from one goal under rising pressure with a known point of decision)*.

**Need and desire do different jobs** *(Truby, built)*. The need is inside and hidden, and unlocks the change; the desire is outside and visible, and gives the audience something to ride along with. Write them on separate lines, then check that the desire pushes the hero into situations that expose the need. In Implied World terms the need is the part of the person below the surface and the desire is the tip (the workshop's reading).

**Necessity, not whim.** Egri: the hero must want one thing badly enough to stake a great deal on it, pushed by necessity from inside and out *(built: a whim can be dropped the moment it costs anything)*. Ask why they must act now and not next month, and name both the inner pressure and the outer one. The reason behind a desire often comes from the need, the ghost or the world; any of them answers the lever's break.

**Break an abstract goal into parts.** A goal like happiness, freedom or respect means something different for each person. Egri splits it into the concrete things this person needs *(asserted, as a technique)*. The story can then threaten one part at a time, which shows the audience exactly what could be lost: the care term of the suspense equation.

## 6. Ghost, backstory and biography

Three words kept apart in this skill:

- **Biography:** everything the writer knows about the character's past (the history in Egri's profile). It is reserve: mostly never shown.
- **Ghost** (Truby's term): the one past event that still hurts the hero now. It usually causes the flaw, and it pulls against the desire like an inner opponent *(built)*. Hamlet's father's murder and his mother's remarriage are a ghost; Hamlet's schooling is biography.
- **Backstory** (in McKee's narrow sense): the few buried past events the plot will bring to light at chosen turning points *(built, and fitted)*. When to reveal them belongs to the `plot` skill: `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`.

**Tests for a ghost.** Does it produce the flaw? Does it hold the hero back in the present? If neither, it is biography. Then swap it, as `hard-to-vary` does: exchange a dead brother for a dead father, or a fire for a flood. If no choice in the story changes, the ghost is loose. Find the choice it must drive, or cut it. The Bond theory's limit applies with full force here: the orphan and the dead spouse feel cheap when they serve the audience's tear ducts rather than the character's story.

**Some heroes start with none.** Truby's rarer opening puts the hero in a settled, happy world with no ghost, until something attacks it *(fitted)*. It suits the Anticipation theory well: the audience sees the normal, and what could be lost, before the threat.

**Timing.** Truby would keep the ghost out of the opening, let the audience sense something withheld, and explain it within the first third, often from another character's mouth; left to the end, the past takes over the story *(partly built, partly fitted)*. In Gap theory terms the ghost is an open question about the past. Once the story keeps pressing on it, it becomes a mystery, which the story has then promised to answer.

**Old experience colours judgement.** Egri: early experiences tint how a person reads events even when they try to act against them *(asserted)*. Give the character one such memory and a moment where they resist it and only half succeed. The same event can also mark a person very differently depending on when in their life it landed.

## 7. Values

**Case.** Truby's reading of *A Streetcar Named Desire*: Blanche's values cluster around beauty, manners and refinement; Stanley's around strength, power and appetite. Stella stands in both clusters, which makes her the ground they fight over.

**Point.** Each character acts on a set of beliefs about what a good life is *(Truby, built: values show only when they collide)*. Give each main character a cluster of values, not a single one, and find each value's good face and its dark face: loyal and clannish, thrifty and miserly, brave and reckless. The dark face predicts where they will stumble while defending what they believe, and it is a ready-made real flaw rather than a cosmetic one. Truby's archetypes work the same way, each with a gift and a shadow (see `building-a-cast.md`). The personal claim (section 2) is the same idea from Egri's side: each character's own claim about life, which should collide with at least one other character's.

*Test:* write each main character's idea of a good life in one sentence. If the hero's and the main opponent's do not contradict each other, the conflict between them is only tactical. For the opponent's side, see `opponents.md`.

## 8. Shaped by conflict, shown by choice

**The substitution test.** Egri's case against ranking plot above character *(built by swapping one part)*: put Hamlet in Romeo's place and he deliberates while Juliet marries someone else; give Nora a head for money and she never forges the signature, and there is no play. This is the `hard-to-vary` swap aimed at a person. Replace the hero with a different kind of person and sketch what happens. If the plot survives unchanged, the plot is not coming from the character. The same swap sorts genre: which kind of person turns this situation into farce, and which into tragedy?

**Action from the people.** At each turning point, ask what in this person made them do it *(Egri, built)*. Remove any coincidental or authorial cause and check whether the event still follows. If it does not, give the character the trait it needs, or change the event.

**Conflict shows people.** Egri's wider claim *(built)*: a character shows who they are only in conflict. One person's decision draws a counter-decision from the other side, that draws another, and the chain exposes each of them; nobody comes through a run of conflicts that disrupt their life unchanged. So design a character by asking what they decide under pressure, not only what they are like.

**Contradiction.** Egri borrows a scheme from philosophy in which a position meets its contradiction and the clash forces a new position *(asserted: it fits any change after the fact, so it predicts little)*. As a prompt it works. The part to keep: give each lead one real inner contradiction, two things they want or believe that cannot both hold. Egri's Helmer is feared at the bank and doting at home. For any reversal, write the explanation an attentive audience could piece together; if you cannot write it, you do not know the character well enough yet.

**Mask and core.** McKee separates *characterisation*, the whole surface of traits and behaviour, from *true character*, the moral and psychological core, which shows only in choices made under pressure, at a cost, in pursuit of a want *(built: looks and words cost nothing, risky choices reveal what someone values)*. His "only" is too strong for the theory, where Interiority builds understanding directly; the two meet if an inner decision counts as a choice, which McKee's own *mental action* allows (`change-and-growth.md`, section 1). The mask does three jobs in his account:

- It **intrigues.** The audience knows a surface is not the self, so an unusual mask raises a question about what is behind it, and the story answers with reveals. That is Bond principle 7 from the other side.
- It **convinces.** McKee's disbelief check is useful in diagnosis: if readers distrust a character because they sense she is lying, the story may intend it; if they distrust her because they do not believe she exists, the draft needs work.
- It **individualises**, above all through speech. Voice belongs to `.claude/skills/dialogue/references/voice.md`.

*Move:* in a key scene, raise what the character stands to win or lose and watch whether their choices start revealing more of them. If nothing more shows, the stake is not reaching them.

## 9. What each part feeds

The workshop's map from the parts above to the Bond levers and rungs. It is a reading for building and diagnosis, not part of the theory.

|---|---|---|---|
| Body | Specificity, Vulnerability, Competence | Recognise, Worry | the detail changes no choice |
| Place in society | Specificity, the reason behind Desire | Understand | the world never presses on them |
| Mind and personal claim | Interiority, Flaw | Understand | every thought is narrated |
| Psychological flaw | Flaw, Vulnerability | Worry | it is cosmetic |
| Moral flaw | Flaw; turns Decency down | Side with, at risk | nothing is turned up to balance it |
| Need | Flaw: the change it makes possible | a surprise that deepens care, if the change was seeded (principle 7) | it is announced, or met too early |
| Desire | Desire, Agency | Side with | it has no reason, or no verdict |
| Ghost | Vulnerability; the reason behind Desire and Flaw | Understand, Worry | it serves the tear ducts, not the story |
| Values and their dark faces | Decency, Flaw | Side with | they never collide with anyone's |
| Choices under pressure | Agency; principle 7 | Understand | the stake does not reach them |
| Connection to others | Connection; principle 5 | Worry, Miss | it is claimed and never shown |

## 10. Rivals

- **Care from desire and need, or from the whole relationship.** Truby: audiences identify with the hero's desire and moral problem, not with traits, and he credits the audience's care to flaw and need. The Bond theory agrees that care is not a verdict on traits, but builds it from time, access to the mind, understanding the want and fearing the loss, with traits as levers; Vulnerability and time together carry Worry and Miss. *Test:* two versions of a supporting character. A has a clear desire and a moral need but three short scenes and is never in danger; B has no stated need but many scenes, one scene of her own fear, and someone who loves her. Kill each at the midpoint. The theory predicts B is missed and A is not; Truby's account predicts A grips at least as well.
- **Likeness as the root, or one input.** McKee roots the audience's involvement in recognising a shared humanity in a character, then pouring its own wishes into them. The Bond theory makes alignment, not virtue, its central mechanism, and keeps likeness as one input: Aristotle's fear, because the sufferer is like us, feeds the Desire and Vulnerability that reach the Worry rung. So the two differ on whether likeness is the root of involvement or one input among several. *Test:* give a hired killer long early access to his thoughts and no appeal at all to shared traits; this measures allegiance without likeness (Ripley and Tony Soprano are the theory's cases). The theory predicts allegiance; a likeness-rooted account predicts little.
- **Harming someone on page one.** Truby: a hero with a moral need is doing harm when we meet him. The theory does not forbid this but prices it: it turns Decency down, and severe flaws alienate unless other levers compensate. *Test:* open on the hero's harmful act twice, once with nothing else turned up, once with Competence, Humour or Interiority raised in the same scene. The theory predicts readers leave the first and stay with the second; Truby's rule, taken alone, does not tell them apart.
- **Trouble at once, or care first.** Truby's page-one crisis against the theories' care built before the threat. The character's form: make the opening trouble a chronic part of the hero's normal, a symptom of the flaw (section 4), and not the story's breach; there the two agree. General form, with its test: `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`, section 11.

## 11. Traps

- **The profile on the page.** Knowing everything is the writer's job; showing everything removes the depth it was meant to give.
- **Traits as labels.** A trait that changes no choice at a named moment is loose. Swap it and see.
- **The tear-duct ghost.** A past event chosen for sympathy, not for the choices it drives.
- **A need dressed as a desire.** "To be happy", "to find himself": no verdict, nothing for the audience to ride.
- **Filling in every box.** Egri's profile and Truby's comparisons are prompts. Many entries will be free choices, loose and harmless; only the ones that change choices need testing.
- **Moral flaw with nothing to balance it.** The hero turns cruel on page one and every other lever stays low.
- **Explaining every act.** Egri's claim that everything can be traced is a writing discipline for the reserve, not a rule for the surface; the Interiority lever breaks when nothing is left to wonder about.
