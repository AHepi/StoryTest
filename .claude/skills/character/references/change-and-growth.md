# Change and growth

*Sources: the Bond theory's principle 7 (surprise deepens the bond), the Flaw lever (makes change possible), the limits "some acts end allegiance" and "the bond is a promise", and its Sherlock Holmes and Walter White cases; the Gap theory's definition of story (inner change counts), principle 7 (settlement) and question 5; the Anticipation theory's uncertainty term; Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing (growth, the seed, pole to pole, transition, static and jumping conflict, strength of will, point of attack); Truby, The Anatomy of Story (the seven-steps chapter: the self-revelation and the sketch of change; the character chapter: range of change, change of belief, double reversal, the hero who learns nothing); McKee, Dialogue (chapter two: mental action); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 What counts as change. 2 Start from the end. 3 The seed. 4 Steps between the poles. 5 Will, pressure and the right moment. 6 The self-revelation. 7 Two who learn, and the witness. 8 Surprise and "out of character". 9 When the hero does not change. 10 Rivals. 11 Traps.

Words used in one sense only in this file: **change** is a lasting difference in who the character is between the start and the end; **steps** are the stages between (Egri calls them *transition*, and calls the whole process *growth*); the **self-revelation** is the moment the character sees the truth about themselves (Truby's term). Provenance marks: *built*, *fitted* or *asserted*, as in `hard-to-vary`.

## 1. What counts as change

**Case.** Two endings for the same man. In one he wins the lottery and moves from a bedsit to a mansion. In the other he spends the story sure that asking for help is weakness, and at the end he asks his estranged son for a kidney. Only the second is a change in who he is; the first is a change in what he has.

**Point.** The Gap theory counts inner change as part of story, alongside outer events. Truby narrows what he calls character change to a revised belief *(built: a change of circumstance with no change of belief shows no inner movement)*: the hero's basic beliefs about himself and about how to live are tested, he revises them, and he proves it by acting differently towards someone. This skill calls that **a change of belief**, to keep it apart from the Gap theory's wider sense. The line between the two is Truby's to draw and is loose at the edges: getting sober can involve exactly the belief he means.

McKee's *mental action* sharpens the test for quiet stories *(built)*: a thought counts as action only when it changes an attitude, belief, expectation or understanding. Even if it never shows in behaviour, the character is a slightly different person afterwards.

*Test:* write the hero's key belief at the start and at the end, and name the act that proves the new one. If either belief or act is missing, what you have is a change of fortune. That may be what the story wants (section 9); it should be a choice, not an accident.

**What the theory says about change.** The Bond theory does not make change a condition of care. Its Flaw lever makes change *possible*; its principle 7 says an unexpected act that still makes sense for the character deepens care; and its limit "the bond is a promise" sets the condition on change: a beloved character can be changed, but it has to mean something for them. Nothing in its account of Holmes depends on his changing: Competence, Specificity and Watson's Connection carry him.

## 2. Start from the end

**Case.** Truby works *The Godfather* backwards. Michael starts detached from the family business and sure he stands apart from it; the act the story forces on him is revenge; he ends as its absolute ruler. The change is radical and believable because he started as the opposite of what the act demands.

**Point.** Plan the change from its end *(built: every step can then aim at the destination, and nothing is filler)*:

- **The self-revelation first.** Truby writes it before anything else, then the need as its mirror, then the desire that will force the hero into that lesson. Put the need and the self-revelation side by side: the second should read as the first, answered. His claim that no other route leads to an organic story overreaches; writers who discover the story as they go can rewrite backwards later.
- **Both poles.** Egri: know where each main character starts and where they will end (his *poles*), as states of mind, not events: "from trusting to disenchanted", "from aloof to committed" *(fitted; the two-pole summary fits almost any work, so use it as a check, not a proof)*. If the two ends are nearly the same, or unrelated, there is no change yet.
- **Flaw against the act.** Truby's sketch of change runs: the flaw, put through the struggle of the story's central act, gives the changed person *(built)*. The useful part is that the flaw must oppose what the act demands. A vengeful man who takes revenge only becomes more himself. Pick, as the central act, the one that most forces the hero to face the flaw.

The Gap theory gives the reason this works: meaning is settled at the end, and the audience reads forward in order to look back.

## 3. The seed

**Case.** Nora in *A Doll's House* looks loving, meek and dutiful. From her first scenes she also hides things, breaks small rules and holds a secret she once acted on alone. When she leaves at the end, the audience can find her leaving already in her (Egri's reading).

**Point.** Egri's *acorn and oak* (his image): a character carries from the start the seed of where they will end, and change that ignores their traits reads false *(built, and testable on any draft)*. Truby's *range of change* (his term) turns this into a planning step *(built)*: plan the end state as one of the selves the opening already allows. The wider the set of selves the opening allows, the more the ending can surprise, and the more it risks disbelief.

This is the writer's side of Bond principle 7: care deepens when a character does something unexpected that turns out to have been in them all along.

*Test:* take the final state and look for its seed in the first act. If it is missing, plant one small early behaviour that could grow into it, and do not announce it. Then check the other way: from the opening alone, could a reader guess the *kind* of ending? Egri wants yes for tragedy. If they can guess the event itself, the Anticipation theory says the story need not go slack: uncertainty moves from whether it happens to how it happens, as in *Chronicle of a Death Foretold*. Keep how, and at what cost, open.

## 4. Steps between the poles

**Case.** Egri strips the last scene of *A Doll's House* to its bare events: Nora thanks her husband, says she is leaving, he objects once, she goes. Now her leaving looks like a whim she might take back tomorrow. In the full scene she makes him sit and talk seriously for the first time, and he throws every argument he has at her in turn (duty, religion, what people will say, an offer to change), and she answers each until nothing is left and leaving is the only move open. Same events; with the steps restored, the audience believes it *(Egri's experiment is rigged in his favour, but the method is sound)*.

**Point.** Change is believed only when the audience can follow it *(built)*. Egri names two failures:

- **Static:** the character lingers on one step. They want nothing, cannot decide, only talk, or only grieve. Two people who end an exchange exactly where they began have made no step.
- **Jumping:** the character leaps from one state to a distant one with the steps between missing. A mild young man who robs a bank the moment he notices his suit is shabby has jumped; a string of blows, each worse than the last, might carry him there.

Moves for the steps:

- **Write the steps.** Name the states between the two poles and give each a scene or a beat. The particular steps are the writer's to choose; what holds is that a large reversal needs many of them, and that some happened before the story opens *(built)*.
- **Show enough, not all.** Egri himself softens the rule: suggest a step as long as the audience can see how the mind is working *(built)*. This is the Implied World's iceberg and the Gap theory's nested inference applied to a change: the audience rebuilds the steps it is not shown.
- **Keep a false step.** A generous act not yet sincere shows the change is under way and not finished (from Egri's steps from hatred to love).
- **Mistakes compound.** A first misjudgement, often from a good impulse, supplies the motive for the next (Lear dividing his kingdom; Orgon trusting a fraud) *(built)*. *Test,* which is `hard-to-vary`'s flip: reverse the first choice. If the story does not collapse, the first choice is not carrying it.
- **Even a flare-up has a process.** Someone who explodes at an insult has still taken it in, weighed the history and judged it *(built, though any chain can be invented afterwards)*. Write the chain for yourself; put one link on the page as a look, a pause, half a line.
- **Use up the alternatives.** Before a decisive act, let the opposition use every argument or tactic it has, each answered, so the audience believes no other way was left.
- **Do not announce each step.** A character who narrates their own change turns the steps into a lecture (Egri on Chekhov's *The Bear*). Let behaviour carry it.

Building the scenes that hold these steps belongs to the `plot` skill: `.claude/skills/plot/references/structure-and-conflict.md` and `.claude/skills/plot/references/scenes.md`.

## 5. Will, pressure and the right moment

**Strength of will.** Egri's term *(built)*: a character who cannot sustain a long conflict cannot carry a story. His test for a weak character is useful in diagnosis: one who cannot, for whatever reason, decide to act. A lead may start feeble and toughen, or start strong and be worn down; either way they need the staying power to endure. *Test:* at their lowest point, what keeps them fighting? If the answer is nothing, raise the stake or choose a tougher character.

**Suffering is not a step.** A character who weeps over a barren life and does nothing about it is static however fine the lines (Egri, *built*). The Bond theory's limit is wider: suffering without response numbs, and a response can be fighting back, adapting, or at least feeling it in a specific way; Vulnerability breaks into pity and then fatigue when there is none. Egri's demand for an act is stricter than the theory's (see Rivals). Where the story needs a step, give them an act, even a futile one; where it needs only care, a grief felt in a way only they would feel it can be enough.

**Refusal can be strength.** Egri counts stubborn refusal (Jeeter Lester in *Tobacco Road*, who will not leave his land) and soft pressure (a mother who smothers her sons with tenderness) as strength *(by redefinition, so loose)*. The Bond theory's Agency lever values acting over reacting. They meet on one condition, which Egri himself states elsewhere: the refusal is chosen, it costs the character something, and it forces the other side to respond. *Test:* name what they refuse to give up and what keeping it costs. No price, no strength.

**Pressure on their own scale.** Egri's miner in *Black Pit* does not act because the threat (his wife giving birth without a midwife) is routine in his world *(built)*. The Gap theory says the same: the world decides what counts as an event and what things cost. Measure each pressure against the character's world, not the reader's.

**The right moment.** If a character comes out passive, the story may have caught them before the decision was ripe (Egri's *point of attack*). His case is a mild bookkeeper who stabbed a man over a joke after decades of humiliation; at every earlier stage he would have been useless to a playwright. Start just before the link in the chain that forces a decision. His slogan that there are no weak characters, only ones caught at the wrong moment, is useful as an attitude and loose as a theory. Where to open the story is the `plot` skill's call.

**Conscious and unconscious will.** Hamlet pursues an aim he knows; Jeeter stays because nothing else occurs to him (Egri, *built*). Both can carry a story, but the pace should fit: a driving plot for a named aim, a slower pressure piece for a habit or a refusal. By the balancing rule, an unconscious will needs Interiority or another lever to make up for the Desire the audience cannot see.

**The one who drives changes least.** The character who starts and pushes the conflict, often the opponent, has usually decided before the story begins, so covers the shortest distance (Egri, *fitted*). Their job is to force others to change; their own change shows in new tactics and a late sign of what drove them. See `building-a-cast.md` and `opponents.md`.

## 6. The self-revelation

**Case.** At the end of *Tootsie*, Michael sees that he has used women all his life, and he apologises to the woman he deceived: a revelation proved by an act (Truby's reading).

**Point.** The self-revelation must answer the need: set the two side by side, and the one should read as the other, met (section 2) *(Truby, built)*. Truby splits it into psychological and moral kinds, matching his split of the flaw (`building-a-character.md`, section 4); a moral one is proved by a new act towards someone.

What it needs underneath, in the workshop's reading: something the hero has kept from himself, which the audience could see first. Stretching a Gap theory term, that self-deception is a perspective gap inside the character: the kind Stevens, as teller, carries through *The Remains of the Day*.

Two checks from the Bond side:

- **Proved, not announced.** A hero who states the lesson sounds like a sermon *(Truby, asserted; he allows a line witty enough to escape preaching)*. The Bond theory gives the reason: care cannot be ordered, and audiences believe what they see. If the change stays inside the head, add the act.
- **Still them.** A revelation that turns the hero into someone nothing in the story prepared breaks the bond (principle 7: it breaks when they contradict who they have been without the story earning it). Check it against the seed (section 3).

## 7. Two who learn, and the witness

**Double reversal** (Truby's term): the hero and the main opponent both reach a self-revelation, each taught by the other *(built on comparison)*. The story's view can then differ from either character's, and the audience is held a little less tightly to the hero, so it sees more. It is common in love stories, where the beloved is structurally the opponent: Darcy's pride, and his effort to master it, make Elizabeth see the same faults in herself. It needs an opponent able to learn. That is Truby's requirement for this form, not the Bond theory's: an opponent who learns nothing, like Lecter, can still be a person. *Test:* write the opponent's revelation, and check that the hero causes it and the opponent causes the hero's. More on the opponent's side: `opponents.md`.

**The witness.** When the hero learns nothing, give the insight to someone close enough to see what he cannot. At the end of *The Godfather*, as a door is shut in her face, Kay understands who her husband now is *(Truby calls it an advanced move; drawn from one example)*. Bond principle 5 is why it works: we judge him partly through the eyes of someone who loved him. In Gap theory terms the knowledge gap settles in the audience's favour while the hero stays blind.

## 8. Surprise and "out of character"

The Bond theory, principle 7: care grows when a character does something unexpected that still makes sense for them, and breaks when they do something that contradicts who they have been without the story earning it. "Out of character" is the audience's name for the second: really, their bond was broken.

**Diagnosing the complaint.** Pin it first: which act, which readers, compared with what earlier behaviour. Then work down the list:

1. **Was the seed there?** Look for the trait in the first act (section 3). If it is absent, plant it or cut the act.
2. **A change in who they are, or one surprising act?** A change needs steps shown or suggested; if it happens between two neighbouring lines, it jumped (section 4). A single act needs a seed the audience can find again (section 3), and steps are optional (Rivals, "Sudden turns"). If the seed is there, check whether readers could see it before looking for missing steps.
3. **Was the pressure big enough, on their own scale, at a moment when the decision was ripe?** (Section 5.)
4. **Is it disbelief, or lost allegiance?** Some acts end allegiance for most audiences, and time and interiority stretch that line a long way but not forever; many viewers can name the moment they gave up on Walter White. In the workshop's reading that is not "out of character": the act fits him, and it ended their siding with him. Check which levers were still holding them, and whether losing them was the plan.
5. **Intended distrust, or failed belief?** McKee's check: readers who sense a character is lying may be reading her rightly; readers who do not believe she exists are telling you about a craft failure. Fix only the second.
6. **Did it mean something for them?** The bond is a promise. A beloved character can be hurt, changed or killed, but it has to mean something for *them*, not only for someone else's change. Killing a character only to motivate another is what Gail Simone named "fridging".
7. **Did someone soften to fit the ending?** Egri: a climax fails if any character gives way for no reason in themselves, only because the ending needs it *(built)*. Keep them firm, or earn the change.

**Fix** by planting the seed or making it visible, adding a step where a change of who they are needs one, raising the pressure or moving the moment, or cut the act. Do not explain it afterwards in dialogue: an explanation tacked on is telling, and audiences believe what they see, not what they are told.

## 9. When the hero does not change

The Bond theory never makes change a condition of care, and the Gap theory asks only what has changed from the opening normal and what it cost, which may be the world or the audience's understanding rather than the hero. So a hero, or another lead, who stays the same can be a choice. Common shapes:

- **The steady figure.** A central figure holds while the hero, the one we follow, changes around them: Scout around Atticus Finch in *To Kill a Mockingbird*; the Banks family, Mr Banks above all, around Mary Poppins. Holmes changes little across dozens of stories while Watson tells them. Care in Holmes comes from Competence, Specificity and a companion who supplies Connection, and for a series the Miss rung, built by time and shared history, is what brings the audience back. In the workshop's reading, a change to what the audience returns for is a risk to weigh; the theory itself does not forbid it (see Rivals).
- **The driver who decided before the story began.** Short distance, new tactics, a late sign of motive (section 5).
- **The refusal or the fall.** Michael Corleone rises in power, falls morally and learns nothing; the witness sees it (section 7). In *Chinatown* the revelation is dark: the detective learns that his meddling only does harm. Truby's rule is that a story shows a change or why it never came; the "or why not" lets the rule fit every ending, but its useful demand is real: show the non-change as a meaningful failure, not an oversight. The Bond theory's limit applies: a fall can take allegiance with it.
- **The audience learns instead.** In much classic Horror the hero ought to learn and does not, and the audience carries the lesson out (see `.claude/skills/genre/references/genres/horror.md`).

*Test* (Gap theory, question 5): by the end, what has changed from the opening normal, and what did it cost? If the answer is "nothing, for anyone", the story has no shape yet. If the hero holds while others or the world move, that is a shape.

## 10. Rivals

- **Must the lead change?** Egri: a character who ends where they started is badly drawn, and the work is poor *(asserted; he reads hidden change into apparent stillness, so the rule cannot lose)*. Truby: every story's final aim is to show a character change, or why it never came, and drama celebrates people growing better. The Bond theory neither requires change nor penalises it: care rests on the relationship, the Miss rung on time, shared history and relationships, and a beloved character can be changed if it means something for them. The Gap theory lets the settlement change the world or the audience instead. *Test:* three versions of a series detective's story: in one he does not change; in one he makes a change of belief whose seed was planted early; in one he makes a change with no seed. Egri predicts both changed versions beat the unchanged one and cannot tell them apart. The theory predicts care holds in the unchanged version if the levers and the time are there, deepens with the seeded change and breaks with the unseeded one.
- **Sudden turns.** Egri bans sudden unexpected acts in drama and argues that the premise leaves only one road. The Bond theory welcomes surprise that turns out to have been in the character all along, which can be sudden on the surface if it was prepared underneath; the Anticipation theory needs the outcome kept uncertain. *Test:* a late act whose seed is planted early, with no steps shown between. The theory predicts care deepens and re-readers find the seed; Egri predicts disbelief. Both predict disbelief if the seed is missing, so the seed, not the steps, is what divides them.
- **Act, or feel it specifically.** Egri: suffering that draws no act is static. The Bond theory: the audience needs the character to fight back, adapt, or at least feel it in a specific way. *Test:* a widow who only grieves, in two versions: in one she grieves in general terms; in the other in a way only she would (she keeps his coat on its hook and talks to it about the bills). The theory predicts care holds in the second and numbs in the first; Egri's rule predicts both are static until she acts.
- **Unwanted sympathy.** Egri says audiences pitied the *Black Pit* informer, whom the author meant them to despise, because his inner contradiction was blurred; sharpen it and the pity goes. The Bond theory says alignment beats virtue: time with a vulnerable man we understand produces sympathy almost whatever he has done. *Test:* sharpen the informer's contradiction but keep his screen time and access to his thoughts; then, separately, cut his time and interiority and leave the contradiction blurred. The theory predicts the pity survives the first change and drops with the second; Egri predicts the reverse.
- **Every motive shown, or some left open.** Egri: on stage the audience must see the reasons for a decisive act, or it reads as whim; Nora explains herself before she goes. The Gap theory splits open questions into mysteries, which the story has pressed on and promises to answer, and wonders, which it never made anyone's goal and promises to leave open; the Implied World values productive gaps. *Test:* two decisive acts, one whose motive the story has kept asking about, one it mentioned in passing and never pressed. The theory predicts the first must be answered and that answering the second deflates it; Egri predicts that answering both helps. The media differ too: prose can carry a motive through interiority without speech.

## 11. Traps

- **The light switch.** Truby's name for change flipped on in the last scene with nothing before it. Look for the scenes where it is being prepared.
- **Fortune taken for change.** Rich, crowned, sober or married is a new state; ask which belief moved.
- **The announced lesson.** The hero tells us what they learned; the audience stops believing it.
- **Fixing a passive lead with traits.** The problem may be the moment, not the person: move the start, raise the pressure.
- **Steps so plain the character narrates them.** The change reads as a lecture.
- **Change for everyone.** The rule that every lead must change is fitted, drawn from past stories. A steady figure, a driver or a refusal can be the right shape.
- **Change bought with someone else's death.** A character killed only to move the hero breaks the promise the bond made with that character.
- **Explaining the surprise away.** A turn that was in them all along needs a seed, not a speech.
