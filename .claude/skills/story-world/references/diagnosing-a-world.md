# Diagnosing a world

*Sources: the Implied World theory throughout (the six key terms, the eight principles, the expansion paradox, the guide for worlds with many departures, fans gathering at the gaps), with the Gap theory (the departure gap; principles 2 Breach, 3 Pressure and 5 Promise, mystery and wonder), the Anticipation theory (principle 4 Lost refuge; principle 3 Show the rules, hide the thing; Horror as a world whose rules turn against its people) and the Bond theory (the world shouldn't care about the hero, but the audience must); Truby, The Anatomy of Story (settings used by formula, a staged approach to a strange world, the outsider who learns the rules by mistake, the title that names a system, minor characters with their own journeys) and The Anatomy of Genres (rules with a cost, vague rules, world against narrative drive, the recognisable future, one-trait peoples, the cartoon dystopia, texture); Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing (moving a conflict to another setting, closing the exits, pressure on the world's own scale, big forces in small lives, exposing the person); McKee, Dialogue (exposition as a cover for the author's need); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 Pin the symptom first. 2 From symptom to principle. 3 Spending: generic, overspent, hard going. 4 Consequences: costume, a rule that breaks the world, arbitrary stakes. 5 A life of its own: set-dressing, and consistent but not real. 6 Showing: the info-dump, shallowness, vagueness. 7 Growing: smaller as it gets bigger. 8 Meaning: a world beside its theme. 9 A world that should frighten. 10 Telling two diagnoses apart. 11 Rivals. 12 Traps.

## 1. Pin the symptom first

**Case.** A writer says readers find her Fantasy world "thin". Asked who: two of five test readers, both people who never read Fantasy. Asked where: from chapter three, the council scene. Asked compared with what: one names *A Wizard of Earthsea* as a world she loved. Now there is something to explain. The council scene names eleven noble houses and three religions in eight pages, and readers who bring no Fantasy baseline have nothing to hang them on. "Thin" turned out to mean "I couldn't hold it", which is overspending, not too little world.

**Point.**
- **Write three things before choosing a principle:** to whom, where, compared with what. Readers name a feeling, not a cause, and "thin", "flat" and "too much" can all name the same fault.
- **Sort readers by what they bring.** Genre regulars carry more baseline than readers new to the genre, so the same page can feel generic to one and overwhelming to the other. A symptom only one group has may be what the genre asks of its readers, not a fault. To decide which, write down whom the book is for before reading the notes. If readers new to the genre are part of that audience, their symptom is a fault. If not, check the genre regulars on the same passage (can they recall the eleven houses after the council scene?); if they can't either, it is a fault for everyone, even though only one group complained.
- **If the writer can't be asked,** say in one line what you assume, treat the draft's own elements as the symptom, and turn each missing fact into a check the writer can run (`SKILL.md`, Diagnosing step 1).
- **Ask what readers wondered about.** The theory predicts that fans gather at the gaps: speculation clusters where the story is silent. If nobody wonders anything, there are no productive gaps. If readers demand answers to what you meant as wonders, your emphasis promised mysteries (Gap theory, principle 5).

## 3. Spending: generic, overspent, hard going

**Case.** The theory's two extremes: a Fantasy that is medieval Europe with dragons spends almost nothing and feels generic because it is; a story with forty invented terms on page one has overspent. *A Game of Thrones* sits between them. It opens on castles, lords, inheritance and bastardy, a baseline its readers own from real history, and spends on a few departures that change what things cost: seasons that last years, a wall at the edge of the world, dragons believed extinct.

**Point.**
- **Generic means nothing spent.** Confirm with a swap: move the story to another world of its genre and change the names. If no scene breaks, the setting is not doing its job *(the Gap theory, principle 2: a breach with no world could happen anywhere; Egri builds the same test from one play)*. The repair is rarely more invention. It is a few departures that matter, led by one that changes what people want or what things cost, each rippled (the theory's "spend on a few departures that matter", Part 2 section 1). Stock settings and weather used by formula are the same fault in a single scene, such as a mountaintop for every insight *(Truby, asserted)*: twist the pairing or make the story need it.
- **Overspent means the cost in attention outran what the story pays back.** Confirm by counting the departures a reader must learn before the first major turn. Then, in order: *cut* any that does no job (a sword can just be a sword); *delay* the rest, each arriving when someone runs into it, so it is learned while the plot is moving *(Truby, Genres, built; `world-from-story.md`, section 10)*; *stage* the strangeness with a half-familiar place before the strange one *(Truby, fitted from the Harry Potter books; `world-materials.md`, section 8)*; *anchor* it: let the reader meet the familiar parts of a future or a Fantasy world first and its new rules after, so they feel inside the world rather than studying it from outside *(Truby, Genres, built)*.
- **Hard going means many departures and no guide,** as the theory predicts. Confirm: is there an outsider who learns alongside the audience, or an outside aid? The outsider need not be the hero. *The Godfather* opens on a minor character who doesn't know how the Don's world works, and the audience learns a rule through his mistake *(Truby, built)*. A title that names a vast system as one thing with hidden strands (*The Matrix*, *Network*) works as an outside aid: it tells the audience it need not grasp everything yet *(Truby, built; his "almost required" overstates it)*. A crossing between worlds can mark where the baseline stops applying (`world-materials.md`, section 8).

## 4. Consequences: costume, a rule that breaks the world, arbitrary stakes

**Case.** The theory's *Dune*: one departure, a shortage of water, followed everywhere, so the world reads as a system. Now an invented costume version: a desert planet where water is said to be precious, while the characters wash, weep and leave cups half full. The departure is announced and never followed. The opposite fault is a consequence nobody planned. The theory's Time-Turner implied that any disaster could be undone. Readers of *The Lord of the Rings* have long asked why the great eagles, who rescue characters more than once, don't simply carry the Ring to the mountain. The story never closes that exit, and the question has outlived its author.

**Point.**
- **Costume is a departure with no consequences.** Confirm by removing it: if nothing else in daily life would change, it is costume. Repair: write what it changes about work, money, food, love, law, death and speech, and let two or three of those reach the surface without comment. Peoples defined by one trait (a nation of stoics, a species of merchants) are costume at the scale of a culture. Give each two or three social rules of its own, about kinship, honour, property or death, and a scene that tests them *(Truby, Genres, built)*.
- **A rule that breaks the world is a consequence you didn't plan.** Poke every rule: what does it make possible that would dissolve the story's problem? Close each exit with a cost the story shows, a limit stated early, or a motive inside the story. In *A Doll's House* the law that a wife may not borrow alone is the rule that closes Nora's easy way out *(Egri, built)*. A limit invented at the moment of need is a patch, and readers see it.
- **A rule without a cost makes no danger.** Write what happens to someone who breaks or misuses each rule; if the answer is "nothing much", raise the cost or drop the rule *(Truby, Genres, asserted; the Anticipation theory supplies the mechanism: audiences predict danger from a threat's rules)*. For any power that cheats death, list its costs, including one that falls on other people *(Truby, Genres, built)*.
- **Arbitrary stakes mean a breach with no world:** it could happen anywhere, because nothing sets what things cost (Gap theory, Part 2 section 2). Confirm: what does this world treat as rare and costly, and is the pressure measured on that scale? Egri's case: a threat that is routine in the character's own world, a birth without a midwife where most women survived one, moves nobody *(built)*. Repair: raise the pressure to what this world counts as serious, or change the world so that it counts. In *Pride and Prejudice* one rule of inheritance sets what every marriage costs.

## 5. A life of its own: set-dressing, and consistent but not real

**Case.** *Alien* opens on the crew grumbling about bonus shares before anything alien appears, and the Company's own agenda turns out to be the plot (the theory's case). Against it, an invented set-dressing world: a village that exists to be burned in chapter one, an inn that exists to hand out a quest, a king who appears only to give an order.

**Point.**
- **Set-dressing is a world with no business of its own.** Confirm with the theory's test: if the protagonist died in chapter one, what would this world be doing next week? If the honest answer is "nothing", repair it three ways. Name one large force (a war, a crash, a drought, a new machine) that has nothing to do with the hero's goal and show it reaching his household anyway *(Egri, built: nobody's security can exceed the world's)*. Give minor characters wants and troubles of their own, ideally each a small version of the world's main pressure *(Truby, built, from the Dublin of* Ulysses*)*. Let institutions carry on in the background: a sport with full rules, a calendar that turns whatever the hero does. Places built from the hero can keep business of their own: `world-from-story.md`, section 9; materials for it: `world-materials.md`, section 4.
- **An oppressive world is set-dressing when it is only oppression.** List the real freedoms and pleasures that keep its citizens loyal. If the list is empty, the society is a cartoon and cannot comment on the real one *(Truby, Genres, built)*.
- **Consistent but not real means no surplus.** Every detail serves the plot, so the world reads as a machine for the plot. The theory's case is Barthes's barometer in a Flaubert story: its uselessness is its job. Repair: add details nobody needs, taken for granted by people who have lived with them all their lives, in a clause or in the background of the frame. A trade's idiom or a slang used without comment does the same *(Egri, asserted)*; putting it into lines is `dialogue`'s work.
- **Test surplus with care.** A surplus detail's specifics are free: swap the barometer for a clock and nothing is lost. What is held is that it is unremarkable *and belongs to this world*. Swap it for something this world could not contain and the world breaks.

## 6. Showing: the info-dump, shallowness, vagueness

**Case.** Tolkien shows songs about stories we never hear, never explains Tom Bombadil, and loses the Entwives offstage. The appendices are the reserve; the novel only lets you feel their weight (the theory's case). Against it stand the two failures M. John Harrison mocked: the encyclopedia chapter, and two characters explaining to each other what both already know. The theory says he is right about showing and wrong about building.

**Point.**
- **The info-dump is the reserve dumped on the surface.** Confirm with two questions. Is the story paused while the world is explained? Is anyone being told what they already know? The second rings false because the characters' visible activity is a cover for the author's real one, reciting background for the audience *(McKee, built)*. Repairs, cheapest first: cut to the tip and trust implication; turn the fact into a consequence someone suffers; give it to someone with a real reason to ask, an interrogation or an outsider's mistake *(Truby, built)*; put it in a clash where it matters to someone *(Egri, fitted)*. In prose a history can be told directly, a few sentences at the moment the story needs it, but not as an encyclopedia chapter (McKee would allow more: `prose-and-film.md`, sections 6 and 11); in drama it must pass unnoticed.
- **Shallowness is nothing beneath the surface, or everything shown.** Depth is what the audience senses beneath the surface, not how much is shown (principle 6). Repair: build a reserve (a history, a geography, what the institutions do), then let glimpses through: a place name nobody explains, a ruin, an old man who fought in a war the story never shows. Real people from the place and time, or characters carried over from the author's earlier work, bring histories into a story even when none is shown *(Truby, built, from* Ulysses*)*.
- **Vagueness is a gap without edges, or a rule never shown.** "Something happened long ago" is vagueness. "The war that emptied the northern cities" is a gap: the audience knows what kind of thing is missing. Confirm: can a reader say what kind of thing is missing, and what the world allows and forbids? If they can't predict anything, the stakes feel arbitrary; Truby ranks vague rules as Fantasy's commonest large failure *(Genres, asserted)*. Repair: show early the rules that bear on someone, and leave their origins open. The Anticipation theory draws this line for a threat, and the workshop applies it to any rule: the rule works like a mystery, stated, tested and kept to; the nature and origin of the thing work better as a wonder.

## 7. Growing: smaller as it gets bigger

**Case.** The theory's case: the midi-chlorians of *The Phantom Menace* turned the Force from a mystery into a blood test. The Anticipation theory notes the same in Horror: franchises grow less frightening as they explain their monsters' pasts.

**Point.** Sequels, prequels, companion books and adaptations grow by turning reserve and gaps into official canon, so a world can feel smaller as it gets bigger (the expansion paradox). Confirm: list what the new material answers, and mark which of those were wonders, mentioned in passing and never pressed. Each answered wonder is a deflation, which the Gap theory counts as a broken promise. Repairs:
- **Expand sideways:** new places, new people, new business, instead of down into the old wonders.
- **Open a gap for each one filled,** with edges, so fans have somewhere new to gather.
- **Explain behaviour, not nature.** What the Force does and what it costs can be canon; what it *is* can stay open.

The opposite fault is the Gap theory's betrayal: questions pressed hard and never answered (its *Lost* case). Which one you have depends on emphasis, and emphasis is scheduled in `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`.

## 8. Meaning: a world beside its theme

**Case.** *The Handmaid's Tale* builds a state around controlling fertility because the book is about who owns a body (the theory's case). An invented contrast: a novel about grief, set in a floating city whose main departure, buildings that defy gravity, is lovingly worked out and has no bearing on loss. Readers praise the city and forget the book.

**Point.** When world and theme sit side by side, the main departure is not carrying the story's question (principle 8). Confirm: write the question in one line, then swap the main departure for a near neighbour. If the question doesn't change, the departure isn't carrying it. Repair one of two ways: change the departure until it asks the question (a city where the dead stay present for a year, and then must be let go), or change the question to the one the world already asks. Places can carry the question too, as the two versions of Bedford Falls in *It's a Wonderful Life* ask what one life is worth *(Truby, fitted)*; growing places from the question is `world-from-story.md`, sections 2 and 4. The fit is to the story's question, whatever kind it is. Truby's version of theme is always moral; the theory's is wider (whether hope can exist without a future), and the workshop follows the theory.

## 9. A world that should frighten

**Case.** In *A Quiet Place* sound kills, so a child's toy that beeps and a birth become dangers. The world's own rule has turned on its people, and a necessity they cannot give up is now the threat (the Anticipation theory's case).

**Point.** In the Anticipation theory's reading, Horror is a world whose rules turn against the people living in it. If a world meant to frighten doesn't, confirm two things: which rule of this world turns on its people, and whether anywhere is still safe. Repairs: make an everyday necessity dangerous (sleep, sound, looking); take away a refuge (the home, the bed, broad daylight); strip away help (the phone line, the police). Show the rules and hide the thing: give the audience a rule to predict from, and keep the thing's nature a wonder. The scene-by-scene work of fear is `plot`'s (`.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`); Horror's default world is in `.claude/skills/genre/references/genres/horror.md`.

## 10. Telling two diagnoses apart

**Case.** "Readers are lost in the first act." Three causes fit: too many departures, no guide, rules never shown. Each has a change that fixes it and not the others. Cut half the departures and change nothing else: that fixes overspending only. Add one scene with an outsider who learns a rule by mistake, cutting nothing: that fixes a missing guide only. State the two rules that bear on the first turn, cutting nothing and adding no one: that fixes vagueness only. Write down what each reader group should say after each change before trying any of them.

**Point.** Most world symptoms have two causes. Name the rival diagnosis, then a change that would fix one and not the other. Common pairs:
- **Generic or set-dressing?** Both feel like a backdrop. Swap the setting for the genre's default version. If nothing is lost, it was generic. If originality is lost but the world still waits for the hero, it is set-dressing.
- **Info-dump or overspent?** Both feel heavy. Deliver the same facts in passing. If the weight goes, the delivery was at fault; if readers are still lost, there were too many facts.
- **Costume or no surplus?** Both feel unreal. Remove the main departure: if nothing changes, it was costume. If it holds but every detail does a plot job, the surplus is missing.
- **The world or care?** "I don't care about this world" may be either. The Bond theory splits the jobs: the world shouldn't care about the hero, but the audience must. If readers would miss the people but not the place, the fault is here; if they would miss neither, go to `character`.

Then test the chosen diagnosis with `hard-to-vary`: remove the suspect part on paper and predict what readers would say. If the prediction is the same either way, the diagnosis is idle.

## 11. Rivals

**A thin setting: more world, or more person?** This is the diagnosis form of a rival also recorded in `world-from-story.md`, section 11. *Egri:* what must be exposed is the person, above all what they want; question the character hard enough and the atmosphere, place and background come along without being aimed at *(built, for realist drama)*. *The Implied World theory:* a world feels real through its own business and its own surplus (principles 4 and 5), a job separate from any one character. *What tells them apart:* take a scene readers call thin. Version A adds only knowledge of the lead (her work, money, family, what she wants today) and lets details follow from that. Version B adds business that has nothing to do with her (a strike on the radio, a neighbour's feud, bread that costs more this week). If readers find A as real as B, Egri's route is enough for this story; if only B works, the separate job is real. A likely outcome: Egri's route suffices in a realist world close to the audience's own, where the baseline carries the rest, and worlds with many departures need the separate job.

**Detail everything, or show the tip.** This is the workshop's one full record of this rival; other files keep only a test for their own genre or material and point here. *Truby, Anatomy of Genres:* give as many of the world's rules as possible, across work, love, sport, money and speech. *Implied World theory, principles 2 and 6:* spend on a few departures and hold the rest in reserve. *Test:* put the full rules of work, courtship and play on the page in the first chapter, or let the same rules surface in passing across the book. The theory predicts the first overspends and the plot loses its pull; Truby predicts a richer world. The workshop's reading: decide as much as Truby asks, in the reserve, and show the tip.

**Recorded where their methods live.** Truby's world designed outward from the hero, against principle 4, and Egri's setting that comes free with character, tested on an invented world: `world-from-story.md`, section 11. Every element given a meaning, against surplus (principle 5): `world-materials.md`, section 9. McKee's longer open telling in prose, against the encyclopedia chapter: `prose-and-film.md`, section 11. For diagnosis they come to the same question as above: a thin or heavy world may need its detail moved, not added.

## 12. Traps

- **Taking the reader's word for the diagnosis.** "Thin" and "too much" can name the same fault. Pin the symptom first.
- **Fixing by adding.** Most world faults are fixed by cutting, delaying or moving facts.
- **Patching a rule at the moment of need.** A limit that appears just when the plot needs it reads as a patch. State limits early, where they cost someone.
- **Curing an info-dump with an outsider who asks about everything.** The questions become the dump. Let the outsider's mistakes teach.
- **Answering a wonder in the sequel.** Each answer shrinks the world. Expand sideways.
- **Blaming the world for a fault elsewhere.** A world nobody cares about may be a care problem (`character`); a reveal about the world that falls flat may be a scheduling problem (`plot`).
- **Reading one kind of reader as everyone.** Genre regulars and readers new to the genre bring different baselines.
