# World materials

*Sources: the Implied World theory (baseline, departure, surface, implication, reserve; principles 1 Minimal departure, 2 Attention budget, 3 Ripple, 4 Indifference, 5 Surplus, 6 Iceberg ratio and 8 Thematic fit; the guide prediction; the medium corollary); the Gap theory (principles 2 Breach, 3 Pressure, 4 Scheduling, 5 Promise and 7 Settlement; the departure gap); the Anticipation theory (the care and time terms, principles 3 Show the rules, hide the thing, 4 Lost refuge and 7 The ratchet, and its line that Horror is a world whose rules turn against the people living in it); the Bond theory (the Competence lever and where it breaks); Truby, The Anatomy of Story (the story-world chapter: land, people, tools and time; what natural settings and weather suggest; houses and cities; miniatures, changes of size, passageways, technology; the uses of story time; and the world parts of the symbol chapter); Truby, The Anatomy of Genres (the Science Fiction and Fantasy chapters: rules and what breaking them costs, society as a system and a culture, hinge technology, the magic system, minor characters as the society); Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing (one condition taken away; large forces reaching small lives); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 Choose the job, then the material. 2 Borrow what the audience brings. 3 Make a departure ripple. 4 Give the world business of its own. 5 Put people under pressure. 6 Show a society in small. 7 Carry the story's question. 8 Mark where the rules change. 9 Rivals. 10 Traps.

Words used in one sense only in this file: **material** is anything a world is made of that the audience can see, hear or be told about: land and weather, buildings, people and how they are organised, tools, and time. A **job** is what a piece of material does for the story, named by the owner-theory principle it serves. A **place** is any distinct location, as in `world-from-story.md`. Provenance marks: *built*, *fitted* or *asserted*, as in `hard-to-vary`.

## 1. Choose the job, then the material

The Implied World theory says worldbuilding is choosing and managing departures, not inventing everything (principle 1). So this is not a list of things a world must have. Truby sorts a world's material into land, people, tools and time *(asserted as a classification, and useful as a checklist)*; this file sorts it by what it does. Decide the job first, then pick the material that does it cheapest.

| Material | Jobs it does best | Sections |
|---|---|---|
| Landscape, weather, seasons | borrow ready-made meaning; pressure through isolation | 2, 5 |
| Houses, rooms, bars, ships | pressure; a society in small; business of its own | 4, 5, 6 |
| Cities | a society in small; pressure from every side | 5, 6 |
| Miniatures: a model, map, game or contest | a society in small; a preview of a later event | 6 |
| Changes of size | ripple from one cheap departure | 3 |
| Passageways between very different places | mark where the rules change | 8 |
| Society: who holds power, by what rules, with what excuse | a society in small; the pressure of a system the hero cannot see | 6; `world-from-story.md`, 5 |
| Tools, machines, magic | ripple; business of its own; the story's question | 3, 4, 7 |
| Rituals and holidays | business of its own; the story's question | 4, 7 |
| Clocks: the day, the deadline, the perfect day | pressure; surplus | 4, 5 |
| A past or future setting | the story's question | 7 |

*Test* (`hard-to-vary`, two routes to one job): for each material in a draft, name its job. Where two materials do the same job, keep both only if each also has a second job of its own.

## 2. Borrow what the audience brings

**Case.** *Midsommar* stages its worst in unbroken daylight at a summer festival. Sunshine usually reads as safety and play; here it becomes the place where nothing can hide, and the fear is worse for it (the Anticipation theory's lost refuge).

**Point.** Landscapes, weather, seasons and familiar buildings arrive with readings the audience already holds, so their price is close to nothing: they ride on the baseline (Implied World principles 1 and 2). Truby catalogues what settings and weather suggest, and claims these readings run deeper than culture *(asserted)*. The Implied World theory takes no stance on whether they are innate; the workshop treats them as learned, because his own examples are plainly cultural (section 9). The job is the same either way: write the usual reading, then play to it or turn it.
- **Write what the material usually suggests**, then decide whether this moment needs that. If it does, use it once and let it work: *Blade Runner* keeps its city in rain and darkness and never comments on the gloom. If not, turn it, as *Midsommar* turns daylight.
- **Most readings come in a bright and a dark form** (a sea can be freedom or a grave), so a reading can justify almost any choice after the fact and cannot choose for you. Decide the job first (section 1).
- **Would the audience predict the pairing?** A stock match of place or weather to moment tells the audience nothing new *(Truby warns against such pairings, asserted)*.
- **Stock is generic.** A world furnished entirely from these readings is the Implied World theory's medieval Europe with dragons: cheap, and felt to be cheap. Borrow freely for most material; spend on the few departures that matter.
- **An older story laid over a realist world** borrows the same way (Truby, *fitted from one film*). *Sunset Boulevard*'s narrator is a screenwriter, so he can see a faded star's mansion through fairy-tale images, and the allusions sound like him, not the author.
- **Medium.** Weather and landscape are almost free on film and take a sentence each in prose (`prose-and-film.md`).

## 3. Make a departure ripple

**Case.** In *Ant-Man* one change, size, turns a bathtub into a flood and a child's train set into a battlefield. The audience learns nothing new; every object is familiar, only rescaled.

**Point.** A departure convinces in proportion to its consequences (Implied World principle 3). Some materials yield consequences at a lower cost in attention than others.
- **Changes of size** (Truby, *partly built*). Rescaling makes the familiar strange with no new rules to learn, which makes it one of the cheapest departures there is. Stage the moment of change, since that is when the rules show. Then check it against the owner theories: a smaller hero faces worse odds, which feeds suspense as long as hope remains (Anticipation principle 1); a much bigger one faces too few, which is the Competence lever where it breaks (the Bond theory's Superman problem). So a character stays huge only briefly *(built)*.
- **Tools** (Truby, *asserted*). A tool extends what a body can do, so what a character uses, lacks or cannot pay for places them. In *Death of a Salesman* every payment on the fridge and the car eats the salesman's earnings: realist ripple, carried by appliances.
- **Invented technology** convinces through everyday effects, not specifications. In *Star Wars* droids are bought second-hand from scrap dealers and turned away at the bar door: one departure, thinking machines, meeting ordinary life. Truby, *Anatomy of Genres* *(built)*: pick one or two **hinge technologies** (his *fulcrum technology*: the tools a society leans on, often for how people travel, talk and keep knowledge), and check that the story's key act depends on them and that they change how people treat each other. Genre defaults: `.claude/skills/genre/references/genres/science-fiction.md`.
- **Rules with a cost** (Truby, *Anatomy of Genres*, *asserted*; the mechanism is the Anticipation theory's). For each rule of the world, write what happens to someone who breaks or misuses it. In *The Fly* a teleport splices the traveller with whatever else was in the pod. A rule whose breaking costs nothing makes no danger; a steep cost turns every use of the rule into a prediction problem (principle 3). Keep an invented law steady once stated: a law with consequences nobody planned breaks the world, as the Time-Turner did.
- **Magic as a tool** (Truby, *Anatomy of Genres*, asks for a full account of each power; these questions are the workshop's, grouped by principle). *Ripple:* what does using it cost, and whom? *Exits:* where does it stop, and what would it make possible that ends the story? Close that with a limit stated early. *Business:* who has it, and what does the society do about the people who do? *Wonder:* where it comes from can stay open. Most answers live in the reserve. Power is the Bond theory's Competence lever: past a point it kills suspense, so each gain needs a matching cost or limit. What magic does works like a mystery, stated and kept to; why it works can stay a wonder (Gap principle 5; the Anticipation theory makes the same split between a threat's rules and its origin).
- **Take one condition away** (Egri, *built by analogy*). Remove one ordinary condition of life (light, sleep, food, an income) and follow it through a whole day. It is ripple aimed at a person (`.claude/skills/character/references/building-a-character.md`, section 2).

Show consequences in passing and keep most in the reserve: the Implied World theory's water-starved *Dune* needs no explanation because every custom follows from one rule.

## 4. Give the world business of its own

**Case.** *Downton Abbey* opens on news that the *Titanic* has sunk. Nobody in the house caused it or wanted it, yet it drowns the heir and remakes the family's future before the first episode is out.

**Point.** A world feels real when it has business that has nothing to do with the hero (principle 4), and details with no plot job are what make a world (principle 5). Materials that carry business well:
- **New machines arriving** (Truby, *fitted*). A society that modernises goes about its own affairs and can run the hero over. In *Downton Abbey* the telephone, the motor car and the typewriter arrive whatever the family wants, and each changes who is needed, or who can leave, below stairs.
- **Large forces** (Egri, *built*). A crash, a strike or a war with no interest in the hero reaches his household anyway (`world-from-story.md`, section 6).
- **Calendars that turn on their own** (Truby, *fitted*). A school year, a harvest, a festival that comes round whatever the hero is doing. In *Jaws* the Fourth of July weekend arrives whatever the police chief does, and the town's need for summer money keeps the beaches open.
- **Households and haunts with a rhythm of their own.** Truby's *buzzing household* *(built)* names a house or bar where everyone has affairs of their own. In *Gosford Park* the servants' hall runs its own day of duties, gossip and feuds whatever the guests upstairs are doing. Test: can the reader place each person somewhere in the house doing something that is not about the hero?
- **Rituals done without comment** (the workshop's reading of Truby's point on ritual). An invented ritual shows a culture's beliefs without explanation, because the people performing it do not remark on it; it is surplus delivered in passing.
- **Routine kept on the page** (Truby, *built*). A plot cuts dead time; keeping some of it says what an ordinary day is worth here. *Paterson* follows a bus driver through a week of the same route, the same lunch and the same evening walk, and the sameness is the world.
- **Real people among invented ones** (Truby, *built*). *Ulysses* mixes Dubliners who existed with figures from Joyce's earlier stories; each arrives with a history the writer knows, which is the reserve doing its work (principle 6).

*Test:* the owner's. If the hero died in chapter one, what would this material still be doing next week? And keep some details that serve nothing at all, like Flaubert's barometer: a detail with a job is plot, not surplus.

## 5. Put people under pressure

**Case.** *12 Angry Men* locks twelve strangers in one small room on the hottest day of the year, with a fan that will not start. The heat and the locked door do half the work of the conflict before anyone raises a voice.

**Point.** A world shows itself best through what it does to people under strain (Gap principle 3). Materials that apply strain:
- **The house turned against its people** (Truby treats the house at length, *built*; the examples here are the workshop's). A house is the first shelter anyone knows, and that is why its turning frightens: the refuge becomes the threat (Anticipation principle 4). Decide which way this house presses: a big old house whose past works on the living (*The Haunting of Hill House*), or a small one where nobody can get away from anyone (the half-underground flat in *Parasite*, open to the street's drunks and its floodwater).
- **A world whose rules turn against the people living in it**, the Anticipation theory's reading of Horror: make a necessity dangerous (sleep, sound, looking), so there is no safe action left (`.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`, section 4).
- **Isolation.** Desert, ice, an island, a ship: nowhere to go and no one to fetch. A large vehicle keeps opponents aboard for the whole journey (`world-from-story.md`, section 3).
- **The city as threat from every side** (Truby, *asserted*): a crowded city where danger can come from any direction. Crime fiction has worn this reading thin, so it needs a turn of your own.
- **Clocks.** A deadline stated early points every strand at one moment and buys speed at the expense of texture *(Truby, built)*. *High Noon* runs close to real time towards the noon train. In a comic journey a deadline does a different job: it keeps the detours from feeling aimless (*Planes, Trains and Automobiles*, trying to get home for Thanksgiving). A single day or night gathers many strands to one close (*Dazed and Confused*, the last day of the school year). The time term itself belongs to `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`, section 1.
- **The perfect day** (Truby's term, *built*). A short stretch of shared work or celebration shows what the story will later threaten: the village feast, the harvest home, the wedding before the war. Keep it short, since harmony holds attention only briefly. It is care built before the threat (Anticipation theory), and it can serve as the ratchet's partial release.

Pressure must be costly on the world's own scale (`world-from-story.md`, section 5), and space that narrows towards the climax is in section 7 of that file.

## 6. Show a society in small

**Case.** *Once Upon a Time in the West*: among a murdered homesteader's things, his widow finds a toy town, the town he meant to build when the railroad came. One model holds a man's plan, a place's future and the forces that killed him for it.

**Point.** The audience infers the unseen from what the surface requires: if there are border guards, someone pays them (the Implied World theory's *implication*). Materials that let a small surface imply a large society:
- **A built space is a society in small** (Truby, *asserted*). Ask of each: who holds power here, who serves, who is shut out, and where the hero stands.
- **Shrink a city to something graspable** (Truby, *fitted*). Into one institution with its own rules and ladder of rank (*Network*, *The Hospital*). Or read it as a landscape: a vertical city as a mountain with the rich at the top (the Batman stories; Truby finds this one predictable); a city as a sea where lives at different depths never learn of each other, entered by a camera drifting from window to window (*Wings of Desire*). In prose, that roving is a narrator dipping from life to life.
- **Miniatures** (Truby, *built*): a model, a map, a snow globe, a game or a contest that shows a world's order at a glance, a side of a character, or who controls whom. The Games in *The Hunger Games* are Panem in small: children from the districts made to kill each other for the Capitol's entertainment. A miniature can also preview a place where something will happen (in *The Shining* the father studies a model of the hedge maze where the ending plays out), which is scheduling (Gap principle 4).
- **Power and its excuse** (Truby, *Anatomy of Genres*, *built*; he calls the two parts system and culture). Write two lines. First, who is on top here and how people get ahead. Second, the story that makes those lower down put up with it. How people treat each other follows from the first line; what they say to justify it follows from the second. Show the same pattern of power at two or three scales (a household, a workplace, the state) and the audience infers the rest (principle 6); Truby calls this *social fractals*.
- **Minor characters as the society** (Truby, *Anatomy of Genres*, *built*). Give each group of people two or three rules they live by, about kinship, honour, property or death. A group defined by one look or one trait is costume at the scale of a culture: a departure with no consequences (Implied World principle 3; `diagnosing-a-world.md`, section 4).
- **One name for a vast system** (Truby, *built*). *The Matrix* and *Network* each name a single thing that is also a web holding people in place. The name tells the audience the world has hidden strands and that they need not grasp them all at once: it does some of an outside aid's work, in the sense of the guide prediction.

A miniature that hints at a larger order serves the iceberg ratio; one that lays the whole world out works against it (Rivals).

## 7. Carry the story's question

**Case.** In *Aladdin* the lamp offers wishes, and each is a way to become someone without changing: a prince by decree, a life saved at no cost to himself. Aladdin's last wish, spent to free the Genie rather than to become the false prince again, is the story's answer: the change has to be earned *(the workshop's example of a reading Truby makes of the magic objects in the first Harry Potter book)*.

**Point.** The world's main departure should embody the story's question (principle 8). Materials that can carry it:
- **Tools and magic as argument.** For each special device, write what it promises its user and what it costs. Look for one that offers the hero a way round the change the story asks of them, and make refusing it part of the arc. Truby adds that invented machines show what the writer fears about people, and a character's handling of magic shows their self-mastery *(asserted)*.
- **A past or future setting as a lens** (Truby, *built from a view of what fiction is for*). Set a story in the past to show the present something it still carries, for good or ill: *Mad Men*'s 1960s office shows habits the audience's own offices have not shed. Set it in the future to enlarge a force already at work and show where it leads: *Children of Men* is not a forecast but a question about hope. The owner theories agree through principle 8. Truby's further claim, that historical fiction must judge the past by present standards, is his own; the owner theories take no stance on it.
- **Rituals and holidays** (Truby, *built from his definition*). A ritual turns a belief into something people do again and again, so staging one puts that belief in play. Write down the belief; decide whether the story endorses it, mocks it, or both. Bring the same kind of gathering back several times and let the changes show the arc: *Four Weddings and a Funeral* returns its friends to five ceremonies, and who stands beside whom tracks the story. That is the settlement read from repeats (Gap principle 7).
- **Cycles and lines** (Truby, *partly built*). A story that comes back round (a year, a day, the same place) says what stays the same, or makes the one thing that changed stand out: *The Lion King* ends where it began, on the same rock, with a new cub held up. A story that stops partway draws a line of change instead. Decide whether the circle is a celebration or a trap. A cycle the audience can predict loses its pull (Anticipation principle 7: the pattern must break before the audience learns it).
- **One object for a society in change**, and symbols generally: `.claude/skills/plot/references/symbols.md`.

## 8. Mark where the rules change

**Case.** In *Spirited Away* the family walks through a tunnel under an old red gate into empty grassland. By nightfall the dry riverbed they stepped across has become open water, and the way back is gone: the audience sees exactly where the ordinary rules stopped.

**Point.** Truby's *passageway*: a threshold between very different places, whose main job is to warn the audience that the everyday rules are about to stop applying *(built: a big change of rules needs a cue, or it feels arbitrary)*. It does part of a guide's work (the Implied World theory's prediction that worlds with many departures need one): it marks *where* the baseline stops. The outsider who learns alongside us, or an outside aid, still teaches *what* replaces it.
- **Find the exact point where the rules change.** Is there a threshold the character crosses there? If not, invent one that belongs to this story, not a borrowed wardrobe or mirror.
- **A crossing can be a scene with contents** (Truby, *asserted*). Every odd thing in it should preview a rule the audience will need anyway, or the lingering spends attention for nothing (principle 2).
- **Where the two worlds are far apart, add halfway places** (Truby, *fitted from the Harry Potter books*). In *Spirited Away* the way from the tunnel runs through a deserted street of food stalls, strange but nearly familiar, before the bathhouse. The price of learning is spread out.
- **Teach the rules on arrival** (Truby, *Anatomy of Genres*, *fitted*). Dorothy has barely landed when the Good Witch explains what has happened and what the shoes are.
- **Close on a gate already seen.** The return through a threshold the audience knows shows how far the hero has come.

## 9. Rivals

**Every element given meaning, or some left free.** This is the workshop's one full record of this rival; other files keep only a test for their own genre or scale and point here. *Truby:* every element of the world should carry story meaning. *Implied World theory, principle 5:* details that serve no purpose are what make a world. *Test:* take a scene and cut every detail with no story job; show both versions to readers. Truby's view predicts nothing is lost and the scene is sharper. The theory predicts the cut version reads as a diagram and the world feels less real. The two can live together if meaning is concentrated in the main places and the symbol web and the rest is left free.

**Readings built in, or learned** (a rival to the workshop's reading, not to the theory). *Truby:* what places and seasons mean runs deeper than culture. *The workshop's reading:* the Implied World theory takes no stance on this; the workshop treats such readings as learned, like the genre part of the baseline, because Truby's own examples are cultural. *Test:* play a Christmas scene to readers who grew up with Christmas in midsummer, once in snow and once in heat. If the snowy version feels cosy and seasonal only to readers raised on northern winters, the reading was learned, as the workshop assumes; if it feels so to both groups, Truby holds for this case.

**The miniature that shows everything.** *Truby:* a miniature's first job is to let the audience see the world whole. *Implied World theory, principle 6:* depth comes from how much is sensed beneath the surface, not from how much is shown. *Test:* open a book with a map that names every region and its rulers, or with the same map with one region torn away and never named. Truby predicts the first orients and enriches more; the theory predicts the second makes the world feel larger, without losing the reader, provided a guide is there, and that readers will speculate about the torn corner (fans gather at the gaps). This is the miniature's form of the rival over showing the tip, recorded in full in `diagnosing-a-world.md`, section 11.

**Detail everything, or show the tip.** Truby's call, in *The Anatomy of Genres*, to put as many of the world's rules on the page as possible, against principles 2 and 6. For materials the reading is the same: decide as much as he asks, in the reserve, and let a few materials show the tip. In full: `diagnosing-a-world.md`, section 11.

## 10. Traps

- **Material before job.** A storm, a castle or a festival picked for its look does no work until you can say which job it does.
- **A borrowed reading as permission.** A landscape or season with both a bright and a dark reading can justify anything after the fact. Test with a swap, not a reading.
- **Stock readings used straight by accident.** Decide on purpose whether each borrowed reading plays to type or against it.
- **Rules without costs.** If breaking a rule costs nothing, it makes no danger.
- **Power creep.** Each new ability makes the character harder to worry about.
- **An empty or endless passageway.** Nothing in it previews the new rules, or it lingers after the audience has adjusted.
- **A miniature that explains the whole world.** Let it hint at an order, not lay it out.
- **A deadline on everything.** Speed bought with texture; keep one where it pays.
- **Everything meaning something.** A world with no surplus feels like a diagram.
