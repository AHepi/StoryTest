# Growing the world from the story

*Sources: the Implied World theory (the central claim that a world seems to go on past what is shown; principles 2 Attention budget, 4 Indifference and 8 Thematic fit; the prediction that worlds with many departures need a guide); the Gap theory (Part 2 section 2, principles 2 Breach, 3 Pressure and 7 Settlement, the knowledge gap); the Anticipation theory's lost refuge and ratchet; the Bond theory's closing note that the world shouldn't care about the hero, but the audience must; Truby, The Anatomy of Story (the story-world chapter: the world as the cast's outward form, the one-line world, the arena, contrasts drawn from values, the opening world that binds the hero, hero and world changing together, the visual seven steps, and his readings of Ulysses, Casablanca and It's a Wonderful Life; the scene chapter's openings for The Godfather and Pride and Prejudice, and its rule for cutting scenes); Truby, The Anatomy of Genres (the Fantasy chapter: world against narrative drive, and the ordinary world against the fantastic); Egri, The Art of Dramatic Writing (the character and conflict chapters on environment: large forces, pressure measured on the character's own scale, a conflict traced to place and period, exits closed by circumstance, each scene a step between poles); McKee, Dialogue (the spine of action, for one rival); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 Two directions. 2 The world in one line. 3 The arena. 4 Places drawn from values. 5 An opening world that presses on the flaw. 6 The world shapes the person. 7 A place for each turn. 8 Hero and world, changing or not. 9 Mirror and business at once. 10 World learned on the move. 11 Rivals. 12 Traps.

Words used in one sense only in this file: a **place** is any distinct location inside the story (Truby's *subworld*); the **arena** is the one bounded space the action stays inside (Truby's term); the **binding world** is the opening world that confines the hero and works on the hero's flaw (Truby's *world of slavery*); **the story's question** is the one the Implied World theory's principle 8 means. *Hero*, *opponent*, *flaw* and *desire* are used as `.claude/skills/character/SKILL.md` uses them (Truby says *weakness* where this file says flaw). Provenance marks: *built*, *fitted* or *asserted*, as in `hard-to-vary`. Owner-theory terms (baseline, departure, surface, reserve, gap, surplus, pressure, settlement) mean exactly what the theories define.

## 1. Two directions

**Case.** In *Pride and Prejudice* the Bennet estate can pass only to a male heir, so five daughters must marry well or face poverty, and every desire in the book bends round that one rule (the Gap theory's own case). Truby reads *Casablanca* from the other end: Rick's café, where everyone waits for exit papers and nothing moves without a bribe, is built to be a picture of Rick's cynicism.

**Point.** The owner theories run the arrow from world to person. The world decides what counts as an event, what people want and what things cost; a character is a desire the world has shaped (Gap principle 2 and Part 2, section 2). Truby runs his method the other way: the cast comes first, and the writer then shapes a world to be their outward form, down to the smallest detail *(asserted; he ties it to T. S. Eliot's *objective correlative*, a physical thing that stands for an inner state)*. The Gap theory already makes room for this. It says the world comes first "logically, even if not in the writing". So the workshop takes Truby's method as an **order of work**, never as a claim about which shapes which. Design outward from the story if that is how you find the world; then check every place back from the world's side (section 9).

Why use the method at all: it gives what the theory leaves open, a way to choose which departures and places to spend on. Truby's *condenser-expander* is the reason *(built: attention is short and audiences link what they see)*: a place that does several jobs at once packs more into the attention budget (Implied World principle 2) than several places doing one job each. The jobs a place can do: show a person, stage a clash, carry the question, give the story a pressure point, or carry the world's own business (principle 4). *Test* (`hard-to-vary`, not a count): remove the place, then swap it for a neighbour place, and name what breaks each time. If nothing does, the place is decoration, unless it is surplus that belongs to this world. Where two places do the same job, keep both only if each also has a second job of its own.

## 2. The world in one line

**Case.** *It's a Wonderful Life* is one small town shown in two versions, the one George Bailey lived in and the one where he never lived. The layout asks the story's question, what one life is worth, before anyone speaks it.

**Point.** Truby's first step *(fitted to examples, with a built core)*: a story is a line, one thing after another, while a world is a space where everything exists at once. Join them by finding one physical layout that holds the story's movement, and write it as a single line. Some shapes that recur (the examples are the workshop's):
- **A route.** *The Lord of the Rings* runs from the greenest, safest corner of its world to the most barren, so the cost of the quest is laid out as ground.
- **One place across time.** *Our Town* shows one small town through a day, a wedding and a funeral, so the question of what an ordinary life is worth stays in one street.
- **Two versions of one place.** Bedford Falls and Pottersville (the case above, Truby's).
- **The plot's trick as a place.** *The Truman Show* builds a whole town as a set, so the deception is somewhere you can walk into.

This is the method side of Implied World principle 8: the main departure should embody the story's question (*The Handmaid's Tale* builds a state round fertility because the book asks who owns a body). *Swap test* (`hard-to-vary`): replace Bedford Falls with any other small town and nothing breaks, so the particular town is free. Collapse the two versions into one and the question has nowhere to be asked, so "one town, two versions" is what holds. If any layout would serve your line equally well, the line is a label, not a design. The story-level version of this line, Truby's *designing principle*, is in `.claude/skills/plot/references/premise-and-theme.md`, section 5.

## 3. The arena

**Case.** In *Alien* the ship is the arena: the crew cannot leave, and every clash happens inside its hull. The Company is never on screen, yet its orders reach in from outside and turn out to be the plot.

**Point.** Truby's *single arena* *(asserted; his reason, that drama leaks away past the boundary, is a metaphor)*: the story keeps to one bounded space, and what lies outside the boundary is not its business. The owner theories give a reason that is not a metaphor: people who cannot leave stay under strain, and a world shows itself best through what it does to people under strain (Gap principle 3). Truby gives several ways to keep a sprawling story inside one arena; the workshop groups them by what holds the wall:
- **A wall you can see.** A sealed place or vehicle: a submarine, a research station, a snowed-in hotel, a besieged town. Everyone, opponents included, stays inside, so conflict builds instead of restarting (*Das Boot*, *The Thing*) *(built)*.
- **Ground of one kind.** A journey still reads as one place if the land keeps one character and changes in one direction only (*The Road*: one burnt country, walked south towards the sea) *(built)*.
- **A home to measure by.** If the story must leave, leave once and come back, or keep the first place in view so the new one can be held against it (*Mad Max: Fury Road* drives out across the desert and turns back for the Citadel) *(built)*.

The disagreement with the owner theories is real: Truby says what lies past the wall is not part of the story, while the Implied World theory says a world feels real when it seems to go on past what is shown. The workshop's rule: **the wall bounds the action, not the world.** What lies beyond it reaches the audience as glimpses (orders, news, a view from a height, a name nobody explains) and never becomes a new stage. Treat each glimpse as a gap with edges: the audience knows what kind of thing is out there, as *Alien*'s crew know exactly what kind of employer they have. *Move:* list every location in the draft and mark the ones outside the wall. Fold each inside as a place, cut it, or turn it into a glimpse.

## 4. Places drawn from values

**Case.** In *Spirited Away* the bathhouse runs on wages, contracts and names signed away, and everything in it can be bought; the witch's sister lives in a cottage out along the railway where things are made by hand and given away. The story's quarrel, between greed and care, is laid out as two places before anyone argues it.

**Point.** Truby's move, in brief: build the world's visible contrasts out of the values the characters clash over *(built on his claim that values are what every quarrel is really about; how many contrasts are enough is his guess, asserted)*.
- For each main pairing of characters, name the values at stake, then a place, object or look for each side. Keep the strongest few and bring them back.
- **Let a place say what a character cannot yet see.** In *Brokeback Mountain* the high pasture is open, cold and free, and the town flats below are cramped and loud, long before Ennis will admit what the mountain meant *(the workshop's example of Truby's move, fitted)*. In Gap theory terms that is the knowledge gap carried by the world: the audience reads in the places what the hero has not yet admitted.
- **When the real conflict comes into view, the contrasts should move with it.** A contrast set in the first act and never touched again has stopped working.

This agrees with Implied World principle 8: the main contrasts carry the story's question. The owner theories add a check Truby does not make. *Swap test:* replace a region with any other of its kind (any enchanted forest, any mine under a mountain). If nothing in the story changes, it is the genre's furniture riding on the baseline. And give every value-built place something that belongs to neither side (section 9). Genre defaults for a world built from values: `.claude/skills/genre/references/genres/myth.md`.

## 5. An opening world that presses on the flaw

**Case.** *Groundhog Day* strands a weatherman who looks down on small towns and small people in the one small town he mocks, on the one day he finds most beneath him, and will not let him leave until the contempt has nowhere left to go. *Vertigo* does it in a clause: the fear of heights exposed in the first scene is the one the plot later turns against the hero.

**Point.** The binding world (Truby's term is *world of slavery*): the opening world keeps charging the hero for the flaw *(fitted from examples; a clear, usable test)*. It is the Gap theory's pressure principle aimed at one person, and it makes the Bond theory's Flaw lever real: a flaw convinces when the world makes the hero pay for it.
- **Three features.** Name the flaw. List three features of the opening world that tempt it, feed it or punish it. If none do, change the world or the flaw.
- **The world's own scale** (Egri, *built*). Is the pressure rare and costly in this world, or everyday? Egri blames a failed play, *Black Pit*, on this: the threat hanging over the hero, a wife giving birth without a midwife, was routine among the miners, so it moved nobody. The world decides what things cost (Gap principle 2), so measure pressure on its scale, not yours.
- **A system the hero cannot see** (Truby's checklist). If a larger system binds the hero, decide why the hero cannot see it. That is the Gap theory's knowledge gap, for the plot to schedule (`.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`).
- **Two worlds** (Truby, *Anatomy of Genres*, *built*). Where the story crosses into another world, make home the flaw in physical form, make the other world reward the flaw's opposite, give that world a catch that makes going home make sense, and let home look different on return because the hero sees it differently. If the last picture of home matches the first, the round trip has not paid.

A binding world can still keep its own business. A crime-ridden city or a refugee bar presses on the flaw while it goes about its own affairs; a world with nothing in it but the flaw is set-dressing (section 9).

## 6. The world shapes the person

**Case.** Egri takes apart a 1930s play, *Brass Ankle*: a man who has risen to lead a small Southern town and campaigns for segregation learns, at the birth of his child, of his wife's mixed ancestry. The town's hostility and his public role lock him into the stance that destroys him. Egri's point: in a big city the same facts would mean far less. Place and period give them their force.

**Point.** Egri runs the owner theories' direction. A person is made by body, place in society and mind, the mind being what the first two produce (the character side is in `.claude/skills/character/references/building-a-character.md`, section 2). Four of his moves work on the world *(each built, one by analogy)*:
- **Move the story.** Transplant the central conflict to the genre's default world or the audience's own. If it survives unchanged, the world is not doing its job *(the Gap theory, principle 2: a breach with no world could happen anywhere; Egri builds the same test from one play)*. This is the `hard-to-vary` swap turned on the whole world.
- **Large forces with no interest in the hero.** A crash, a strike or a war reaches the household anyway: Egri's shopkeeper is squeezed by a wage war he did not start, whichever side he takes, and the squeeze reaches his family. One force serves indifference (Implied World principle 4) and pressure (Gap principle 3) at once.
- **One pressure, many answers.** In *Dead End* the street boys commit the same crimes, each for a reason of his own. The world shows itself in the spread of answers, not in the hero's alone.
- **Close an exit with a rule of the world.** Nora in *A Doll's House* cannot raise a loan on her own signature, so she forges one. A law or custom that closes an easy way out asks less of the audience's belief than a convenient death does.

## 7. A place for each turn

**Case.** Truby's *Star Wars*: a desert farm where the hero feels stuck; the opponent's battle station; a garbage compactor whose walls close in while something moves in the water, the hero's lowest point and his brush with death in one room; a narrow trench run towards one small target; a great hall where the victors are honoured.

**Point.** Truby's *visual seven steps*: give each main turn of the structure a place of its own, chosen to express it, so the arc shows without comment *(built for the rule that narrowing space raises pressure; fitted for the rest, and loose, since almost any place can be said to express a turn after the fact)*. The jobs, grouped here in four:
- **Where the flaw lives.** The opening place (section 5).
- **Where the opponent's power lives.** A place that shows the opponent's reach and ability to strike at the hero's flaw, often a harsher version of the hero's own binding world.
- **Where the exits run out.** The lowest point and the final clash come where the people have the fewest exits, the least refuge and no help. Walls are one way to do it (*The Wild Bunch* ends in a walled courtyard); open ground can do it too: the three-way duel in the circular cemetery of *The Good, the Bad and the Ugly*, the chase across the stone faces of Mount Rushmore in *North by Northwest*. Truby's rule that the climax belongs in the most enclosed place of all is fitted and overstated, a pattern to question; what holds is lost exits, not walls. Squeezing space and time together raises tension further (a way out closing while a fuse burns; *tension* as `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`, section 7, defines it). Taking exits away step by step is a ratchet built out of space (Anticipation principle 7; it still needs its partial releases), and taking away every exit does the work of lost refuge, as a cut phone line does.
- **Where the ending shows.** The last place renders what the hero won or lost.

A place can also fight its turn on purpose: George Bailey says he wants to escape the town while standing in a loving family home, which sharpens both the desire and the town's grip *(fitted from one film)*. For a large, shapeless arena (a city, a ship, a network), borrow or invent a sequence of stations and give each one or two turns *(built: landmarks cut the effort of following a complex world)*. *Ulysses* lays the Odyssey's episodes over Dublin places.

*Move:* a table with columns for turn, place, what in the place expresses the turn, and which material it uses (`world-materials.md`). Rank the places by the exits, refuge and help left to the people in them; the ranking should tighten towards the climax. Look for turns that share a bland place for no reason, and places that express nothing. Two checks from the owner theories: each new place spends attention (principle 2), so merge where one place can do two turns; and a world where every place exists for a turn is the world that exists only where the camera points (section 9). The structure itself: `.claude/skills/plot/references/structure-and-conflict.md`.

## 8. Hero and world, changing or not

**Case.** In *Up* the house changes with the hero: full of a shared life at the start, dragged across a continent as a burden, and at last let go to settle by the falls, which is Carl's change made visible. *Chinatown* goes the other way: the water scheme goes through whatever the detective does, and the city he tried to set right is exactly as it was, except that now he knows it.

**Point.** Truby holds that hero and world usually change together, since the world is the hero's outward form *(asserted; "usually" absorbs counterexamples)*. He lays out several patterns *(fitted; a menu, not a test)*. The workshop's framing is simpler: the world changes with the hero, parts from him, or holds still while he changes, and each is a choice.

The Gap theory's settlement asks how the world's normal differs at the end from the start, and what that cost. A changed world and an unchanged one are both answers; choose on purpose.
- **Re-dress one place.** Pick a place that appears at the start and at the end. Decide how it looks, feels and is used each time. The difference is the settlement made visible.
- **One object whose fate tracks the world**, such as the cinema in *Cinema Paradiso*: `.claude/skills/plot/references/symbols.md`.
- **Remove the hero** (the indifference check). Does the world still change? In *Cinema Paradiso* it does: the town modernises for reasons of its own, and the cinema would fall with or without the boy. If the world changes only through what the hero does, that is fine, but name the cause in the story (the station destroyed, the law repealed). The parallel is rigged only when the world changes in step with the hero's inner state with no cause the story shows (the rain stops as he forgives). A fable can live with that; a world that asks to be believed cannot (Implied World principle 4).

## 9. Mirror and business at once

**Case.** Truby reads Rick's café as both Rick's mirror and a world that runs without him. *Do the Right Thing* shows the same double at the scale of a street: Mookie's deliveries carry the story down one Brooklyn block on the hottest day of the year, but the corner men, the old drunk, the radio DJ and the woman at the window all have days of their own, and the heat presses on every one of them.

**Point.** The working rule, and the reconciliation Truby's own best examples point to: **let the story choose which slice of the world we visit and what pressure it applies; let the world keep its own business and its own people.** The Bond theory puts the same split in one line: the world shouldn't care about the hero, but the audience must.
- **The owner's test.** If the hero died in chapter one, what would this place be doing next week?
- **One thing that is not about the hero.** For each place built from the hero, name one thing in it that has nothing to do with the hero: a routine, a feud, a debt, a festival. If there is none, it is set-dressing.
- **Swap the mirror.** Replace the place with a neighbour that mirrors the flaw just as well (another cramped flat, another corrupt town). If the story survives, the mirror is not what holds the place. Look for what the world's own rules give it that the neighbour lacks.
- **Give minor characters their own version of the world's bind** *(Truby, built, from his reading of* Ulysses*)*. On the *Do the Right Thing* block each neighbour carries the street's bind in a different shape. The story's question recurs across many lives, and the world gains business of its own.
- **Let it be a home first.** A house that is the hero's fear given walls frightens more if the audience first sees it as a real home with routines of its own: then its turning is a lost refuge, not a set built to scare (`.claude/skills/genre/references/genres/horror.md`).

## 10. World learned on the move

**Case.** *Mad Max: Fury Road* teaches its world almost entirely during a chase. Nobody stops to explain the Citadel, the War Boys or who owns the water; each is learned at the moment it blocks or helps someone who is fleeing.

**Point.** Every departure is learned with attention the plot also needs (Implied World principle 2). A world fact learned while someone is chasing something pays twice: it teaches the world and raises an obstacle. Learned while nobody wants anything, it takes attention and gives the plot nothing back *(Truby, Anatomy of Genres, makes the same point from narrative drive, built)*.
- **Put a pursuit in place early**, and move each piece of world explanation to the moment someone runs into it. Cut what nobody runs into.
- **Let a newcomer's desire drive the first scene** inside an unfamiliar world *(Truby, built)*. *The Godfather* opens on a man who knows nothing of how the family works, asking the Don for revenge; the audience learns the rules as he does. This is the Implied World theory's outsider guide, lasting a single scene.
- **Open on the world's normal business** *(Truby, fitted)*: the Don holding court at his daughter's wedding, the whole system gathered, before anything breaks it. The Anticipation theory asks for care before the threat, which in Gap theory terms means showing the normal before the breach. The rival, opening on the conflict, is in `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`, section 11.
- **State the governing rule and cut to the people who will test it** *(Truby, fitted)*. *Pride and Prejudice* opens on a mock-solemn general truth about rich single men and wives, then drops at once to one family.

When the middle sags after setup, a narrower goal with a clear finish line is the plot's repair: `.claude/skills/plot/references/structure-and-conflict.md`, section 9.

## 11. Rivals

**Mirror or business of its own.** This is the workshop's one full record of this rival; other files keep only a test for their own genre or scale and point here. *Truby:* the world is designed as the outward form of the hero, and each place is chosen to express him. *Implied World theory, principle 4:* a world convinces when it has business that has nothing to do with the hero. *Test:* in a draft where every place mirrors the hero, cut one mirror scene and put in its place, at the same length or shorter, one strand of the world's own business that never touches the hero and shows the world under some strain (a strike, a feud among the regulars, a failed harvest). Truby's view predicts a loss: less meaning per page. The theory predicts the world feels more real, and that the pressure on the hero now reads as the world's doing rather than the writer's. *The workshop's verdict:* the two fit when the hero's values design the main departures and places and the world's business and surplus sit on top of them (section 9).

*At the scale of a scene.* A scene that shows the world at its own business is not a scene that changes nothing, in the workshop's reading: if the business shows a departure, it changes the departure gap; if it is baseline business, it does the work of principles 4 and 5 (indifference, surplus). The attention budget (principle 2) limits how long it may run, and it does most when it also does a plot job, as *Alien*'s grumbling over bonus shares does. The book rules that point the other way are the books' own, patterns to question with this rival as the check: every scene moves each main character a step (Egri), a scene whose action serves some other want has left the spine (McKee), and a scene that neither advances nor sets up the hero's development is cut (Truby). Whether a whole scene of world business with no other job may stay is a question for the owner; the workshop's present reading is that it may, if it is short and shows the world under some pressure. Scene construction: `.claude/skills/plot/references/scenes.md`.

**Which shapes which.** *Truby:* the cast exists first and the world is built as their form. *Gap theory, principle 2:* the world decides what people want and what it costs them. *Test:* keep the cast and remove the world's key rule (end the entail in *Pride and Prejudice*). If the characters' desires change, the world was shaping them, as the Gap theory says. If nothing changes, the world was fitted round them afterwards and is not holding the story. Egri's move-the-story test (section 6) runs the same check from the other side.

**The wall.** *Truby:* what lies outside the arena is not part of the story, and opening the wall lets the drama leak away. *Implied World theory:* a world feels real when it seems to go on past what is shown. *Test:* add three glimpses past the wall that never become stages (a radio report, an order from far away, a ruin on the skyline nobody explains). Truby predicts slacker tension; the theory predicts a deeper world with tension unchanged, provided each glimpse is a gap with edges and not a new question the plot must answer.

**Setting comes free with character.** *Egri:* know the person well enough and the atmosphere, place and background fill themselves in; mood is not a task of its own. *Implied World theory, principles 1, 4 and 5:* the world's own texture and business are a separate job, and whatever is not designed is filled from the baseline. *Test:* open an invented world from character study alone. Egri predicts the world arrives with the person; the theory predicts the audience furnishes it from genre habits and it reads generic. In realist fiction set in the writer's own world the two mostly agree, which is why Egri's own advice is to write the world you know.

**Every element given a meaning, or surplus left free.** Truby's rule that every element of the world should carry story meaning, against principle 5. Recorded in full, with its test, in `world-materials.md`, section 9.

## 12. Traps

- **A world that exists only where the camera points.** Every place a mirror of the hero is set-dressing, however well designed.
- **Meaning read in afterwards.** Truby's rule that every element should carry meaning can always be satisfied after the fact. Use the swap test instead of the reading (the rival: `world-materials.md`, section 9).
- **A place for every turn, and too many places.** Each has a price in attention. Merge before you add.
- **A binding world whose pressure is routine** in that world. Measure it on the world's scale.
- **Genre furniture chosen for its look.** A region with no value of its own and no business of its own is the baseline showing through.
- **The hero's arc dictating every fact about the world.** The order of work may start from the story; the world still has to explain its own rules.
- **A world that changes in step with the hero's feelings.** A change the hero causes is fine if the story shows the cause; a world that brightens as he forgives, with no cause shown, is a fable. Choose the fable on purpose or give the change a cause.
- **A glimpse past the wall that turns into a promise.** Pressed and chased, it becomes a mystery the story must answer (Gap principle 5).
