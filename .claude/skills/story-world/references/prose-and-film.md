# Prose and film

## 1. Adding and taking away

**Case.** A market street. On the page it does not exist until it is named. Write "a market street" and the reader furnishes an ordinary one from the baseline; every stall, smell and voice beyond that costs a sentence, and every sentence spends the reader's attention. On screen the street arrives complete in the first frame: stalls, crowd, weather, the colour of the light, whether or not anyone chose them. The novelist's question is what to add. The film-maker's is what to take away.

**Point.** Prose builds by adding and film builds by taking away (the medium corollary). In a book nothing exists until it is named; in a film everything in the frame exists at once. Each medium fails when it tries to do what the other does cheaply. So fix the medium before judging how a world is delivered, and ask of each world fact whether it is cheap or expensive here. Then move it to where it is cheap, and pay for the expensive ones only where the story needs them.

## 2. What is cheap and what is expensive

The theory's table, with what follows for a writer:

| | Prose | Film |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | empty: nothing exists until named | full: everything in the frame exists at once |
| Cheap | history, law, belief, inner life | texture, crowds, objects, sound |
| Expensive | sensory density: each detail costs a sentence | abstraction: institutions, rules, history |
| Surplus lives in | a clause in passing | the background of the frame |
| Gaps live in | whatever goes unnamed | offscreen space and sound |
| Typical failure | the info-dump | exposition dialogue, title crawls, clutter |
| So spend on | one sensory detail per place that implies more (section 6) | one person suffering the rule (section 5) |
| And save by | letting the baseline furnish the rest | letting the background carry the texture unremarked |

Three more points come from the other theories. The Gap theory says each medium leans towards one of its five gaps: prose towards the perspective gap (between what happened and what the teller says), film towards the knowledge gap (between what the audience knows and what the characters know) (section 7). The Anticipation theory notes that in prose, mentioning a threat points straight at it, while on screen it can sit unnoticed in the frame; the same holds for any world detail. The Bond theory notes that prose has inner life built in, while film has to borrow it (voice-over, confidants, talking to camera), which fits the table: belief, cheap on the page, is expensive on screen.

## 3. Where surplus lives

**Case.** The theory's prose case: "The door dilated", a clause no character remarks on, tells more about a society than a paragraph on doors. The screen equivalent is *Blade Runner*'s streets: crowds under umbrellas with glowing shafts, signs in many languages, a street patois, a blimp advertising a new life off-world. The film explains almost none of it. *Children of Men* and *Mad Max: Fury Road* keep their worldbuilding in the background in the same way (the theory's cases).

**Point.**
- **On the page, grammar sets the emphasis.** A detail in a subordinate clause, in a list, or in a character's unconcerned action reads as taken for granted. The same detail given its own sentence, or its own paragraph, reads as something to notice. Since naming is already emphasis in prose, surplus on the page has to be made unemphatic by syntax and by the characters' indifference to it.
- **On screen, the camera's refusal to look is what makes surplus.** A background detail the camera never pushes in on stays texture. Push in, hold on it, or let a character look at it, and it becomes a question the story has promised to answer (Gap theory, principle 5: emphasis sets the promise). Keep surplus out of focus, at the edge of the frame, unmentioned.
- **In both, the carrier is someone who has lived with it all their life.** The detail convinces because nobody in the scene finds it remarkable.
- **Clutter is surplus fighting the foreground.** A frame so crowded with invented objects that the audience can't tell what the scene needs spends attention on everything at once. Keep the foreground to what the scene needs and let the background stay unremarked. The page has the same fault in a paragraph where every noun is new.

## 4. Where gaps live

**Case.** In the first *Star Wars* film the Clone Wars are a name spoken in passing, never shown: a gap with edges (a war, long ago, in which the old man who guides the hero fought). Fans speculated for more than twenty years, as the theory predicts, and then the prequels filled it. In prose, *The Road* never names the catastrophe that burned the world; the reader knows only that it was fire, ash and the end of things.

**Point.**
- **In prose, gaps live in whatever goes unnamed** (the theory's table). A thing named but never explained is a gap if nobody, the author included, fills it in (Tom Bombadil); it is a glimpse of the reserve if the author knows the answer (the appendices). Either way it stays a wonder only if the naming is in passing. Its edges are carried by a phrase: what kind of thing is missing.
- **On screen a gap lives offscreen:** a name heard and never shown, a sound from beyond the frame, a door the camera never goes through, a trace (a scorch mark, a memorial, a ruin). Film's difficulty is that whatever it shows is decided (a face, a size, a colour), so it keeps a gap open mainly by not showing.
- **On stage**, in the wings and offstage (section 9).

## 5. Paying for abstraction on screen

**Case.** *Mad Max 2* opens with a remembered narration that sketches how the world fell and ties the fall to what happened to Max. The history is told once, briefly, and it doubles as the hero's past *(Truby, Genres, fitted from one example)*. *The Godfather* teaches the rules of the Don's world through a petitioner who doesn't know them and makes a mistake *(Truby, built)*.

**Point.** Institutions, rules and history are film's expensive goods, and its typical failures (exposition dialogue, title crawls) are what happens when they are paid for in talk or text. Ways to pay, cheapest first:
- **Show what the rule does to one person, now.** The frame cannot show a law; it can show a man turned away at a checkpoint. This is the Gap theory's principle 3: a world shows itself best through what it does to people under strain.
- **Let an outsider's mistake teach it.** The outsider is the guide the theory says a many-departure world needs.
- **Name the system with one image,** ideally in the title, so the audience knows it is entering a world of hidden strands and need not grasp them yet *(Truby, built)*.
- **Use an outside aid in a small dose:** a crawl, a title card, a place-and-date caption. The story trips for a moment and moves on *(McKee, built)*. The theory puts the crawl both among the outside aids and among film's typical failures; both hold, and the difference is the dose.
- **Keep a voice-over short and tie it to the hero,** so the history is also character (Truby, above). The Bond theory adds that voice-over is one of the ways film borrows an inner life.
- **Last, dialogue that explains,** and only when someone has a real reason to say it to someone who doesn't know it: `.claude/skills/dialogue/references/prose-stage-screen.md`, section 8.

## 6. Paying for texture on the page

**Case.** In the novel of *The Handmaid's Tale*, Offred notices that the shop signs are pictures, not words. The detail costs half a sentence, and it implies a law (women may not read), an economy built round it, and how settled that law already is. One sensory detail bought a rule as well.

**Point.** Sensory density is prose's expensive good: every detail costs a sentence. Ways to pay:
- **One detail per place, chosen to imply more than itself.** A detail that carries an implication buys texture and a rule at once.
- **Let the teller's habits choose** (section 7): what a person of this world would notice is already a selection.
- **Save the full sensory passage** for the place where the story needs it, and let the baseline furnish the rest.
- **Don't write a film's opening sweep.** Showing the whole arena and its edge before moving inside it is one shot on screen and a page in prose *(the workshop's reading of Truby's opening sweep)*.
- **Telling is cheap; the encyclopedia chapter is not.** History is the page's cheap good, so a narrator can give it directly, a clause or a few sentences at the moment the story needs it. What fails is the reserve set out on the surface while the story waits: prose's typical failure, the info-dump (`diagnosing-a-world.md`, section 6). McKee would allow a longer stretch of open telling if the sentences hold; that is a rival (section 11), and until it is tested the procedure follows the theory: show the tip.

## 7. The teller as the world's filter

**Case.** *Never Let Me Go* is told by Kathy, who grew up inside the system that uses her and takes it for granted. The truth about the students reaches the reader early and quietly, in her ordinary words for it, and what appals is her acceptance (the Gap theory's case). A camera finds this harder, because it cannot take a word for granted the way a narrator can: what it shows, it shows in full. What it can do instead is show a world detail that nobody in the scene notices.

**Point.**
- **In prose the world arrives through a teller,** who can only name what they notice and will not explain what they take for granted. That is surplus in passing built into the voice. Choose a teller who takes the departures for granted and the world is shown the cheapest way; choose an outsider and the telling becomes the guide. A teller's trade or reading can frame a whole world: the screenwriter who narrates *Sunset Boulevard* sees a star's decaying mansion through a Dickens novel and a fairy tale *(Truby, fitted from one example)*.
- **On screen the camera can know more than anyone in the scene.** A background the characters ignore is the audience's alone, which is the knowledge gap, the one film leans towards, put to work for the world.
- Choosing and setting a teller is `plot`'s work: `.claude/skills/plot/references/telling-and-perspective.md`.

## 8. Adaptation

**Case.** Tolkien never settles whether the Balrog has wings; the text can be read both ways, and readers have argued about it for decades, which is the theory's prediction that fans gather at the gaps. Peter Jackson's film had to decide, and gave it wings of shadow. The same films left Tom Bombadil out: a wonder that could not be filmed without being decided was cut instead.

**Point.** Filming a book forces a decision on every gap the author left open: a face for each character, a size for each creature, a look for each room. That is why adaptations often feel smaller than the book even when they are lavish (the theory). Moves:
- **List the book's gaps and wonders.** For each, choose: decide it, cut it, or keep it open by the screen's means (offscreen, sound, darkness, a glimpse). The last route is the workshop's reading of a seeming clash inside the theory: its table puts film's gaps offscreen, while its adaptation claim says filming forces a decision on every gap. Keeping a gap offscreen is itself a decision, and the film may still read smaller for it.
- **Move cheap goods to cheap goods.** A history the novel tells in a paragraph becomes an object in the background (a ruin, a monument, a scar). A belief given from inside a mind becomes a ritual someone performs.
- **Book to screen, dramatise the inner life.** A camera cannot film thought *(McKee, built)*, and much of a novel's world lives in what its teller thinks and assumes. For each thing the narration conveys that no one says, find a deed, an object or a scene.
- **Screen to book, choose the texture.** The frame gave it free; the page must pay for each detail, so pick the few that imply the most.
- **In a franchise, an adaptation is also an expansion.** Each decision becomes canon (`diagnosing-a-world.md`, section 7).

## 9. Stage, television and games

The owner theory covers prose and film. What follows extends it as the workshop's reading of the notes on McKee and Truby: a pattern to question, not part of the theory.
- **Stage.** The frame and the curtain give the drama its boundary *(Truby, asserted)*, and the audience listens first and looks second *(McKee, fitted)*. So the stage is cheap for talk, a few objects and the audience's imagination, and expensive for crowds, spectacle and quick changes of place. Its gaps live offstage: the violence a messenger reports in Greek tragedy, the axes heard at the end of *The Cherry Orchard*. A bare stage can hold a whole town when a narrator builds it in words and the actors mime the rest, as *Our Town*'s Stage Manager does; he is a guide in the theory's sense *(McKee, asserted)*. The stage's shared pretence (everyone agreeing to treat live actors as the people they play) makes a strange or heightened world easier to accept, but the pretence holds only while the world stays consistent *(McKee, built)*.
- **Television.** Its means are close to film's; what changes is its length. Dozens of hours need more world than two, and the easiest supply is the reserve and the gaps, so a long series meets the expansion paradox from inside. *Lost* raised questions insistently and did not answer them (the Gap theory's case of betrayal). Decide which open questions are wonders before the second season, not after.
- **Games and other interactive work.** Not yet covered. The notes do not support a section, and the owner theories assume an audience rebuilding a world from a surface someone else chose; a player who chooses where to look changes what the surface is. Treat any advice here as open until the owner supplies a source.

## 10. Questions for a draft

1. Which medium is this, and which of the world's facts are expensive in it?
2. For each expensive fact, can it move to a consequence someone suffers, a clause (prose) or the background (film)?
3. Where does the surplus live, and does the medium's emphasis keep it unremarkable?
4. Where do the gaps live, and what gives them edges: a phrase, a sound, a trace?
5. Who or what delivers the world: a teller who takes it for granted, an outsider, a camera that sees what nobody notices?
6. Is the draft failing in its medium's typical way: an info-dump; exposition dialogue, a crawl that runs long, a cluttered frame?
7. In an adaptation, which gaps did the new medium decide, and could any stay open by its own means?

## 11. Rivals

**Is a world's force the same in every medium?** *Truby:* calling film a visual medium misleads; what moves an audience is the story world, a web of elements that all carry meaning, and this holds in every medium alike *(asserted)*. *The Implied World theory:* prose and film build worlds in opposite ways and at different costs. *What tells them apart:* take one image-rich world design (contrasting places, weather at each key moment, a distinct place for each stage of the story) and render it twice in prose, once describing every element and once with one implying detail per place. If readers of the full version follow the plot and the people as well as readers of the lean one, and the full version feels no heavier on the page than the same design did on screen, Truby's claim holds. If the full prose version loses readers while the film carried the same design lightly, the corollary holds. Truby's own city-as-ocean example concedes part of the point: in a film that is not a Fantasy the floating effect is done by the camera, and in prose it would need a roving narrator who dips from life to life.

**Can a long stretch of telling hold a reader?** *McKee:* in prose, background can be set out openly and at length if the language holds the reader *(built, from the difference between reading and performance)*. *The Implied World theory:* the encyclopedia chapter is the info-dump; build as much as you like and show the tip, since depth comes from what is sensed, not from how much is shown (principles 6 and 2). *What tells them apart:* give readers the same history two ways in one chapter: once as a two-page, well-written narrated block, once spread through the chapter as clauses in passing. If readers of the block don't skim and rate the world as deep as the spread version, McKee holds for this case. If they skim, or call it a lore dump, the theory holds. Until it is tested, the procedure follows the theory.

## 12. Traps

- **Judging a world's delivery before fixing the medium.** A page that would be clutter on screen may be exactly right in prose, and the reverse.
- **Writing a film's opening sweep in prose, or a novel's history lesson on screen.** Each medium fails when it does what the other does cheaply.
- **Pointing at surplus.** Push the camera in on it, or give it its own sentence, and it becomes a question.
- **Expecting an adaptation to keep the book's size.** Filming forces a decision on every gap (the theory). Keeping a gap offscreen is one possible decision, but expect the film to read smaller wherever it has had to decide.
- **Treating the crawl as free.** It is an outside aid in a small dose and a failure in a large one.
- **Taking the stage, television and games notes as theory.** They are the workshop's extension and should be tested before they are relied on.
