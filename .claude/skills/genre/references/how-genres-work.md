# How genres work

*Sources: the Implied World theory (the baseline; principles 1 Minimal departure, 2 Attention budget, 3 Ripple, 4 Indifference, 5 Surplus and 8 Thematic fit), the Gap theory (principles 1 Nested inference, 2 Breach, 4 Scheduling, 5 Promise and 7 Settlement; the knowledge gap), the Anticipation theory (the suspense equation; principles 5 Broken contract, 6 Knowledge position, 7 The ratchet and 8 Residue) and the Bond theory (care, and principle 7); Truby, The Anatomy of Genres (the introduction, the closing chapter and the appendix, with the genre families and contrasts from the genre chapters), and Truby, The Anatomy of Story (genre plots, drawing a story's form out of its premise, turning a genre's symbols over); in the workshop's own words.*

Contents: 1 What a genre is, here. 2 Promises and guarantees. 3 Beats: jobs in a system. 4 The genre's question. 5 Twelve genres at a glance. 6 Families and neighbours. 7 Truby's order of genres. 8 Mixing genres. 9 Rising above a genre. 10 Where storytelling is heading. 11 Rivals. 12 Traps.

## 1. What a genre is, here

**Case.** Say "a body in the library of a country house" and the audience has done a great deal of work before anything else is written. It expects a closed circle of suspects, a death that happened before the story began, an investigator with a gift, clues it could spot for itself, and an answer at the end that surprises and still fits. It relaxes about some things: the investigator will not be the next body. And it is already asking how the truth gets found. None of this is on the page.

**Point.** In this workshop a **genre** is a shared baseline, a set of promises, a set of guarantees and a characteristic question about how to live, which the audience brings before the story starts. Three parts come from the owner theories; the fourth is Truby's, framed by the Gap theory:
- **Baseline** (Implied World theory): what the audience assumes by default, the real world plus the habits of the genre. The genre's share of it is its **defaults**. Truby adds *(fitted)* that the defaults cover events as well as settings: an audience expects a Love Story's first dance as surely as it expects a Western town to have a saloon.
- **Promises** (Gap principle 5): what the genre undertakes to deliver, including an answer to the question the audience expects the story to press (section 2).
- **Guarantees** (Anticipation principle 5): protections the audience assumes, so part of it relaxes (section 2).
- **Question** (Truby's outlook on living, restated as a question; Gap principle 7 frames how it is answered): what the genre asks about how to live, which the story answers by how it settles (section 4).

Truby's own model *(asserted)*: each genre has fifteen to twenty signature events, his *beats*, which work together as a *story system*, and what they exist to carry is the genre's outlook on living, his *Mind-Action story view*. In the workshop's terms the beats are the genre's usual way of keeping its promises, and the outlook is its question.

Two things follow from "before the story starts". The genre belongs to the audience: a writer can use it, move it or break it, but cannot switch it off by ignoring it. And the genre sets which breach the audience expects. The body in the library is a breach in a Detective story and a Love Story alike: it breaks the household's own normal (Gap principle 2). A Detective audience came for that breach, so it surprises nobody. In a Love Story it also breaks with the genre's defaults, so the audience revises what kind of story this is: a guest genre has arrived, or a new lead (section 8). The Gap theory has the world decide what counts as an event; that the genre also sets which events the audience expects is the workshop's extension.

## 2. Promises and guarantees

The genre files keep these apart, because breaking each does something different.

**Case.** A Love Story promises to settle whether the two end up together, and to let the audience feel the love rather than hear about it. It guarantees, in the usual case, that they will. *Casablanca* keeps the promise and breaks the guarantee. The question of union is pressed from the moment she walks in, and it is answered on screen, by the hero's own choice: no, for a cause the audience believes is larger than the two of them. The audience grieves; it does not feel cheated. Now suppose the film had let the question drop: she stops appearing, and the last act is only about the war. The same outcome, reached that way, would be a betrayal.

**Point.**
- A **promise** is something the genre undertakes to deliver: the moments its audience comes for, and an answer to the question the genre leads the audience to expect. The genre only sets up the expectation. The story makes the question a *mystery*, an open question it promises to answer, by pressing it: lingering on it, having characters chase it, bringing it back (Gap principle 5; that the expectation is there before page one is the workshop's reading). Nearly every story in a genre presses its genre's question, and then owes an answer, not a particular one; a guest genre's question the story never pressed is not owed (section 8). Leaving a pressed question unsettled, or dropping the moments, reads as betrayal (Gap principle 5; the theory speaks of open questions, and the workshop stretches the word to a genre's owed moments because dropping one reads the same way). A promise can be twisted (section 9); it cannot be quietly dropped.
- A **guarantee** is a protection the audience assumes, so part of it relaxes (Anticipation principle 5: the star survives, the child is safe). Break one early and convincingly and every later threat is real; break all of them, so that characters die all the time, and care collapses, and suspense with it. Principle 5 is about fear, and a late break cannot make later threats real, since none are left. The workshop extends it: broken at the end, a guarantee gives the ending weight or leaves residue (Gap principle 7; Anticipation principle 8).
- **The test that tells them apart** (the cheated-or-unsafe test): if it went, would the audience feel *cheated*, or lose something it had relaxed into? Cheated marks a promise: something owed was not delivered. The loss of a protection marks a guarantee: broken early, it leaves the audience *unsafe*; broken at the end, it leaves grief, as *Casablanca* does. Some things that look like guarantees are promises by this test: a monster keeping its stated rules, a fair solution in Detective, a world's physics holding in Science Fiction. Breaking one of those is not a twist.
- **That the question is answered is a promise; how it is answered is the genre's usual answer.** Where the audience relaxes into that answer as a protection for someone it cares about (the couple united, the hero coming through), the answer is also a guarantee. Where it is only a habit of the genre (a battle at the climax, the suspects gathered for the solution), it is an expectation, and overturning it is a twist on how the promise is kept (section 9), not a broken guarantee. Either way, change the answer and keep the promise, and the audience gets its answer, just not the one it expected.

## 3. Beats: jobs in a system

**Case.** A Western draft has a gunfight in the main street because Westerns have one. Cut it and nothing earlier needs to change: no code was set up for it to test, and the town's future does not hang on it. In *High Noon* the same event is held in place by everything before it: the marshal's code, a town that leaves him to face it alone, a clock running down to the train. Cut it and the film has no ending.

**Point.** A **beat**, in this skill, is one of the recurring events a genre is known for, named by the job it does (Truby's genre beats; not the small beat inside a scene that `.claude/skills/plot/references/scenes.md` means). A **trope** is a recognisable genre element that does no structural job. Truby's claims, and how the workshop uses them:
- **Beats form a system; tropes decorate** *(partly built: a beat's force comes from what prepared it)*. Test any genre element by removal: if nothing later changes, it is a trope. Connect it or cut it, unless it does another job. A detail that serves no plot can still make the world feel real (Implied World principle 5, surplus), if it is particular to this world and taken for granted; a stock genre element is a default instead, and adds to the generic (section 11).
- **Each beat serves the genre's outlook** *(asserted; Truby borrows the architect's slogan that form follows function)*. The check: beside each beat, write which promise it keeps or which part of the question it presses. A beat that does neither is a trope, or a candidate to twist.
- **Every beat must appear** *(fitted, from commercial fiction)*. The workshop does not follow this as a rule. A missing beat is a question, not a fault: what job did it do, and does the draft get that job done some other way? If the job is done, the beat can go. If the job was a promise and nothing does it, the audience will notice (Truby's example: a Love Story with no first dance).
- **Genre plots are big but preset** (*The Anatomy of Story*, *built*): told so often, they lose whatever belongs to one hero only. The test for every beat: what about it could only happen to this hero? If nothing, it is riding on the defaults.

The genre files regroup each genre's beats by job, in the workshop's own names, with a question beside each for a draft that lacks it.

**Under the beats.** Truby's appendix restates his general model of how a hero changes, seven steps from flaw to a new balance (his *Story Code*), and holds that each genre bends it to carry its outlook *(fitted from many stories)*. Each genre file's sections 5 and 6 show the bending: the flaw its hero usually carries, the shape of her goal, the kind of opponent. The model itself, read as jobs, is in `.claude/skills/plot/references/structure-and-conflict.md` (section 2), and change in `.claude/skills/character/references/change-and-growth.md`.

## 4. The genre's question

**Case.** Two stories end with the hero walking away from the town she saved. In a Western the walk answers the genre's question: the protector who makes a home possible for others cannot share it. In a Love Story the same walk is a farewell, and it answers how much she could love. The event is the same; the genre decides which question it settles.

**Point.** Truby gives each genre an outlook on how to live and a recipe for living well *(asserted)*. The workshop keeps these as the genre's **question**: something the story answers through how its gaps settle (Gap principle 7), not a lesson it delivers. Three uses:
- **Test the climax against the question, not the recipe.** A story that only illustrates the recipe preaches. One that answers against it (the recipe costs too much, or fails) still keeps the genre's promise, because it answered.
- **Let the world ask it.** The world's main departure should embody the story's question (Implied World principle 8); in a genre story, the genre's question is a strong first candidate.
- **Give the other side its best case.** An opponent who argues a real counter-answer keeps the question open until the end.

## 5. Twelve genres at a glance

One row per genre file. The questions are the workshop's short forms of Truby's outlooks. The last column is the workshop's short form of what each genre file says (in its sections 2 and 9) about which of the Gap theory's five gaps the genre mainly runs on (departure, breach, withholding, perspective, knowledge). If the two ever differ, the file leads and this row is corrected.

| Genre | Family (Truby) | Its question, for a draft | Runs mainly on |
|---|---|---|---|
| Horror | speculative | What is this hero running from, and what would turning to face it cost? | knowledge, and the fear ladder |
| Action | mythic | Will you stand and fight, and what will winning make of you? | withholding, as suspense: how, not whether |
| Myth | mythic | What is this hero really looking for, and what of them will last? | departure: the world's wonders; withholding: who the hero really is (the audience often guesses first) |
| Memoir and Coming-of-Age | none | What pattern, or what belief, keeps her unfree, and what act breaks it? | withholding, as curiosity about the past; perspective: then against now |
| Science Fiction | speculative | Which habit does everyone use without thinking, and what will she choose once she sees it? | departure |
| Crime | crime | Who, if anyone, may live above the law, and what payment settles the debt? | withholding, as suspense: the culprit is known; will he pay? |
| Comedy | none | Which front does she hold hardest, and what does giving it up cost her? | knowledge: the audience sees the front |
| Western | mythic | Stand or run, and what does making a home for others cost the one who makes it possible? | withholding, as suspense: the showdown stretched in time |
| Gangster | crime | How does he win the city without losing his soul, and does he ever see the price? | withholding, as suspense: the fall is expected, the how is not |
| Fantasy | speculative | Who could she be with the ordinary rules switched off, and can she bring it home? | departure: the other world and its rules |
| Detective and Thriller | crime | How is the truth found, and who carries the blame? (Thriller: can she trust those closest to her?) | withholding, as curiosity (Thriller: withholding, as suspense, and fear) |
| Love Story | none | How much can this person love, and what will they give up for it? | withholding, as suspense: will they unite? |

## 6. Families and neighbours

Truby groups genres in families that share many beats *(fitted)*: a mythic family of fighter heroes (Myth, Action, Western), a crime family (Crime, Detective, Thriller, Gangster) and a speculative family (Fantasy, Horror, Science Fiction). Comedy, Love Story, and Memoir and Coming-of-Age stand outside all three. In Implied World terms a family is a set of genres whose defaults overlap, which is why borrowing inside one is cheap and why neighbours get confused. Some genres are blends in his reading *(asserted)*: Thriller crosses Detective with Horror, Crime joins a known wrong to Action's contest, and the Buddy Story joins Action to Love Story.

**Sorting questions for neighbours** (the workshop's use of Truby's contrasts). Ask what the middle of the draft mostly does:
- works out who did it (Detective); survives while finding out (Thriller); hunts a criminal already known (Crime); rises and falls inside a criminal organisation (Gangster);
- grows along one long road (Myth), or jumps into another world and back (Fantasy);
- fights a known opponent, asking *how* the hero wins (Action), or uncovers who the opponent really is (Thriller);
- builds its other world to test values (Fantasy) or to model a system (Science Fiction); closes doors on a hero who shrinks (Horror) or opens them on one who grows (Fantasy);
- knocks the hero down again and again, and she gets up each time (Comedy), or lowers her with no way back (Horror);
- changes both leads, each wanting the other (Love Story), or has one lead want and the other be won (Action);
- tests a young self on one step towards answering for who she is (Coming-of-Age), or follows a real teller looking back for the pattern that trapped her (Memoir).

If none of these settles it, ask which question in the table in section 5 the middle keeps asking.

A related question sorts the fighter genres from Horror: where does the hero's bondage live? Inside her (Horror), in her own immaturity (Myth), or in an outside enemy she must fight (Action).

**Doing and finding out.** **Test:** mark each scene A (someone acts) or L (someone, or the audience, learns something new). Truby's placement *(fitted)*: the mythic family leans to doing, Detective and Thriller to finding out, and Crime and Gangster sit between. In Gap terms, learning is the audience closing gaps as the plot schedules them (principle 4). A finding-out genre with long runs of A and no reveals has drifted off its genre; so has a doing genre that stalls in reveals.

## 7. Truby's order of genres

Truby ranks his genres on a ladder, after Hegel, with Horror at the bottom and Love Story at the top *(asserted)*: roughly, a higher rung means a harder flaw for the hero to beat and a wider outlook, and he ends by calling every rung necessary. The genre files carry his chapter numbers, in the same order.

The workshop treats the ladder as a value judgement, not a craft rule: none of the owner theories ranks genres, and their mechanisms work in all of them. The workshop offers a different order for a writer's purposes, drawn from the Gap theory: by the gap a genre mainly runs on (section 5), because that tells a writer which tools the genre leans on. One use of the ladder survives *(the workshop's inference, not Truby's)*: in a blend of a lower and a higher genre, let the hero's arc climb from the lower genre's outlook to the higher one's, with the lower genre's missing piece as the hero's flaw.

## 8. Mixing genres

**Case.** *Star Wars* (1977) layered Fantasy in space over Science Fiction, the Western, Arthurian Myth and the samurai film, a branch of Action, and beats from different systems arrived so close together that a practised viewer could not guess the next one. Two years earlier *Jaws* was a single genre, nothing added. Truby dates the shift to blends from the first *(fitted, largely from that one film)*: most popular stories now combine two to four genres.

**Point.** A blend buys density and surprise: more beats, from systems the audience cannot run in its head at once (the uncertainty term; Anticipation principle 7, break the pattern before it is learned). It also costs. A genre's habits cost nothing only when they are already in this audience's baseline; for an audience that does not know a genre, each of its habits is a departure that costs attention (Implied World principles 1 and 2). *Star Wars* could move fast because each borrowed genre let the audience furnish a world from memory. And beats collide: a Love Story beat that needs the couple alone and unhurried cannot live inside an Action sequence that never lets up.

**Mixing, as decisions:**
1. **Choose the lead genre.** The lead is the genre whose question the ending settles and whose promise the story presses hardest; the others are **guests**. Its defaults dominate, and the lead changes what the same materials mean: with Science Fiction leading and Myth borrowed, the story shows a society being made; with Myth leading, the same materials give a myth of a species growing up (Truby's own pair). **Test** (`hard-to-vary`, flip): flip the outcome of each genre's strand in turn. The flip that most changes what the whole story means marks the lead. If no flip changes it, nothing leads yet, and the ending is settling nothing.
2. **Set the promises and guarantees side by side and find the clashes.** Where two promises conflict, keep the lead's, and drop only a guest genre's promise the story never pressed; a promise pressed and then dropped is betrayal whichever genre it came from (Gap principle 5). Where guarantees conflict, decide which protection the audience keeps. A death the audience sees breaks the comic mood, Truby warns, unless the story is Black Comedy or Crime Comedy *(built in part)*. In owner terms it spends the safety the laughs rest on. Either the story absorbs it as weight (*Four Weddings and a Funeral*), or the laugh turns uneasy and the story becomes Black Comedy. Decide which.
3. **Decide the size of each guest.** A strand that runs through the story, or a single scene. Truby's case: *The Godfather* plays the hospital scene, where Michael realises someone is coming to kill his father, as Horror. In owner terms a borrowed scene climbs the fear ladder and changes who knows what (Anticipation principle 6) inside a story that is otherwise not about fear.
4. **Pair genres that rarely meet**, for freshness (*Inception*: Science Fiction with the Caper) *(fitted from a few hits; odd pairings that failed are rarely heard of, so the evidence leans one way)*, and check each unfamiliar guest against the attention budget.
5. **Use viewpoint.** Characters can believe they are in different genres: one thinks she is in a Love Story, another is investigating a crime. The distance between their genres is a knowledge gap the audience can see, and it makes dramatic irony (Anticipation principle 6).

## 9. Rising above a genre

**Case.** *McCabe & Mrs. Miller* keeps the Western's final gunfight and turns its job over: the town the fight ought to save is busy elsewhere and never sees it, so the beat that usually founds a community shows one with no use for the man who made it. Truby (*The Anatomy of Story*) calls turning a genre's familiar signs over *undercutting* the genre *(built: familiar symbols set up predictions, and turning each one over makes the audience rethink)*. His condition is that the reversals grow from a reversed theme, not from a wish to be different.

**Point.** A beat-perfect genre story is interchangeable with its neighbours *(Truby, asserted)*. He names three routes above it (his *transcending the genre*), restated as jobs:
- **Twist a beat.** Keep its job and change its order, its form, who carries it out or how it comes out. The audience gets what it came for, in a shape it has not met *(built: familiarity dulls attention, and keeping the job keeps the pleasure of recognition)*. In Gap terms, changing when the audience learns of a beat is scheduling (principle 4), while moving the event itself changes the story; in Anticipation terms it breaks a pattern before the audience learns it (principle 7). **The limit**, in the workshop's terms: a twisted beat still counts while it still does its job. If the job goes, it is a broken promise, not a twist.
- **Make the genre's question the story's own.** Let the hero's choices under pressure test the genre's outlook, and let the ending answer it, against the usual answer if the story earns that (section 4).
- **Take apart a field of human life.** Truby treats large human activities (justice, business, religion, politics, the mind) as story forms of their own, each suited to certain genres *(asserted)*. The job: name the one institution the story really dissects, and let its workings, not just its look, decide what characters want and what things cost. Where the field is not an institution (the mind, happiness, the making of a self), pick the institution through which it acts: for a Love Story, the marriage law or the entail. That is Gap principle 2 (the world decides what people want and what things cost) and the Implied World theory's indifference (an institution with business of its own).

**From the owner theories:**
- **Depart from the genre's defaults, not only from the real world.** A world riding on its genre's defaults feels generic because it is (Implied World theory); Truby's twisted beat is the plot-level version of the same move. Choose one departure, ideally one that embodies the story's question (principle 8), follow its consequences (principle 3), and hand its building to `.claude/skills/story-world/SKILL.md`.
- **Break one guarantee, convincingly** (section 2).
- **Surprise that stays true.** Care deepens when a character does something unexpected that was in them all along (Bond principle 7). A twisted beat works the same way: unexpected, and still the genre.

## 10. Where storytelling is heading

Truby's closing forecasts *(asserted, from trends)*: stories the audience steers, stories that mix every genre at once and even switch genre with the viewpoint, and new forms of Myth for present-day problems (his Ecological, Rejuvenation and Female Myths; see `genres/myth.md`). What a writer can use now:
- **Every route keeps its promises.** In a branching story, plan which genre's promises each route carries, and check that each route settles the questions it pressed. A route that raises a mystery and ends before answering it is a betrayal on that route.
- **Guard the schedule.** The Gap theory assumes the audience rebuilds a story from a plot someone else ordered (principle 1). Where the audience chooses the order, the writer no longer fully decides when each fact is learned (principle 4), so a reveal that depends on order has to be reachable only after the facts it needs (the workshop's reading; Truby does not raise it).
- **Genre by viewpoint works in linear stories too** (section 8, decision 5).

## 11. Rivals

- **What matters most.** *Truby:* the beats are the biggest single factor in a genre story's success. *Owner theories:* suspense is a product and care is its first term, so flawless beats around a hero nobody cares about give spectacle, not suspense (Anticipation theory, the suspense equation; Bond theory). *Test:* compare a draft that hits every beat but builds no care before the first threat with one that skips two beats and builds care. Truby predicts the first holds readers better; the theories predict the second.
- **Every beat, or every job.** *Truby:* leave out a classic beat and the genre's fans will not come. *Workshop (Gap principle 5):* what the audience is owed is the promise, and a beat is one way to keep it. *Test:* in a Love Story, cut the first dance and let the couple show they move as one in another shared act. Truby predicts fans reject it; the promise reading predicts they accept it, and reject only a draft where the job is done by nothing.
- **Theme as lesson, or as settlement.** *Truby:* theme is the author's moral stance, and each genre ships a default one. *Gap principle 7:* a theme is the story's answer to the question its world poses. *Test:* end a draft twice, once with a character stating the genre's recipe, once with only the settled gaps to show it. The theory predicts the second lands harder and the first reads as preaching.
- **More genres, or fewer departures.** *Truby:* more genres, more story. *Implied World principle 2:* an unfamiliar genre's habits are not in this audience's baseline, so each is a departure that costs attention. *Test:* blend a familiar genre with one the intended audience does not know (an old subgenre, a form from another tradition). The theory predicts that blend slows and confuses where a familiar pairing does not.
- **Cut what does no structural job, or keep it as surplus.** *Truby:* an element with no structural job is decoration. *Implied World theory:* details with no plot purpose make a world (principle 5), but only particular ones, taken for granted; stock genre elements are defaults, and make a world generic (Part 2 sections 1 and 3). So both views agree a stock trope does nothing, and part over the particular detail. *Test:* in a Western draft, replace the stock saloon with one purposeless detail only this town has, mentioned in passing. Truby predicts no gain, since both are decoration; the theory predicts the town feels more real.
- **Genre first, or premise first.** Truby disagrees with himself. *The Anatomy of Genres* says hit every beat of the genres you choose; *The Anatomy of Story* holds that a story's form should grow from what its premise needs (his *designing principle*), and that a genre fitted on from outside gives a story that could belong to anyone. The owner frame sides with neither alone: genre is a baseline the audience brings, to be used, moved or broken on purpose. *Test:* develop one premise twice, once from the genre's beats and once from what the premise needs, then check the second against the genre's promises. The owner frame predicts it keeps the promises that matter while riding on fewer defaults.
- **A hero-centred spine, or an indifferent world.** Truby's model of change defines the opponent and the world by the hero's goal; Implied World principle 4 wants a world with business of its own. Mild, and the two fit if the world is built apart from the arc. *Test:* if the hero died in chapter one, what would the genre's world be doing next week? If nothing, it is set dressing.
- **Ranking genres.** The ladder (section 7) is Truby's value judgement, and no owner theory ranks genres. No change to a story could tell the two views apart, which is why the ladder stays out of the procedure.

## 12. Traps

- **Treating the beats as a checklist.** They are fitted from past stories. A beat present but doing no job is worse than one absent whose job is done elsewhere.
- **Curing "generic" with more genre.** Every added default makes it more generic. The cure is a departure the story owns.
- **Calling a broken promise a twist.** A twist keeps the beat's job. A pressed question left open, an unfair solution, a monster breaking its own rules: those are betrayals.
- **Breaking every guarantee.** If characters die all the time, care collapses, and suspense with it. Break one convincingly, so that every threat is real, not all of them.
- **Preaching the recipe.** Truby's recipes are default answers to test, not morals to state.
- **Blending for its own sake.** Each guest genre costs attention and brings promises the story then owes.
- **Leaving the lead unchosen.** The ending then settles no single question, and the story reads like several drafts at once.
- **Writing "horror" for the rung.** Horror is the genre; *the horror rung* is the top step of the fear ladder.
