# Crime

*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Genres, chapter 7, in the workshop's own words; framed by the owner theories (Gap theory principles 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7 and its fourth writer's question; Anticipation principles 1, 3, 5, 6 and 7 and the uncertainty term; Implied World principles 1, 3 and 8; Bond theory's levers, principles 2, 5, 6 and 7, and the limits of care).*

Contents: 1 In one line. 2 The promise. 3 The baseline. 4 Guarantees, and which to break. 5 Hero and opponent. 6 The beats, as jobs. 7 The genre's question. 8 Where the care comes from. 9 Suspense and knowledge. 10 Subgenres and mixes. 11 Twisting it. 12 Examples. 13 Rivals and doubts.

## 1. In one line

Crime sets a lawbreaker who believes the rules are not for him against someone who stands for them, and keeps the books: every wrong is a debt, and the ending shows how it was paid, and by whom.

Words kept apart in this file: *Crime*, with a capital, is the genre; *the crime* is the act that starts the story. The *criminal* is the lawbreaker; the *pursuer* is whoever hunts him (Truby's *master cop*, who may be a police officer, an investigator, a crime boss or an ordinary person). Either can be the hero, and the other is then the opponent. The *ledger* is Truby's *moral accounting*: the running tally of what each person owes and is owed, where every harm done to another is a *debt* that calls for *payment*. A *code* is a person's own rules about what is worth wanting and which means are allowed. *Settlement* keeps the Gap theory's meaning: how the ending settles the story's gaps, which close, which stay open, and what has changed at what cost; in Crime much of it is which debts are paid.

## 2. The promise

From Truby's account the audience comes for *(mostly fitted)*: **a crime worth watching**, one that shows this criminal's particular mind; **a pursuer who looks like less than he is**; **a long fight between equals**, move answered by countermove; **deception on both sides**, with the criminal's tricks exposed late; **the line crossed**, the pursuer coming to resemble his quarry; **hard choices** between two wrongs; and **a reckoning** in which the debts are paid, at best in a coin shaped to the crime.

In Gap theory terms the crime is the breach (principle 2: a departure from the world's own normal), and the world's law and sense of fairness decide what counts as one. The question the story presses is about the future: *will he be caught, and at what price?* That is suspense, an open question about what will happen, not curiosity, an open question about what already did, and it is the quickest way to tell Crime from its nearest neighbour. **Test** *(fitted)*: read down the middle scenes. If most are about finding out who did it, Detective leads (`detective-and-thriller.md`); if most are moves between a known criminal and a pursuer, Crime does. The ledger is a promise too: once the story has made a debt loud, paying it, or showing why it cannot be paid, is a mystery the story owes (Gap principle 5). Dropping it reads as betrayal.

## 3. The baseline

The *baseline* (Implied World theory) is what the audience assumes by default: the real world plus the genre's habits. Before being told anything, a Crime audience assumes *(fitted)*: **a society with laws, police and courts** that roughly track right and wrong, but not exactly; **a split between rich and poor**, with the honest way up mostly closed, so that crime reads as a response to blocked wants rather than random wickedness; **people living shallow lives**, desperate or unreflecting, in a world somewhere between anything-goes (ruled by whoever has money and guns) and a police state (where nearly every want is banned); and **a culprit known early, a pursuer who bends the rules, and a last gunfight somewhere grim**.

**Crime spends its attention on a moral departure** (the workshop's reading). It rarely needs a physical one. The audience's own morality is most of the baseline (Implied World principle 1), so the writer spends only where the story's moral order differs from it: a code the audience does not share, or a law that has come loose from what is right (*Antigone*; *Les Misérables*, where the distance between the two breaks the pursuer, Javert).

**Case.** In *In Bruges* professional killers live by a code in which a paid killing is business and the target is assumed to have earned it. So the story's real crime is not the hit on a priest but a boy killed by accident, a debt the code cannot absorb. The world decides what counts as an event (Gap principle 2), and one rule about bystanders is followed through all its consequences (Implied World principle 3, ripple).

**Point.** Build the moral order before breaking it *(built: a rule must be on the page before breaking it can register)*. Truby's first job for the writer, restated: lay out the culture's rules, including how it decides who is to blame (the person, the family, fate, the state), so that the hero's own code, and the place it gives way, have something to stand against (section 6). A draft whose crime could happen in any city, whose pursuer bends the usual rules and whose climax is a shootout in a disused factory is riding on the genre's defaults. Find its moral departure, and hand the world to `story-world`: `.claude/skills/story-world/references/diagnosing-a-world.md`, section 3, to confirm it is generic, and the Building steps 2, 4 and 6 of `.claude/skills/story-world/SKILL.md` to build the departure.

## 4. Guarantees, and which to break

A *guarantee* is a protection the audience assumes the genre gives without being told; while it trusts one, some part of it relaxes. Anticipation principle 5 (broken contract): break one early and convincingly and every later threat is real; break all of them and care collapses, and suspense with it. Crime's guarantees protect the people on the law's side. Truby does not use the word; the table is the workshop's, drawn from his endings and examples.

| Guarantee the audience leans on | Broken by | What breaking it buys |
|---|---|---|
| The pursuer survives | *The Departed*: the captain running the undercover man is killed before the last act | Nobody on the law's side is safe; every later meeting carries real threat |
| Those the pursuer loves are safe | *The Dark Knight*: the woman loved by both the hero and the district attorney is killed | Later threats to anyone close to the hero are believed (but see the fridging test, section 13) |
| The moral centre survives | *In Bruges* | The code's price lands on the one who questioned it, and in the chase that follows nobody is safe |

- **Expectations, not guarantees.** Four things a Crime audience may expect look like guarantees but protect nobody. That the criminal is caught is the genre's usual answer to its expected mystery (section 2), not a protection for anyone: what is owed is some payment, and a thief who beats the law while the payment comes in another coin is a twist (section 11). That the legal and the right coincide is not even the baseline, which says only that they roughly do (section 3); pulling them apart is the genre's usual moral departure (*Antigone*, *Les Misérables*). That the truth comes out is not assured either: a society kept going by a lie is one of the three usual verdicts (section 6), as in *The Dark Knight* and *The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance*. That the teller is innocent is an expectation about the telling (section 9; *Atonement*). By the cheated-or-unsafe test (`.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`, section 2), overturning any of them leaves the audience rereading, not unsafe: a twist on how the promise is kept, not a broken contract.
- **The slide is a promise, not a break.** The pursuer crossing the line is part of what the audience comes for (section 2), and a criminal hero's partial right (section 5) is the genre's usual design; neither spends a guarantee. The slide is now so expected that audiences wait for it; Truby calls predictability the genre's biggest plotting problem *(built)*. A pattern must break before the audience learns it (Anticipation principle 7): hide *which* line he will cross, or have him refuse one (the workshop's suggestion).
- **Which one to break.** One, convincingly, and preferably the guarantee the story's question is about (the workshop's suggestion, after Implied World principle 8): *In Bruges* spends the one who questioned the code. Choose the place: an early break makes later threats real (*The Departed*), a late one gives the ending weight (how-genres-work, section 2). Test by restoring it (the captain lives): if the story loses nothing, the break was doing no work.

## 5. Hero and opponent

**One person on both sides of a line** *(asserted, and a good design prompt)*. Either criminal or pursuer can lead, and whichever does not is the other's reflection across the law. **Test:** write a paragraph on how your pursuer would commit this crime and one on how your criminal would catch himself. If they read like two species, pull them closer.

**The pursuer.** Ordinary to look at and a master of the job *(fitted, from Porfiry in Crime and Punishment through Columbo to the police chief in Fargo)*: an outsider in the society he serves who believes in its rules more than it does, and whose worth shows in doing the work well and straight *(built, from the contrast with a criminal who thinks his gifts exempt him)*. His flaw: loneliness, an obsession that carries him over the line, and a code split against itself *(built)*: the law is sacred, yet officers who trust each other with their lives keep quiet for a colleague who breaks it. His need is moral: to reaffirm the society's worth, and beyond that to make justice where the law does not reach. What he wants: to bring the criminal in, a goal set only after the crime *(fitted, as in Horror)*, with the culprit usually known early, so the weight falls on the contest.

**The criminal.** **Test:** write his private justification as one sentence beginning "People like me..."; if it claims no special status, strengthen it. That sentence is Truby's *super criminal* in small: a gift for beating the system, and the belief that the gift sets him above the rules the rest obey. Truby doubts that thieves keep faith with each other, since a criminal by definition serves himself at others' cost *(asserted)*; the gang's code of silence is another matter (`gangster.md`).

As **hero**, give him a partial right to break the law *(built)*: a hungry family, an unjust law, a stolen share. It works on allegiance: an audience already aligned with him (Bond principle 2) now has a reason of its own to want him to win, and the pursuer's code divides, so that he hunts a man he half hopes will escape (Valjean and Javert; the investigator who falls for Thomas Crown). His flaw: a false sense of superiority, a home-made code with a clause that exempts himself, or a code walled into compartments so the crimes never touch his picture of himself (Walter White). His need: to see others as equals, to confess, or to atone.

As **opponent**, the strongest version is a thinker with a thesis about human nature who designs his attacks as experiments, each forcing the hero to choose between two wrongs, each harder than the last *(built: every attack then tests the hero's values, so plot and theme move together; reading the Joker this way is Truby's interpretation)*. In Black Comedy the police may be all but absent, and someone else, such as the boss in *In Bruges*, does the enforcing. For building either, see `.claude/skills/character/references/opponents.md`.

## 6. The beats, as jobs

The beats are fitted, drawn from past stories: a missing beat is a question, not a fault. Ask what job it does, and whether the draft gets that job done some other way. Names and grouping are the workshop's.

| Beat | The job it does | If the draft lacks it, ask... |
|---|---|---|
| The two-faced city | Shows a society split by money with the honest way up closed, so the crime answers the world | Why does this person turn to crime here, and which honest route was closed to them? |
| The signature crime | Lifts the criminal to new wealth or power and shows his particular mind (Truby's *Ingenious Crime*) | Could anyone have planned it? What in it could only this criminal have thought of? |
| The codes | Sets out what each side lives by, with the split inside each that the story will test | What rule does each side hold, and where do its two halves pull apart? |
| The goal after the blow | The pursuer's goal starts with the crime, and he knows the culprit early | When does he know who it is? If late, is Detective leading? |
| Blow for blow | The middle as a contest between equals, each winning rounds (Truby's *cat and mouse*) | Who wins each exchange? If one side wins them all, the fight is not even |
| The slide | Obsession carries the pursuer over the line until he resembles his quarry (Truby's *Obsessive Cop*) | Where does he first break a rule, where a bigger one, and who names the likeness? |
| Hidden campaigns | Each side disguises where, how and why it strikes, and above all its overall plan (Truby's *Deceptive Attack*) | Did the audience see this attack coming? Can its place, method or purpose be hidden while the rules stay clear? |
| The snowball | One wrong act forces the next, so the moral argument moves by cause and effect, not by speeches | Does each wrong answer the consequences of the last? |
| The criminal's win | The pursuer's low point: the criminal escapes or scores big | What does it cost the pursuer, and does it mean something for whoever pays? |
| Closing in | Space, time and allies shrink before the end; often a chase or a worse dilemma | What narrows around the hero before the last clash? |
| The reckoning | The last clash, or in higher forms a late unmasking of the criminal's tricks | Is the showdown in a stock place? Could a reveal do the same job? |
| The verdict on society | The pursuer reaffirms law and shared life, or society loses, or it is kept going by a lie | Which of the three, and is the hero's cost visible? |
| Payment in kind | The debt paid in a coin shaped to the crime, often by the wrongdoer's own hand (Truby's *poetic justice*) | Could the villain's own method, rule or choice bring him down? |

**The higher forms add jobs for the hero's code** *(built)*, which Truby places at three points:
- **Stated early and plainly**, so the ending can hold the character to it. *L.A. Confidential* sets out a young detective's code in an early scene; the boss in *In Bruges* makes a vow the ending collects.
- **Strained in the middle.** When the hero is losing badly, offer a wrong act that would win; a code never offered that trade is untested. An ally who shares the goal attacks the means, and guilt attacks from inside.
- **Decided at the end.** Squeeze the conflict into one choice between two ways of living (Truby's *Final Decision*). **Test** *(built)*: if either option would cost the chooser nothing, or touch nobody else, it is not yet a moral choice. Just before it, clear away the outside reasons to choose rightly, so the choice is plainly the hero's own: in *Crime and Punishment* another man confesses, the one witness to Raskolnikov's guilt is dead, and he gives himself up anyway.

**The chain that holds them:** a wrong that lifts the criminal, a pursuer who knows him early, a contest between equals that drags the pursuer across the line, and a payment shaped to the wrong *(fitted)*. Cut a link and the ending reads as luck.

## 7. The genre's question

Truby's picture of life in Crime *(asserted)*, in gist: moral philosophy told as story, with the ledger at its centre, and the law as the share of the ledger the state enforces, close to the moral share but never the same, so the legal and the right can part. His key question, in the workshop's shape: **what, if anything, would exempt someone from the rules everyone else keeps?**

His *thematic recipe* (a genre's plan for living well) reads differently from each side. For the pursuer: keep the shared rules and enforce them, then go further and make justice where the law does not reach, including for people whose ledgers were unbalanced from birth. For the criminal: break rules that hold your particular gifts down, most of all when the law has lost its moral ground, but beware the belief that you are a higher being, the road to tragedy or Black Comedy. **As a question for a draft:** where do the legal and the right part company, which does the hero choose, what do the hero's methods cost, and who pays?

**The ledger as a tool** *(built, but loose: almost any exchange can be called a debt, so it can explain any ending after the fact)*. Use it to plan, not to predict. For each main character, two columns: what they believe they are owed, and what they come to owe. Walter White believes he is owed a share of a fortune, recognition, and a way to provide for his family before he dies; what he comes to owe runs from the harm his product does, through a death he could have stopped, to many more. The second column should grow, and one rationalisation should wall it off from the first. The settlement (Gap principle 7) then has a concrete job: decide which entries are paid, which stay open, and whether the legal and moral ledgers close the same way. They need not: a court can close the legal account and leave the victim still owed.

## 8. Where the care comes from

Care is the first term of the suspense equation and is built before the threat arrives.
- **The pursuer** runs on **Competence** (the skill the plot keeps proving), **Decency** (he plays fair and believes in the rules), **Specificity** (the rumpled coat, the pregnant small-town chief) and **Vulnerability** from low status. **Connection** runs low: he does not fit in, and the society he protects does not thank him. Rather than have others praise him, show him being fair to someone who cannot help him (Bond principle 5).
- **The criminal hero** runs on **Competence** (the signature crime is the pleasure of watching skill), **Desire**, a partial right standing in for **Decency**, and above all **Interiority**: Raskolnikov's guilt, Walt's rationalisations. Alignment beats virtue (Bond principle 2): we tend to side with the one whose mind we share, often whatever he has done.
- **Start ordinary** *(built)*. *Breaking Bad* opens on a mild teacher with none of the genre's markers of a criminal, so the audience cannot file him as a type and is already aligned before the first crime.
- **The one who judges.** An ally or loved one who sees what the hero is doing (Sonya in *Crime and Punishment*, the partner in *In Bruges*) teaches the audience how to weigh him (Bond principle 5).
- **The opponent's** Competence, wit and philosophy make him a person rather than a function, so the audience can come to care what happens to him (Bond principle 6).

**The genre's central risk** is the Bond theory's limit: some acts end allegiance for most audiences, and time and interiority only stretch the line. Truby reports the *Breaking Bad* writers debating how far their hero could fall before viewers left him, and calls that perhaps the central question of any great series; the Bond theory uses Walt as its limit case. **Method:** list the hero's moral decisions in order, each worse (or better) than the last; mark the one most likely to lose the audience; decide whether to cross it, and what will hold them if you do: time spent with him, and the Interiority and Connection levers.

## 9. Suspense and knowledge

**The equation.** Suspense = Care × Threat × Uncertainty × Time. Crime gets its Threat from the opponent's skill (the criminal's brilliance, or the pursuer's), its Time from the pursuit, and its Uncertainty from equal opponents *(built)*: when each side wins rounds, every exchange stays in doubt. The sweet spot holds: suspense climbs as the hero's odds fall, and collapses once hope is gone.

**Knowledge position.** Crime usually lets the audience know who did it early. The audience is then level with or ahead of the pursuer, and knowing who did it swaps curiosity, an open question about the past, for suspense, an open question about the future. When the ending is as sure as it is in *Columbo*, which shows the murder first, uncertainty moves from *whether* to *how* (Anticipation, Part 2 section 1), and the show is tense anyway. With a criminal hero the audience is usually level with him and fears *with* him; when it sees the pursuer closing in before he does, it fears *for* him. Black Comedy puts the audience ahead of characters with fierce wants and too little knowledge, who walk into mistake after mistake: the suspense of helplessness, played for grim laughs (*Fargo*, *Parasite*).

**Moral suspense** (the workshop's extension). When the hero is losing and a wrong act would win, *will the hero cross the line?* is a suspense question, and care is its multiplier: the more the audience cares, the more the offer frightens.

**Show the rules, hide the plan.** Truby's cure for predictability is deception: the more the criminal deceives, the better *(asserted; the rival is recorded in full in `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 8, with Crime's test in section 13 below)*. The Anticipation theory sets a limit: the audience needs enough of a threat's rules to predict danger (principle 3). Keep each side's means and limits clear, and hide the plan. Plan the reveals before drafting, each exposing one part of the opponent's single hidden campaign (Truby's *Final Reveal*) *(built)*; that is also how a writer avoids holding something back only for a twist (the Gap theory's fourth writer's question).

**The criminal as teller.** In *The Usual Suspects* the criminal narrates: he improvises his account from what is in the room, poses as a weak ally, and invents a feared rival to hide behind. A teller who hides what he knows is cheating unless the hiding is part of who he is (Gap principle 6); here the lying is the character, so it is fair. **Test:** list the covers your criminal uses (a false story, a false role, a false self, a false culprit); each is a reveal you can spend later. *Atonement* turns the move round: the teller is the one who owes the largest debt, and the late reveal moves the moral weight of everything before it.

**The ratchet.** Blow for blow gives rises and partial releases; the snowball tightens the bind step by step. Both become rhythms the audience learns, so break the pattern before it does (Anticipation principle 7). More: `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`, `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md` and `.claude/skills/plot/references/telling-and-perspective.md`.

## 10. Subgenres and mixes

**Subgenres Truby lists:** Film Noir, Caper (Heist, shared with Action), Courtroom Drama, Social Drama, Prison Escape, Criminal as Hero, Crime Comedy, Crime Fantasy and Fish out of Water, plus the two higher forms in section 11. Social Drama keeps to one line when it has one hero (*Erin Brockovich*) and branches into strands when it has several (*Traffic*, *Crash*) *(fitted)*.

**Neighbours by contrast.** Crime takes Detective's known wrong and Action's contest *(asserted)*. **Detective** is the search for the truth, with guilt decided only at the end; if the search takes over the middle, Detective leads (`detective-and-thriller.md`). **Action** sides with the individual rising above others, Crime with a society of equals where nobody may exploit the rest *(asserted)*; if the hero is admired mainly for rising, Action leads (`action.md`). **Gangster** shares the family and makes the organisation and its silence the subject (`gangster.md`). Truby hands on to **Comedy**: Crime judges ends and means but says little about everyday conduct among people (`comedy.md`).

**Mixes named:** with Myth and Fantasy (*The Dark Knight*); Thriller (*The Silence of the Lambs*, *Strangers on a Train*); Coming-of-Age (*Trainspotting*); the anti-Western (*No Country for Old Men*, *Hell or High Water*); Gangster (*The Departed*); Horror (*Diabolique*); Comedy (*Beverly Hills Cop*); Love (*The Thomas Crown Affair*, *Atonement*); the police procedural (*The Wire*). **Family drama** has fed Crime since Cain and Abel *(fitted from one myth)*: ask whose approval, love or share the criminal thinks is being taken from him, and whether his grievance is aimed at its real source or at a substitute. On which genre leads a mix: `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`.

## 11. Twisting it

Truby's general routes above a genre are in `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`. For Crime, the larger form of life is justice: right, wrong and what is owed. His chapter's own moves, restated:
- **Build the moral universe; do not borrow it.** Truby's *moral universe* is one in which every act between people carries moral weight, whether or not anyone behaves well. For a series or long novel, his *Never-Ending Moral Challenge* in gist: an arena harsh enough to keep every character under moral pressure, with the tests ordered so each costs more. In a serial the season, not the episode, is the unit *(built, from Breaking Bad)*.
- **Let the law lose, and keep the payment** (the workshop's reading). The criminal caught is the genre's usual answer, not a guarantee (section 4). *The Thomas Crown Affair* lets the thief win: the legal ledger fails, the moral one does not, and the question moves from whether he pays to what winning cost him, since he ends alone.
- **Widen the ledger to a lifetime.** The simple Crime story accounts for one act. The higher one uses that act as a trigger and pays out what the hero owed and was owed across a whole life.
- **Take it to tragedy.** In Truby's *Epic Crime Tragedy* a gifted person ruins himself in the effort to show he is exceptional, and the society's moral order resists change. *Crime and Punishment* reverses the usual slide: the pursuer barely investigates, and guilt does the pursuer's work, carrying the killer from the crime to confession. Truby holds that guilt never pays a debt off *(built, though stated without evidence)*: if the hero punishes himself, consider withholding outside punishment and letting guilt do the work. *Atonement* fits the tragedy to an ordinary person: one lie spreads and ruins three lives, and some debts cannot be paid.
- **Take it to Black Comedy.** In Truby's *Crime Black Comedy* a code works as a destructive system: fierce wants plus ignorance give a chain of small causes running downhill to a payment the audience senses coming (*Parasite*, *Fargo*). *In Bruges* is his model: an upside-down but consistent code, a vow stated early, and a boss who, believing he has done the one thing he swore he would die for, keeps his word.

**One idea from the owner theories.** Make the world's main departure a law that has come loose from what is right (Implied World principle 8), then follow its ripples into ordinary scenes (principle 3): who informs, who profits, who is quietly exempt. The story's question then lives in the world, and the criminal hero's case is made by the world rather than by a speech. Test with a swap: replace the unjust law with plain greed as his motive. If the story's question does not change, the law was not yet doing its job.

## 12. Examples

- *Crime and Punishment*: a known killer; a plain investigator who wins the argument; guilt doing the pursuer's work; the right choice left easy to avoid.
- *The Dark Knight*: a signature crime; an opponent with a thesis and escalating dilemmas; society kept going by a lie.
- *The Usual Suspects*: the criminal as teller; stacked covers; the unmasking in the last minutes.
- *In Bruges*: a consistent upside-down code; a vow stated early and kept; payment in kind.
- *Breaking Bad*: an ordinary start; a ledger across a life; moral decisions ordered season by season.
- *Columbo* and *Fargo*: the plain-looking master pursuer; the culprit known, the tension in how; in *Fargo*, the chief's quiet bafflement that anyone would kill for so little.
- *Les Misérables* and *The Thomas Crown Affair*: a criminal hero partly in the right; a pursuer split in two; the thief who wins and ends alone.
- *Atonement* and *Parasite*: the teller owes the debt, tragedy for an ordinary person; Black Comedy's chain of small causes against a corrupt class order.
- *L.A. Confidential*: a code set out in an early scene. *Antigone*: the legal against the right, paid for with a life. *Hamlet*: schemers undone by their own scheme.

## 13. Rivals and doubts

- **More deception, or clear rules.** Truby's every added deception improves a Crime story, against the theories' limit on what is hidden from the audience (the two sides deceiving each other is not limited); recorded in full in `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 8. *Test for Crime:* hide the criminal's plan but keep his means and limits clear; then hide both. The theory predicts suspense in the first and, once the reveal lands, a sense of arbitrariness in the second; Truby predicts the second plays better.
- **The later the reveal, the better.** Truby ranks lateness. The Gap theory treats early and late as a trade (the bomb under the table; Ishiguro telling early on purpose). *Test:* move the main reveal to the middle and tell the audience before the pursuer. The theory predicts that surprise turns into suspense about what he will do with it, not a loss; Truby predicts a weaker story.
- **A loved one killed to mark the criminal's win.** Truby's example of the low point, from *The Dark Knight*, kills the hero's beloved. The Bond theory's limit (the bond is a promise) warns against killing a character only to move another, the abuse Gail Simone named fridging. *Test:* cut the scenes in which she wants something of her own. If her death works exactly as well, it was only moving him.
- **Payment will come.** Truby's lifetime ledger promises payment, timing unknown. Taken as a law it can explain any ending, while the Gap theory's settlement is specific. *Test:* before writing the ending, write down which debts will be paid and how. If any ending you could write would count as payment, the ledger is doing no work.
- **The ferries.** Truby calls the ending of *The Dark Knight* false to the view of people the film had built, and the workshop reads the theories the same way: the world decides what counts as an event and what it costs (Gap principle 2), so an ending should follow from what the world has shown. But Bond principle 7 allows a surprise that was in them all along, so the dispute is over preparation. *Test:* plant one earlier act of restraint by ordinary citizens; the theory predicts the ending then reads as earned, while Truby's objection, resting on his view of human nature, would stand.
- **Doubts.** Truby treats fate and destiny as excuses for dodging responsibility; the owner theories let a world depart from ours on agency too, if its rules are clear (Implied World principle 1), so use a character's appeal to fate as a flaw the story can expose, not as a rule for every story. The beats are fitted mostly from English-language film and television and a few classics. That Crime ranks society above the individual more than any other genre, and Crime's place on Truby's ladder between Science Fiction and Comedy, are asserted value judgements. Reading the Joker as a philosopher is interpretation. Truby's verdicts on *Crime and Punishment* (a weak premise, too light a sentence) are his moral views, not craft rules.
