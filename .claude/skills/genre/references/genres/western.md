# Western

*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Genres, chapter 9, in the workshop's own words; framed by the owner theories (Implied World: the baseline and principles 3, 4, 5 and 8; Gap principles 2, 3, 4, 5 and 7 and the knowledge and perspective gaps; Anticipation principles 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7; Bond levers, chiefly Competence, Decency, Connection, Vulnerability and Flaw, the Miss rung, principles 4 to 7 and the limits of care).*

Contents: 1 In one line. 2 The promise. 3 The baseline. 4 Guarantees, and which to break. 5 Hero and opponent. 6 The beats, as jobs. 7 The genre's question. 8 Where the care comes from. 9 Suspense and knowledge. 10 Subgenres and mixes. 11 Twisting it. 12 Examples. 13 Rivals and doubts.

## 1. In one line

A fighter from the wild uses his gun so that others can make a home, and cannot share the home he made possible.

Truby's model is *Shane*, and his image for it is the *Warrior Moses*: a leader who brings his people to the border of the promised land and is never let in *(fitted, from one film treated as the pattern)*. His larger claim *(asserted)*: the Western is a philosophy of history in story form, a founding myth that squeezes a society's growth, from wild land to town, into one place and one fight. Its higher form, the *anti-Western* (his term), shows the same society closing in on the man who made it possible.

Words kept apart in this file: the **protector** is the Western hero (Truby's *cowboy*, at his best the *supercowboy*); the **builders** are the farmers and townspeople he protects (Truby's *secondary hero*); the **outlaw** is the classic opponent (Truby's *bad cowboy*); the **frontier** is the line between wild land and settled society; the **town** is the young community on it; the **code** is the protector's rules for living (Truby's *Code of the West*); the **showdown** is the public gunfight that ends the story.

## 2. The promise

From Truby's account the audience comes for *(fitted, mostly from Hollywood films of the late 1930s to the early 1970s)*: **open land**, and the belief that going west lets a person start again; **a master of the gun who holds back**, skill reined in by a code; **a town worth saving**, new, easily broken and joyful in its way; **two men of one kind**, a good gunfighter and a bad one with the same skills, so the only difference is what each uses them for; **fights that grow**, from fists to guns; and above all **the showdown**, provoked in public, stretched out in time and decided in an instant, with the town's future hanging on it. Truby ranks it the genre's most important step *(asserted, well supported by his examples)*. The end is **bittersweet**: the town saved, the protector riding away. The anti-Western promises an elegy instead, the passing of these men and their world, funny and heartbreaking at once.

**Promise or guarantee.** By the cheated-or-unsafe test (`.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`, section 2), a reckoning with the opponent, in person and in public, is a promise: let the outlaw die offstage or by accident and the audience feels cheated. That the good man *wins* it, fairly, is a guarantee, a protection the audience assumes for the man it has come to care about (section 4). A *breach*, in Gap terms, is what breaks a world's normal: here the outlaw breaks the town's fragile normal, which sets the stakes. The cost comes from somewhere else *(the workshop's reading)*. A character, in the Gap theory, is a desire the world has shaped, chasing something the world resists: the protector wants the open wild, and the town he saves is the settled life that is closing it, so each fight he wins for the builders takes him further from what he wants. In the anti-Western the world's normal itself is changing, the frontier closing, and the settlement shows what that cost him (Gap principle 7). The question pressed hardest, a mystery the story owes an answer to (Gap principle 5), is whether he will stand, and what standing will cost him.

## 3. The baseline

The *baseline* (Implied World theory) is what the audience assumes by default, the real world plus the habits of the genre; a *departure* is any way the story's world differs from it. Unless told otherwise, a Western audience assumes *(fitted, from Truby's beats)*:
- **open country on the frontier**, rich enough to farm and wide enough to feel free, and **a newborn town** with only the first buildings a society needs, a saloon and a jail among them;
- **weak law**, so personal force settles disputes, a gun on every hip, and a public test of manhood (Truby's *shame culture*): a man judged in the open by his skill, who cannot turn down a challenge without being branded a coward;
- **white hats and black hats**, the rule that the good man never draws first, and ranchers against farmers, or builders against the peoples already on the land;
- **the late nineteenth century**: the 1870s and 1880s for the classic form, the 1890s for the anti-Western.

**The frontier is the genre's main default.** Measured against the audience's own world alone it would be a departure (in the Gap theory's sense, the departure gap between reality and the world), but once the genre is named the audience brings it for free, so it is baseline. Truby reads it as a border in time as well as space, between two stages of society *(built: people caught between stages are pushed by the change and push it along, so everyone is under strain, which is where a world shows itself, Gap principle 3)*. So a classic Western spends almost nothing on departures of its own, which is why the whole kit can be moved to a desert planet (section 10), and why it goes generic so easily. The genre's question is built into that default: open land that invites freedom and can be claimed only by force. Implied World principle 8 asks for more, a departure the story owns that carries the question further: the fence, a water right, a survey line, *McCabe*'s wet forest (the test below). His ladder of stages, wild land, village, city and the city that oppresses, is a quick map *(asserted, useful)*: place the story on it, name the change it straddles, and let the protector belong to the stage that is passing.

**Land, people, tools.** Truby builds every genre world from its land, its social order and its tools *(asserted, a checklist)*. In the Western each carries the theme. The **fence** is his best case *(built)*: a few rails turn shared land into property, property brings gaps in wealth, and barbed wire stops the cattle drives, so ranchers fight farmers; one small change with widening consequences works like Implied World principle 3 (ripple) in miniature. The **gun** follows from weak law *(built)*: it decides a claim at once and all or nothing, and it marks the man who uses it, one reason he never stays. His readings of the horse, hat and badge are *asserted*.

**Generic Westerns ride on these defaults.** If the town could be swapped for any Western town and the outlaw for any black hat, the draft is using the furniture. **Test:** find the object that marks the change from shared to owned, and let it start a fight; if there is none, the frontier is scenery. For a departure of the story's own, hand to `story-world` (`.claude/skills/story-world/references/diagnosing-a-world.md`, section 3). *McCabe & Mrs. Miller* moves the town into wet forest, and the audience knows at once this is not the usual West. Today's audience also brings what it knows of the real West (section 13).

## 4. Guarantees, and which to break

A *guarantee* is a protection the audience assumes the genre gives: here, that the protector, the man it cares about, has the skill and the backing to win a fair fight. Break one early and convincingly and every later threat is real; break them all and care collapses, and suspense with it (Anticipation principle 5). Truby does not discuss guarantees directly; the table is the workshop's, drawn from his beats and examples.

| Guarantee the audience leans on | Broken by | What breaking it buys |
|---|---|---|
| The town stands behind its protector | *High Noon* | He faces the threat alone; care moves from the town to him |
| The fight is fair, and the good man can win it (the hero coming through: an expected ending kept as a protection for him) | *The Wild Bunch* | Force no skill can beat: he was doomed from the start |
| The protector's skill with the gun is real | *McCabe & Mrs. Miller*: the gunfighter's reputation is rumour | Force may not answer; every threat to him is real |

- **The cheated-or-unsafe test, applied** (`.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`, section 2). That the protector holds his skill back is a promise (section 2): drop the restraint and the audience feels cheated of the code. That the skill is real is a guarantee: take it away and the audience feels unsafe for him. *Destry Rides Again* twists the promise rather than breaking the guarantee: a crack shot who will not wear a gun, restraint pushed as far as it goes.
- **Expectations, not guarantees.** Two things the classic form leads the audience to expect protect nobody: that the town, and the man who saves it, are good (*High Plains Drifter*: a stranger who is no saint exposes the town's hidden rot, and allegiance is tested), and that dying for the code buys something (*3:10 to Yuma*, below). Overturning either leaves the audience rereading, not unsafe: a twist on how the promise is kept, answering the genre's question (section 7) against the usual answer, and it sits with the anti-Western's reversals (section 11).
- **Keep the code's rules as rules: a promise, not a guarantee.** The rule that the good man never draws first works like a monster's rules in Horror: stated, tested and kept, as a mystery is (Anticipation theory, Part 2 section 3; Gap principle 5). The protector may break it, but as a choice made on screen, at a price (the beat *The draw*, section 6). Broken for the plot's convenience, it is a broken promise, not a twist.
- **Count what a death buys.** In *3:10 to Yuma* a father takes on a deadly job so his son will stop thinking him a coward, loses his allies until he stands alone, and the prisoner will probably get away anyway. Truby calls this a false moral accounting and blames it for the classic form's decline *(built for the case; the historical blame is asserted)*. **Test:** if the protector dies for his code, what does his death buy? If only a watcher's admiration, the story is a critique of the code, not a celebration; know which you are writing.
- **Which one to break.** One, early and convincingly, and preferably the one the genre's question is about (the workshop's suggestion, after Implied World principle 8). The Western asks what making a home for others costs the one who makes it possible, so the sharpest break is the town's support. *High Noon* breaks it scene by scene: each citizen who refuses makes the showdown more real. Test by removal: restore the town's support, and if the showdown loses nothing, the break did no work. (The anti-Western breaks nearly all of them at once; section 13.)

## 5. Hero and opponent

**Hero: the protector** *(fitted)*. He does not come west to build a home; the builders do that, and he comes to get away from people (Truby counts the confusion as the genre's great mistake). At his best he is a **lawman** with a gunfighter's nerve (Wyatt Earp in *My Darling Clementine*) or a **lone gunman** ruled by hard-won self-control (Shane), slow to use his power and impossible to bribe *(Truby dresses this in labels from Aristotle and Nietzsche that add flavour, not mechanism)*. Often he is the visiting helper (Truby's *Traveling Angel*, a form shared with Detective and Comedy; `comedy.md`, `fantasy.md`): a near-flawless outsider who arrives in a troubled town, mends the people in it and rides on. **Two heroes** *(built)*: the genre most about how a society grows has the most solitary hero of all, and Truby resolves the oddity with two heroes, the protector who fights and the builder who builds. **Test:** their goals should depend on each other while their fates part. If the protector could happily settle down at the end, the Western's core irony is gone.

**Flaw, need and desire.** The flaw is the shadow of his strength *(built)*, and its job is to set the price paid at the doorway: the freedom that makes him fearless also keeps him out of love, and so out of the town he saves. The shame culture (section 3) closes the trap *(built: a town too new to survive a shirker enforces courage by public judgement)*: with the town always watching, refusing a fight is not open to him. The need he meets, before the showdown, is to accept that he must kill to protect others; the need he never meets is to belong. His desire is doubled *(built)*: privately, to escape settled life; in the story, to protect the builders, with a particular goal each time that only fighting can reach *(asserted, a practical rule)*. **Test:** write the two desire lines, his and the builders'. They should clash in values and work together in plot.

**Opponent** *(fitted, from the genre's history)*. In early Westerns, native peoples, drawn through the builders' prejudice as a faceless Other; Truby marks this as the settlers' view, not the truth. Later and more central, the **outlaw**, often a cattleman who sees fences as the end of freedom. At his best he is the protector's **double** *(built)*: the same skills, the same life lived through the body, and the only difference is whether he uses the gun to lead or to dominate (*Red River*). He is defined by the bargain he refuses *(built, the chapter's tightest mechanism)*: in a boomtown with next to no law, most settlers give up the chance of a quick fortune for rules that let them keep what they build, and the outlaw refuses the trade. Truby calls the drift he serves *social entropy*, a community's slide into lawless grabbing. Make him a *nemesis* (Truby's term), the opponent best placed to hit this hero's worst flaw, here the shame trap and the reluctance to kill, and as strong as the protector is. In the anti-Western the outlaw shrinks to a hired hand, and the real opponent is a company boss, often never seen (section 11). For building either figure, see `.claude/skills/character/references/opponents.md`.

## 6. The beats, as jobs

The beats are fitted, drawn from past stories: a missing beat is a question, not a fault. Ask what job it does, and whether the draft gets that job done some other way. Names and grouping are the workshop's; the anti-Western's turned-over beats are in section 11.

| Beat | The job it does | If the draft lacks it, ask... |
|---|---|---|
| The frontier | Puts wild and settled side by side, with law too weak to hold, and shows what building here will cost | Where is the line between wild and settled, and can the audience see both sides of it? |
| Two heroes | Splits fighting from building, so the win can cost the one who wins it | Who builds, who fights, and do their fates part at the end? |
| The code | Sets the moral terms the showdown will test | What three or four rules does he live by, and where do two of them collide? |
| Why he can't stay | Plants the flaw: the freedom that shuts him out of love, and the public test he cannot refuse | Which of his strengths is also why he cannot belong, and who is watching when he chooses? |
| The doubled want | Gives him a private want that the story's goal works against, and a goal only fighting can reach | What does he want for himself, and how does fighting for them take it from him? |
| A town worth losing | Shows the builders' fragile, shared joy before the threat, so there is something to lose | Did we see the town happy before we saw it attacked? (*My Darling Clementine*: a dance in a church that is only a floor and a steeple) |
| The double | Sets a man of the same kind against him, so the moral difference stands alone | What separates the outlaw from the protector except how he uses the gun, and what bargain does he refuse? |
| The raid | Strikes at what the town has just built (fences cut, farms burned, the street terrorised) and makes the builders want to give up | What has the town just built, and does the attack hit it? |
| Fists to guns | Fights that grow in scale and danger: a *ratchet* (tension rising in steps, with partial releases) towards the showdown | Is each fight bigger than the last, and does one break the pattern before the audience learns it? |
| "It has to be me" | Breaks his reluctance to kill: Truby moves the genre's first self-revelation ahead of the showdown *(built: the showdown cannot happen until it does)* | Where does he accept that he must kill, and does it come before the showdown? |
| The showdown | Settles in an instant whether the town will have law: a ritual with watchers, a provocation, a rule and a long wait before a brief act | Who watches, who provokes, what rule binds him, and how long is the wait? |
| The draw | Puts the moral argument into one act: wait and risk death, or draw first and become a killer | Is the deciding choice his, not the outlaw's, and does keeping the rule cost him something? |
| Outside the door | Shows the price in one image: the public win, the private loss, a man outside the home he saved (*The Searchers*) | What image shows he has won and lost at once, and does he know it? |

**The chain that holds them** *(built)*: the code sets the rule, the shame culture forces the fight, the town's future hangs on its outcome, and the flaw that lets him win is the one that shuts him out. Cut a link and the showdown becomes a gunfight in a street (`.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`, section 3). **Shape** *(fitted)*: the classic Western runs in a straight line, one hero, one goal, one main opponent; the anti-Western rises and falls, like Gangster (`.claude/skills/plot/references/structure-and-conflict.md`, section 8).

## 7. The genre's question

Truby's picture of life in the Western, its *Mind-Action story view* in his term *(asserted)*: a founding myth in which the gods are ordinary people making a country, and settling the land is a moral success. Its root question is Action's, hold your ground or run to live, and frontier morality allows only one answer: stand. A second follows: is justice done with the gun, or by persuading people of what is right? Underneath lies an existential theme: to exist is to be on the way to an end, and the wild, the protector's way of life and the protector himself are all passing *(asserted; broad enough to fit many stories)*.

His *thematic recipe* (a genre's plan for living well), in gist *(asserted)*: fight so that others can make homes, and a society follows in which everyone can live freely. **As a question for a draft: stand or run, and what does making a home for others cost the one who makes it possible?**
- **The funnel** *(built)*: the whole social theme narrows to one person's act in a split second in the street. If the showdown decides only a private quarrel, widen what hangs on it.
- **Settlement.** The Gap theory puts meaning in how the gaps settle (principle 7), and asks what has changed from the opening normal and what it cost. The classic Western answers in two halves: the town's normal is restored and improved; the protector's is gone. The meaning sits in the space between the public win and the private loss.
- **Keep it a live question.** Truby's account of why the classic Western was dead as art even at its most popular *(argued from timing; loose)*: it dramatised a belief in westward destiny that had peaked half a century or more before the films, so its answer was settled before the story began. In Gap terms, the workshop's reading, a question already answered is not open, so it makes no promise. **Test:** is the draft's question still open for today's audience (who pays for progress, what freedom means inside a system)? If the story only celebrates a settled belief, it reads as nostalgia.

## 8. Where the care comes from

Care is the first term of the suspense equation and must be built before the threat arrives. The classic protector runs on these Bond levers:
- **Competence** (the fast draw) and **Decency** (the code, defending the weak) run high, and **Agency** is certain: he stands. Pushed to the top, these risk the Superman problem, a hero who cannot lose; the genre offsets it with his reluctance, his doom and his stunted heart (the workshop's reading of Bond theory, not Truby's).
- **Connection** is turned deliberately low: he is a loner. Bond principle 4 says turn another lever up, and the genre routes care through others (principle 5): the child who looks up to him, the family who need him. **Vulnerability** comes from the shame trap, the reluctance to kill and a doom he half knows. His **Flaw** is real and costs him the home he saves. If the story has built a bond at the **Miss** rung, through time spent with him and his ties to the child and the family, the doorway image is where the audience feels that loss while he is still alive; the Flaw makes the loss happen, the time together makes it hurt.
- **Specificity and Humour** come from what his gear and habits show about the man (a detail should reveal, not decorate) and from Truby's pantheon of ordinary heroes *(fitted)*: rough, bawdy, mortal, sometimes absurd, closer to Greek gods than to saints. Bond theory agrees that goodness with no flaw is bland.
- **The outlaw needs care too** (Bond principle 6). As the protector's double, and a warrior the protector respects across the gulf between their values, he is a person the audience wants something to happen to. A native opponent drawn as a faceless Other is a function, and to today's audience a prejudice (section 13).

**The anti-Western balances the other way.** As Competence and Decency fall, Humour (Butch's banter), Connection (the buddy pair, Etta, McCabe's love for Mrs Miller) and Vulnerability rise. A cowardly town makes the lone man more sympathetic by contrast. Sundance's turn from sulky loser to the best shot in the West is Bond principle 7: a surprise that was in him all along. The avenging stranger whose punishments include rape and murder tests where some acts end allegiance (Bond theory, the limits of care).

## 9. Suspense and knowledge

**The equation.** Suspense = Care × Threat × Uncertainty × Time. The Western's signature term is **Time**: the showdown's split second drawn out until it feels endless *(built)*, Hitchcock's bomb applied to a gunfight. *Once Upon a Time in the West* keeps three gunmen waiting a long time at a remote station for an exchange that ends in an instant; *The Good, the Bad and the Ugly* stretches a three-way showdown with close-ups of the eyes. Stretch the wait; keep the act brief. The ritual's rules do what Anticipation principle 3 asks of a threat's rules, giving the audience enough to predict with: an insult in public means someone will die, a man cannot walk away, the good man does not draw first. Each rule turns waiting into suspense.

**Uncertainty, refuge and the ratchet.** Truby's pressure technique, from *3:10 to Yuma*, adds guns against the protector and takes allies away until he stands alone *(built)*. Suspense climbs as his chances drop, and collapses once hope is gone (principle 1): the raid should leave the builders hopeless and the audience not, so keep one thin chance in view. The farm and the town are the refuge the raid invades (principle 4, lost refuge); in the anti-Western the town that will not help is help stripped away. Fists to guns is a ratchet (principle 7). Truby does not add that the pattern must break before the audience learns it; the theory does, and one fight that goes wrong, or never happens, resets the rhythm.

**Withholding and knowledge.** The Western's main gap is withholding, as suspense: the audience knows the showdown is coming, not how it ends. The *Traveling Devil* form (section 10) adds curiosity, a town's hidden wrong scheduled for release (Gap principle 4). For *knowledge position*, whether the audience knows more than, the same as, or less than the characters (principle 6; the Gap theory's knowledge gap), *Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid* is the chapter's best worked case *(built)*. The opening card game keeps the audience level with a man who thinks he is about to shoot a cheat, so it learns who Sundance is when he does: a surprise, and a triumph. The last scene puts the audience ahead: it sees the soldiers on the rooftops that the two wounded men, planning their next escape, do not, so every joke hurts. **A pattern from one film** *(fitted, so a question, not a rule)*: for a doomed end, putting the audience ahead turns every joke into the suspense of helplessness, as here; for a triumph, level gives a shared surprise, and ahead still gives suspense (the bomb under the table). Choose scene by scene (principle 6). The film then freezes on the pair as they run out, and the audience hears the shots without seeing the deaths: it stops below the horror rung (principle 2) and lets the audience's imagination supply the rest. More: `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`; on who knows what, `.claude/skills/plot/references/telling-and-perspective.md`, section 5.

## 10. Subgenres and mixes

**Subgenres Truby names:** the anti-Western (section 11), with two later forms: the *Traveling Devil*, a stranger who seems evil and exposes a town that seems good (*High Plains Drifter*), and *Revenge by the Stranger*, an avenger back from death or the distant past to punish a corrupt town, whose mix of just punishment and atrocity makes him, in Truby's reading, a Gangster hero dressed as a cowboy. Also the Contemporary Western, the Western Comedy (*Cat Ballou*, *Blazing Saddles*) and the space Western; the visiting helper is shared with Detective and Comedy.

**Mixes.** Science Fiction is the commonest partner: Truby says the Western did not die but moved to the future (*Star Wars*, with Han Solo as a cowboy in space; *The Mandalorian*; *Cowboy Bebop*) *(fitted)*. In Implied World terms the move is cheap: the beats call up the Western's defaults wherever they are set, so the audience furnishes a frontier on a planet from memory. Crime in the modern West: *No Country for Old Men*, *Hell or High Water* (`crime.md`).

**Family and descendants** *(asserted genealogy)*. The Western belongs to the mythic family with Myth and Action, all genres with a fighter hero (`myth.md`, `action.md`). Truby jokes that the unmarried cowboy still had sons: the detective, who reasons (`detective-and-thriller.md`), the gangster, who takes (`gangster.md`), and the anti-Western's cowboy, who can no longer fight. **Sorting questions for a draft:** if the hero wants to put down roots himself, the story is about the builder, not the protector; if the fights grow but no town hangs on them, Action has taken the lead; if the hero follows the West into the city and rises there, Gangster has (Truby places Gangster next, as the story of the city the Western ends at). On which genre leads a mix: `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`, section 8.

## 11. Twisting it

Truby's general routes above a genre are in `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`, section 9. For the Western his route is one form, the anti-Western, which he counts among the richest in theme *(asserted)*. Set the story where the frontier closes, around 1890 to 1900, as the city and the company arrive, and turn each beat over. His condition (how-genres-work, section 9): the reversals grow from a reversed theme, here a society that destroys the man who built it, not from a wish to be different. Keep the protector someone who stays out of settled life: a builder as hero makes the form heroic, while a hero outside the good he wins makes it tragic, because the audience sees what building costs a person *(built)*.

| Classic beat | Turned over | What it buys |
|---|---|---|
| The frontier | **The city arrives**: power moves from open to hidden, rules multiply, a man cannot ride far before hitting one *(built)* | The protector's tools stop working |
| A town worth losing | **The town that won't help**: cowardly, petty, no longer moved by shame (*High Noon*; *McCabe*'s grubby loners, drawn together only by cards) | Care moves to the lone man; help is stripped away |
| The gun and the horse | **New machines**: the railway (*Once Upon a Time in the West*), a car and a machine gun (*The Wild Bunch*) | One period object tells the audience which stage the world is in |
| The code | **The obsolete antihero**, whose code has outlived its world, or who has no code beyond gain; perhaps unable to shoot | A wrong belief that costs him everything: a real flaw, in Bond terms |
| The double | **The unseen boss**, acting through hired guns and a posse of the best lawmen in the West (*Butch Cassidy*'s rail tycoon, never on screen) | Power made indirect: the theme becomes the opponent |
| "It has to be me" | **No revelation**: someone tells him plainly that his time is over, and he cannot hear it (`.claude/skills/character/references/change-and-growth.md`, section 9) | The audience holds what he lacks: dramatic irony |
| Before the showdown | **The false high point** (Truby's *utopian moment*): a glimpse of the life they could have had (the Wild Bunch's rest in a lush village) | A partial release before the final blow, and a sight of what could be lost, which feeds the Worry rung |
| The showdown | **The massacre**, or a fight nobody sees: McCabe hunted through the snow while the town is busy saving its unused church from a fire | Remove one support (the fair rule, the watching town, the even odds) and let the end follow from that |

**Smaller twists from the chapter** *(fitted)*:
- **Move the showdown.** *Once Upon a Time in the West* puts its best gunfight at the start, where it founds a legend instead of closing one.
- **A fake crisis, and a real one to echo it.** *Butch Cassidy* opens on a card game that looks like Sundance's death and turns into his legend, introducing both men, their double act, their fame and their coming doom in one scene *(built: a crisis forces choices, choices show character, and a false one shows it without the cost)*. The finale repeats the tight room and the crisis with the one difference that matters: now it is real.
- **One line where the theme gathers** (Truby's *thematic vortex point*): a line false or ironic when spoken and true by the end, like Butch's joke that Sundance may be past it. The ending reframes it (Gap principle 7).
- **Expose what the founding myth leaves out.** Truby says the blank-slate story is wrong yet useful for a strong anti-Western: treat it as a hypothesis, and look for who was already there and who was forced to build. In Gap terms, the workshop's reading, the blank slate is a teller's bias, and showing what it hides works the perspective gap.

**One idea from the owner theories.** Let the frontier close on its own timetable (Implied World principle 4, indifference). The railway, the survey line and the company's offer advance whether the protector wins his fight or not. **Test:** if the protector died in chapter one, what would the frontier be doing next week? In a classic Western, waiting for a hero; in an anti-Western, closing, on schedule. A world with business of its own makes his obsolescence felt rather than announced, and it embodies the question (principle 8): the home he makes possible was never going to be his.

## 12. Examples

- *Shane*: the pattern; the protector who clears the valley for farmers and rides on. *The Searchers*: the loner at his most extreme; the doorway he does not walk through.
- *My Darling Clementine* and *High Noon*: a fragile, joyful town; then a town that leaves its marshal alone, and the badge thrown in the dust. *High Plains Drifter*: the Traveling Devil and the avenging stranger in one.
- *Red River*: the double, a trail boss turned tyrant and faced down by his adopted son. *3:10 to Yuma*: allies stripped away; a death that may buy nothing.
- *Once Upon a Time in the West*: the showdown stretched in time and moved to the opening; the railway as the new age.
- *The Wild Bunch* and *Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid*: machines, a false paradise, the massacre; a fake crisis, the audience level at the start and ahead at the end. *McCabe & Mrs. Miller*: a wet forest town; a builder who dies for the myth in a fight nobody watches.

## 13. Rivals and doubts

- **A theory of history, or the audience's real baseline.** *Truby:* the Western is a theory of history, not history, so complaints that it is unrealistic miss. *Implied World theory:* the baseline is the real world plus the genre's habits, and the audience fills in from what it knows, whatever the genre intends; Truby half concedes it. *Test:* keep the classic raid by faceless native warriors and show it to a present-day audience. Truby predicts it reads as myth and the town's danger holds; the theory predicts the raiders read as the builders' prejudice and care for the town drops. Give the people already there faces and a case, and the theory predicts the danger returns as tragedy.
- **The unseen opponent.** *Truby:* the anti-Western's real enemy need never appear. *Bond principle 6:* a villain fails when he is a function, not a person. They fit if a visible agent (the hired gun who murders a family in *Once Upon a Time in the West*, the posse that never tires) carries the care while the boss stands for hidden power. *Test:* cut the visible agent. The theory predicts the last act loses heat; Truby's praise predicts no loss.
- **Pressure to certain death, and every guarantee broken.** *Truby:* strip allies until the hero faces near-certain death, and in the anti-Western turn the showdown into a massacre he cannot win and nearly every guarantee over with it. *Anticipation principles 1 and 5:* suspense collapses once hope is gone; break one guarantee convincingly, not all. *Test:* write the showdown twice, once with one thin way out and only the town's support broken, once with no way out and everything turned over. The theory predicts suspense in the first, and in the second despair rather than suspense (and, with every guarantee gone, a risk that care collapses too). Truby's aim in the second is elegy, which the theory does not address; know which feeling you want.
- **Every tool a symbol, or some things just there.** *Truby* loads horse, fence, hat, gun and badge with theme; *Implied World principle 5* says details that serve no purpose make a world feel real. *Test:* add to the town one detail that means nothing. Truby's approach predicts it is wasted; the theory predicts the town feels more lived in, and that a town where every object signifies reads as a stage set.
- **Doubts.** Truby's claim that the Western alone shows a whole society's growth is not argued against the historical epic. "Being is becoming", the *Historical Code* (Truby's name for showing history as one movement of land, people and tools) and his claim that history swings between growing and settling fit any story after the fact: maps, not laws. The claim that the move from village to city always turns the hero into an antihero overreaches, and his verdict that the classic form was dead as art could be fitted to any genre in decline. The beats are fitted mostly from Hollywood films of four decades and one country's founding myth: question them for other frontiers.
