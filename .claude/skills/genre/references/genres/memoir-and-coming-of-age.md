# Memoir and Coming-of-Age

*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Genres, chapter 5, in the workshop's own words; framed by the owner theories (Gap theory's perspective and withholding gaps and principles 2, 5, 6 and 7; Anticipation theory's suspense equation, anomalous suspense and principles 4, 5 and 6; Bond theory's levers, chiefly Interiority, Flaw, Vulnerability, Desire and Connection, principles 2, 3, 4 and 7, the Recognise, Side with and Miss rungs, and the point that care can't be ordered; Implied World principles 1, 2, 4, 5 and 8).*

Contents: 1 In one line. 2 The promise. 3 The baseline. 4 Guarantees, and which to break. 5 Hero and opponent. 6 The beats, as jobs. 7 The genre's question. 8 Where the care comes from. 9 Suspense and knowledge. 10 Subgenres and mixes. 11 Twisting it. 12 Examples. 13 Rivals and doubts.

## 1. In one line

Both genres show a self being made: in Memoir a real person looks back to find the pattern that trapped her, so that she can act differently from now on; in Coming-of-Age a young person stops believing she is unfree and starts answering for who she is.

Words kept apart in this file. **Memoir** is a true account of one strand of the author's life, chosen and ordered for effect. **Coming-of-Age** is the invented story of a self being formed (its older name is *bildungsroman*, the novel of formation). Memoir has three persons, never swapped: **the author**, the real person; **the teller**, the self who narrates on the page (the Gap theory's teller), a character built out of choices and more limited than the author; and **the past self**, the younger person inside the events. **The hero**, in Memoir, is the teller and the past self together: the author as a character. A **reveal** is the moment a withheld fact reaches the audience, a character, or both (the plot skill's definition); a **self-revelation** is the hero seeing herself in a new way. Truby pairs the two genres because he sees both as tracing one lifelong job, becoming someone *(asserted)*.

## 2. The promise

**Memoir** promises "this happened, and I was there": the closeness of a mind speaking in the first person, joined to the weight of events nobody made up. That is the genre's contract, not a guarantee to twist: a memoir caught inventing (the uproar over *A Million Little Pieces*) loses the contract rather than bending it. Truby holds that a remarkable true experience hits harder than the same events invented *(asserted; plausible, untested)*. The audience also comes for insight paid for in pain, for proof that a person can change, and for his paradox *(asserted)* that the more exactly one life is pinned down, the more strangers see themselves in it. The Bond theory backs only the first half: specific details make a particular person (its Recognise rung). **Move:** ask what only a witness could supply (what it smelled like, what she thought in that second) and put it early. It is the genre's credential.

**Coming-of-Age** promises strong feeling, above all the tears that come when someone long cut off is finally joined to others (called **the bridge** here) *(built: a separation the audience has felt for the whole story is crossed)*, and the pleasure of seeing one's own passage to adulthood reflected.

In Gap theory terms, the question the story presses hardest is about the self: what was the pattern, and what did it mean? Because the book keeps returning to it, it is a mystery (Gap principle 5: an open question the story promises to answer), and the ending must answer it. Memoir moves the settlement (Gap principle 7: meaning is how the gaps are settled, which close and which stay open) into the teller's present (the workshop's reading): the settlement includes how the teller's normal differs at the end from the start, what she will now do differently, and what that cost.

## 3. The baseline

The *baseline* (Implied World theory) is what the audience assumes by default: the real world plus the genre's habits. **Memoir allows no invented departures**: every departure from the audience's baseline must be true, whether an unfamiliar place (the death zone on Everest), a closed community or the rule one family lives by. The strictness belongs to the truth contract (section 2), not to the baseline. On top of the real world the audience assumes *(fitted)*: a family, or a group acting like one, as the arena; one turning point, often in childhood, seen from adult hindsight; a teller who survived; an honest account; an insight at the end. **Coming-of-Age** assumes an ordinary social world pressing on the young (parents, school, the expected career), and the audience's own adolescence furnishes it for free. Both are cheap under Implied World principles 1 and 2 (minimal departure; the attention budget). The departure that matters most is usually inward: the rule one family lives by, which nobody inside it can see (the workshop's reading).

**Case.** In *Educated* the family's rule is that danger lies outside: in schools, doctors, the government. The rule keeps the children out of classrooms and hospitals, puts them to work in the scrapyard, and leaves a violent brother unnamed, since the family has room for only one kind of danger. The book's long turn is her learning to see the rule.

**Point.** Truby's strongest memoirs set the hero against a whole group held together by one belief (his *system of slavery*; here, **the unspoken rule**), and the heart of the investigation is seeing the rule beneath the separate opponents *(built: a harm nobody can name cannot be refused, which is why escape is hard and why naming it is a discovery)*. In owner terms the rule is the world's normal: it decides what each person wants and what it costs (Gap principle 2), it grinds everyone, not just the hero (Implied World principle 4), and it embodies the story's question (principle 8). **Test:** write the rule down, then check that each opponent's harm follows from it. If it does not, you have villains without a world. **Generic Memoir** rides on the defaults (the drinking father, the cold mother, the small town) and could be anyone's; the fix is the rule only this family lived by. For the world side hand to `story-world`; for the people, to `.claude/skills/character/references/building-a-character.md`.

## 4. Guarantees, and which to break

A *guarantee* is a protection the audience assumes without being told. Anticipation principle 5: break one early and convincingly and the audience stops relaxing; break them all and care collapses. Memoir sells insight rather than fear, so here an early break makes the account's stakes real rather than its threats (the workshop's extension). The table is the workshop's, drawn from Truby's examples; "it really happened" is the contract (section 2), not a guarantee.

| Guarantee the audience leans on | Broken by | What breaking it buys |
|---|---|---|
| The teller survived | Almost never: *When Breath Becomes Air*, published after the author's death with an epilogue by his widow | Every page read against a loss the reader knows is coming |
| The teller is the innocent party | *Into Thin Air*, where the author weighs his own share in the deaths | The account is no longer taken on trust, so every choice the teller made is weighed |

**Expectations to overturn.** Two more patterns are not protections but expectations about how the story ends, broken late; what they buy is truth or residue, not the fear principle 5 describes. *The young hero grows fully*: *Moonlight* ends on one small, uncertain step, truer to how growth usually goes. *Growing up ends happily*: *The Graduate*'s last ride on the bus leaves freedom as a burden just beginning.

**Which one to break.** In Memoir, the teller's innocence, early. What the break buys is doubt about the account, which turns the reader from listener into judge. **Test by removal:** restore her innocence (cut the scene where she hurts someone) and ask whether the opponents flatten into villains and the book into a defence of the author. If nothing flattens, the break was doing no work. Break one guarantee, not all, and keep that one in proportion: a teller guilty of everything loses care as surely as one guilty of nothing (Bond theory: severe flaws alienate unless other levers make up for them). The survival guarantee is nearly unbreakable, so build suspense on how she survived and at what cost, not on whether (section 9).

## 5. Hero and opponent

**The Memoir hero** investigates her own past (Truby's *detective of oneself*): the case she is solving is herself, which is why the whole book bends towards self-revelation *(built)*. Three jobs, in the order a writer usually meets them:
- **Give each layer a want** *(built)*. The teller wants, now, to understand; the past self needs one concrete goal that holds for most of the book. A real life offers dozens of unconnected wants, so pick the strand where one goal runs longest, and let a young past self grope towards it, each attempt better aimed than the last. In Gap theory terms, one want for the telling and one for the story (the workshop's reading). If most chapters neither advance nor block the second, the book is a diary.
- **Turn the wound into a weakness.** Truby's *ghost* is a past event that still haunts the hero; in Memoir usually harm done inside the family. Keep the **wound** (what was done to her) apart from the **weakness** (what she now does wrong because of it) *(built)*. If she can name the wound but not the weakness, the wound is not yet doing work. Check too that the span (Truby's *frame*: where the story starts and stops) reaches the deepest wound; if not, move the span.
- **Make one weakness moral, and own it** *(built)*. Her need has two sides: stop replaying the pattern, and stop a specific harm she does to others, named as an act. Without the second the book becomes her case for the defence: a teller who blames others for everything reads as a victim, and a spotless hero leaves everyone else a villain. Where she truly was a victim, Truby makes it a story of survival and self-worth instead. The Bond theory agrees: pure victimhood turns to pity, then fatigue, unless she pushes back.

**The Coming-of-Age hero** starts in *bad faith*, the existentialist term Truby borrows: sure she is not free, and living by what others expect. Carry that weakness through the "I can't, because..." test in section 6. Or give her a failing typical of her age: Truby reads each of four Shakespeare tragedies as built on one stage of life's failing *(fitted)*, rash young love in *Romeo and Juliet*, for instance. Her need is to answer for her own life instead of blaming its walls; in Bond terms, to turn a Flaw the audience forgives into Agency it can respect.

**Opponents** are people she cannot simply fight, because she loves them: the family, or a group acting like one *(built: the people you fight are the people you need, so hostility cannot come out cleanly)*. **Test:** for each main opponent, list what the hero loves in them, what she hates, and what each is trying to control; an empty column means the bond is too simple for this genre, and by the end the lists should raise a question of forgiveness. Beneath the people sits the unspoken rule. In adventure memoir the opponent is nature plus fellow travellers; in Coming-of-Age, society's pressures, bullies, and above all the hero's own false self. More: `.claude/skills/character/references/opponents.md`.

## 6. The beats, as jobs

The beats are fitted, drawn mostly from two memoirs and a spread of novels and films: a missing beat is a question, not a fault. Ask what job it does, and whether the draft gets that job done some other way. Names and grouping are the workshop's.

| Beat | The job it does | If the draft lacks it, ask... |
|---|---|---|
| **Memoir: the telling** | | |
| The trigger | A present event (a death, a diagnosis, a disaster) that forces the teller to look back now, and gives the telling a want of its own | What is at stake for the teller today in telling this? Without it the opening is an announcement |
| Span from the end | Picks where the story starts and stops, aimed at a last conflict life never tidily supplied; decided at the premise, not in a late polish | Which scene is the last conflict, and does the span lead straight to it? |
| The limited teller | Makes the narrating self a character with blind spots, so memory itself can be tested | Where could her memory or bias be wrong, and does the book ever let us see it? |
| **Memoir: the past** | | |
| The unspoken rule | Gives the opponents a shared cause, so they are people caught in a system | What rule did this family live by, and does each opponent's harm follow from it? |
| The hero's make-up | A wound turned into a weakness (including a moral one), a want for each layer, and an opponent she loves, which together turn episodes into a line (section 5) | What does she now do wrong because of the wound? Does most of the book advance or block the past self's want? Which column of the opponent test is empty? |
| **Memoir: the drive to the end** | | |
| Reveals that reread | Turns where life gave few: a loved one proves her fiercest opponent, or an old event means something else now | What did she not know then, and where would learning it change how we read everything before? |
| The chain of choices | Her actions towards the goal shown as decisions and their costs, so the book argues how to live instead of keeping a diary of feelings | List her major decisions (Truby suggests ten or more), each with its cost. Are chapters built on these, or on moods? |
| The last confrontation | The final face-off with the main family opponent over the harm (or, in adventure memoir, with nature) | What act or conversation confronts the chief opponent? If life gave none, which real event can serve? |
| Two self-revelations | One for the past self as the events close; a second, weightier one for the teller that only writing the book could produce | Does the second add to the first, or reverse it? If it only repeats it, why does the book exist? |
| Forgive or leave | The outward act that proves the inward change | Which did she choose, and does it fit what this person deserves rather than what seems kind? |
| The cost of telling | Publishing as a moral choice with present stakes (Truby's *moral effect*) | Who could this book hurt, and who could it help? Is that weighed on the page? |
| **Coming-of-Age** | | |
| The unfree self | The opening belief that she is not free, stated so the ending can answer it | Can you write it as "I can't, because..."? Which walls she blames are real, and which only accepted expectations (*The Graduate*)? |
| The age's test | A failing typical of her stage of life, which the events then test exactly | What failing belongs to her age, and are the events aimed at it? |
| The seeing | Self-revelation: the opening belief seen for what it was, and her own part in it owned | Set the opening belief beside it: are they clear opposites, or one grown from the other? |
| The new act | A new moral action proving the change; at best a bridge built in the terms of her isolation (*CODA*) | What act shows the belief has changed? Is the climax specific to her isolation? |

## 7. The genre's question

**As questions for a draft.** Memoir: *what pattern did this life keep repeating, and what will the teller do differently now she has seen it?* If nothing, the reflection is nostalgia, not story. Coming-of-Age: *what does she believe makes her unfree, and what act proves she has stopped believing it?* In Gap theory terms a theme is the story's answer to the question its world poses; here the world is the unspoken rule, so the theme answers the rule.

Behind them sits Truby's view of life *(asserted)*. Memoir says an adult grows by review: seen from a distance, a life shows its pattern, and a seen pattern can be broken. His *Way of Becoming*, the genre's recipe for living, comes down to one job: find the pattern, then act against it. For Coming-of-Age, identity is made, not found (his *Existential Code*, existentialism in story form, *asserted*): the story should show her building a self through what she chooses and commits to, not "finding herself". A big turn that is only a physical milestone (first sex, first job) with no belief changed is, in his view, the shallow version *(asserted)*. The workshop's test for the real thing: has a belief changed, and has an act proved it?

**A caution** *(asserted by Truby, explained by the owner theories)*: Memoir leans on theme by design, which risks an inward, preachy book where little happens. Telling the reader what something meant jumps ahead of the settlement, and the Bond theory's point that audiences resist being told what to feel (care cannot be ordered) applies to meaning too. **Test:** mark the pages where the teller explains meaning and the pages where something happens; where meaning wins, turn explanation into a scene of conflict. More: `.claude/skills/plot/references/premise-and-theme.md`.

## 8. Where the care comes from

- **Interiority** leads: first person gives direct access to the teller's mind. With Bond principle 2 (alignment beats virtue) it keeps the reader with a teller who admits harm, which is why the break in section 4 costs less care than a writer fears. **Specificity** and voice carry recognition: *The Catcher in the Rye* survives on voice alone.
- **Flaw** is essential: Truby's insistence on a moral weakness is the Bond theory's warning against pure victimhood and flawless decency, reached from the other side. **Vulnerability** comes from the ghost and the wound and runs high, so she has to push back.
- **Connection** comes from the love half of love-hate. The bridge (*CODA*) pays it off at the **Side with** rung (we wanted her to get it); the farewell is where the **Miss** rung is felt (we would grieve if they were gone). When a story is about clinging to childhood, feeling goes to the one who grows up and says goodbye: Wendy in *Peter Pan*, Andy giving away his toys in *Toy Story 3* *(fitted, two examples)*.
- **Desire** is structurally weak in real lives and has to be engineered through the two wants; **Agency** runs low for a child or a victim, and the firming plan and the final moral act raise it.
- **Balance** (Bond principle 4). A Coming-of-Age hero who opens in self-pity and blame, as Truby's does, is running high Vulnerability and an alienating Flaw at once, and the Bond theory warns that pure victimhood turns to pity, then fatigue (a constraint Truby does not state). Something else must carry her early: a want she chases hard, a voice, humour.

## 9. Suspense and knowledge

**Curiosity before suspense.** In the Gap theory, curiosity is a gap about the past and suspense a gap about the future. Memoir runs mainly on curiosity, because the teller plainly survived. In the suspense equation (Care × Threat × Uncertainty × Time) the known outcome moves Uncertainty from *whether* to *how* and at what cost; an immersed reader may still feel the danger (anomalous suspense; section 13). Put suspense also on others' fates (*Into Thin Air*'s companions) and on moral outcomes (will she forgive; how far is she to blame?). The family is the refuge turned dangerous (Anticipation principle 4), which is why threat in Memoir cuts deep even when no life is at stake (the workshop's reading).

**The Storyteller Structure**, Truby's term *(built)*: the whole book told from a present moment just after a crisis, looking back until the past catches up with it. It is not a flashback, which is one remembered event. In owner-theory terms it does two jobs (the workshop's reading): when the facts are fixed, order is the only freedom, and the device licenses any order a memory might take; and it gives the teller a want in the present, so the telling has a line of its own. **Case.** *Milk*, a biopic, opens on him taping a message for use if he is assassinated, jumps back eight years, and runs forward to the killing. In prose the same device is the Anticipation theory's signature tool, the narrator who knows the ending. More: `.claude/skills/plot/references/telling-and-perspective.md`.

**Keep the framing question open.** The question the opening raises (what happened here, and why?) is a mystery, and once it is answered curiosity drops. *The Liars' Club* opens on police in the house and the parents gone, and explains that scene at the close of part one; the later parts are weaker for it *(fitted, one case; the mechanism is Gap principle 5)*. Move the answer later, or open a new framing question to carry what follows.

**Reveals that reread** *(built)*. Because the teller and the past self are one person, the perspective gap (what happened against what the teller says) is itself hidden information. For each key event, write what she believed then and what she understands now, and stage the re-understanding as a scene. Such a reveal usually moves the blame: off her, onto her, or sideways. By Bond principle 7 it deepens the bond when it proves true all along. More: `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`.

**Knowledge position** (Anticipation principle 6). The teller knows more than the past self (though not everything; see the limited teller), so the audience's position against the past self is set by what she lets slip: ahead of the past self when she hints at what is coming, level when hindsight is held back, behind when she keeps something back. That last is fair only when the keeping back is part of who she is (Gap principle 6): a teller still ashamed may withhold; a teller saving a twist is cheating.

**When a memoir sags, find the low term** (the workshop's mapping of hindsight problems Truby lists, *fitted*). **Threat** falls in years where nothing presses, and when reflection stops the action. **Care** falls when the life has no single want (Bond principle 3), or the teller has gone cold on her own past. The cures are the Storyteller Structure, the two wants, and a borrowed Detective or Thriller shape *(built)*: make the central question about the past a case, and each chapter a step in solving it. Both fit because the hero is already investigating.

**Coming-of-Age** often keeps suspense low and pays out in feeling. *CODA*'s school concert is shown from the deaf parents' side with the sound taken away, so the audience is level with them and feels their exclusion before the bridge. Antiplot (below) pushes Threat and Uncertainty near zero, which explains the boredom Truby notes in it.

## 10. Subgenres and mixes

**True stories.** Memoir leads a wider family: autobiography (the whole life; one life can hold many memoirs, each a single strand), the biopic (told from outside the subject, where Memoir is told from inside), reportage and documentary. **Move:** name the strand your memoir covers and cut any episode that does not bear on it, however vivid; a little off-strand detail may still earn its place as surplus (Implied World principle 5).

**Coming-of-Age by the step the self takes.** Truby lays these out as a ladder of inner *self stages*, apart from the social life stages everyone passes through; they are grouped here by direction. **Move:** name the step your hero takes, then check that the events test exactly that step.
- *Refusing the step*: the childhood that never ends (*Peter Pan*), or responsibility accepted while the deadening adult role is refused (*The Catcher in the Rye*), told as *antiplot*, Truby's term for a story where almost nothing happens.
- *Child to adult*, the common case: dropping a false picture of self and world and acting to right past wrongs. It can carry a nation's conscience (*Huckleberry Finn*), be split across child and father (*To Kill a Mockingbird*), or come in halting sections (*Moonlight*).
- *Rarer steps*: leader or visionary (native to Myth), machine to human (*Westworld*), shaping another's growth (*The Intouchables*), and the artist's formation, the *Künstlerroman* (*CODA*), where family loyalty pulls against the art; give the art an outside conflict with real stakes or it stays private.

**The Fantasy bildungsroman** *(built, partly fitted from one huge success)*. *Harry Potter* joins growing up to becoming a wizard at a school, which every reader has attended: in Implied World terms the school is the baseline that makes the magical departures cheap, and Harry is the outsider who learns alongside us. **Move:** anchor a fantasy coming-of-age in an institution the reader knows from life. See `fantasy.md`.

**Hybrids with Myth** (see `myth.md`). Truby's *Psychological Epic* is Myth with the fantastic journey turned down and the family quarrel turned up, as in Greek tragedy. Two moves do the work here: give the hero a desire strong enough to carry the road's detours, and bring the family on the road, so the conflict travels with her; and *(built)* tie her hidden moral fault to a visible disorder in the world, so investigating one investigates the other (*Oedipus Rex*). *Personal Myth* sends a fictionalised self on a mythic journey (*Big Fish*); by Gap principle 5 its magic works best as wonder, never explained.

**Other mixes.** Memoir with Detective or Thriller for drive (`detective-and-thriller.md`); Coming-of-Age with Love (*Jane Eyre*), Sports (*Whiplash*), Crime (*Boyz n the Hood*) and a society modernising (*Cinema Paradiso*). A memoir that keeps reaching for social critique may need a society genre's tools; Truby points to `science-fiction.md`. On which genre leads a mix: `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`.

## 11. Twisting it

Truby's general routes above a genre are in `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`. Here the larger form of life behind the genres is the making of a self, and the chapter's own moves are, restated:
- **Sharpen the telling beats**: a present crisis with a strong trigger, a teller who owns her share of the blame, a second self-revelation only the writing could produce, and an ending on the cost of telling.
- **In fiction, pick an unusual step for the self** (visionary, machine to human, artist, shaper of another), or rebel against the form knowing the price: antiplot needs a voice strong enough to be the attraction, or mythic parallels that lift the ordinary. Without either, add events.
- **One arc at every scale** *(built)*, Truby's *social fractals*, modelled on *Cinema Paradiso*: the hero's paradise and its loss matched by the village cinema's and the town's, the town modernising as the hero ages, told by a man come home decades later. **Move:** name the hero's arc in a few words, find an institution and a community that go through the same arc for reasons of their own, and time their turns to meet his.

**One idea from the owner theories: leave one person a wonder.** Memoir's pull is to explain everyone, since the book is the author's case. Gap principle 5 says a question the story never presses can stay a wonder (an open question the story promises to leave open), and the Implied World theory's expansion paradox warns that turning gaps into settled fact can make a world feel smaller. So let one part of one opponent, often the parent most loved and most feared, be seen clearly and never accounted for. Keep it unpressed: if the book circles it, it has become a mystery and must be answered. **Test with removal:** add a chapter that fully explains it. If the person shrinks into a case study and the book into a defence, the wonder was doing work.

## 12. Examples

- *The Liars' Club* and *Educated*: parents loved and hated, a framing mystery answered too early; a family's unspoken rule, and a teller learning to see it.
- *Into Thin Air* and *Milk*: the trigger at the summit and a teller who shares the blame; the Storyteller Structure opening on the ending.
- *Cinema Paradiso*: one arc at three scales and across fifty years, told by a man coming home.
- *The Graduate* and *Moonlight*: false walls and freedom's burden starting; growth in halting sections, ending on a small step.
- *Peter Pan* and *Toy Story 3*: the childhood that never ends, with feeling given to whoever says goodbye.
- *Huckleberry Finn*, *To Kill a Mockingbird*: a nation's conscience grown on a river journey; a coming-of-age doubled across child and father.
- *The Catcher in the Rye* against *David Copperfield*: voice carrying an antiplot, against theme carried by a great many events.
- *Harry Potter* and *CODA*: the fantasy anchored on a school; the bridge built in the terms of the isolation.

## 13. Rivals and doubts

- **Suspense for a teller who lived.** *Truby:* the Storyteller Structure makes readers fear for the teller herself. *Anticipation theory* holds two things: a hero who plainly cannot lose gives no suspense (multiplication), yet felt uncertainty survives a known ending while the reader is immersed (anomalous suspense), and its signature prose tool is the narrator who knows the ending. So it backs Truby, with a limit: the uncertainty moves from whether to how. *Test:* stage the teller's crisis twice, once laced with present-day reminders that she survived ("I still have the scar"), once as close, immersed scene without them. If the immersed version brings back tension about whether she survives, anomalous suspense is at work and Truby is right; if neither does, multiplication rules. Either way the safe move is a companion whose survival is open.
- **What plot is.** *Truby:* plot is conflict plus reveals, so Memoir's chief problem is too little plot. *Gap theory:* plot is the order, selection and emphasis of what the audience learns, so every memoir has one, and the lack in Gap terms is more often a thin breach. (No single want is a Bond diagnosis; low Threat and Uncertainty an Anticipation one.) Much of the clash is vocabulary, since reordering moves reveals, which Truby counts as plot too; the real question is whether a flat chapter needs new events or a new schedule. *Test:* make two versions, (a) one conflict event added in the old order, (b) the old facts reordered with nothing added. Which lifts the chapter says which cure it needs, not which theory is right.
- **One moment or many.** Truby says Coming-of-Age makes the self in a single self-revelation, then praises *Moonlight* for growth in fits and starts: a tension inside his chapter that the owner theories do not decide. The nearest owner hooks are the Bond theory's point that care can't be ordered, which warns against a declared revelation, and Bond principle 7, a change that turns out to have been in them all along, which fits either shape. *Test:* end a halting story on a grand declared self-revelation instead of a small act. If the declaration reads as told rather than earned, the single moment is one option, not a law.
- **One arc at every scale, or a world with business of its own.** Matching the town's rise and fall to the hero's risks a world that exists only to mirror him, against Implied World principle 4. *Test:* remove the hero; does the town still decline? In *Cinema Paradiso* it does, because modernising has causes of its own. If yours would not, the parallel is rigged. See `.claude/skills/story-world/references/world-from-story.md`.
- **Doubts.** The full memoir breakdowns rest on two books, both about harm within a group; the beats may fit a memoir of a happy childhood or of work less well. Truby's claim that Memoir is transcendent by nature, the genre ladder that places it after Myth and before Science Fiction, and the split into genres of the individual and of society are *asserted*. His ten decisions are a rule of thumb with no stated reason for the number; the principle under it is what matters. The claim that artists' stories are scarce because they lack drama is a commercial observation, not a mechanism.
