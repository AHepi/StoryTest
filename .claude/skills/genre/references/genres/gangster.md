# Gangster

*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Genres, chapter 10, in the workshop's own words; framed by the owner theories (Bond theory principles 1 to 7, the levers, the Miss rung and the limits of care, above all principle 2, alignment beats virtue; Implied World principles 1, 3, 4 and 8; Gap principles 2, 3, 4, 5 and 7 and the knowledge gap; Anticipation principles 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8).*

Contents: 1 In one line. 2 The promise. 3 The baseline. 4 Guarantees, and which to break. 5 Hero and opponent. 6 The beats, as jobs. 7 The genre's question. 8 Where the care comes from. 9 Suspense and knowledge. 10 Subgenres and mixes. 11 Twisting it. 12 Examples. 13 Rivals and doubts.

## 1. In one line

An outsider crowns himself king of a city by murder and fraud, in a republic that allows no crowns; he rises in money and rank while sinking as a person, and ends dead, or winning with his soul gone, while someone just as bad waits to take his place.

Words kept apart in this file: *Gangster*, with a capital, is the genre; *the gangster* is its hero. *The gang* is the criminal organisation (Truby's *crime family*); *family* means only blood relatives. The two often overlap in this genre, which is why they need two words. *The system* is the wider order of money and power the gang belongs to. *Crime*, with a capital, is the neighbouring genre. *Debt* is what the hero owes the gang, which is never paid off. A *reveal* is the moment a withheld fact reaches the audience, a character, or both (the plot skill's definition); the *self-revelation* is the hero's moment of seeing the truth about himself. Truby's framing *(asserted)*: Gangster takes Crime's moral concerns and spreads them over a whole society, so that the social order itself is the lawbreaker, and its master pattern is the *king in a democracy* (his term).

## 2. The promise

From Truby's account the audience comes for five things *(mostly asserted)*: **the climb, from inside**, a supremely confident outsider climbing from the street to the top on bribes and bullets; **money you can see**, success in countable form turned into things (a mansion full of strangers, a bath of banknotes) that later turn sour; **betrayal on every side**, because nobody in a kingdom held by force can be trusted, and every switch of side brings a reveal; **violence that climbs** from a quarrel over a deal to open war, ending in a mass killing or a spectacular fall; and **the double pleasure** of enjoying his success along the way and then seeing what it cost. The *higher forms* (Truby's routes above the basic genre, section 10) add the pleasure of seeing how the respectable system really works.

In Gap theory terms (principle 2: the world decides what counts as an event), a petty theft in a rotten city is barely a breach; the real breach is the hero's entry into the gang. Whether he will pay is the question the audience feels surest about, so the ones the story can keep open are how, when and at whose hand (section 9). The usual price is a bullet or the law. A draft that lets him off with no price of any kind denies half of the double pleasure the audience came for, and reads as a broken promise; a price paid in an unexpected form delivers it (section 11).

## 3. The baseline

Say "the mob" and the audience has furnished the city before the first scene ends. Unless told otherwise it assumes *(fitted, from the founding films and Truby's beats)*:
- **a corrupt, divided city**, great wealth beside deep poverty, rotten where the poor live: honest work rarely lifts anyone there; in the classic form, poor immigrant neighbourhoods set against a settled middle class;
- **the gang as a kingdom**, often built round one family: the group comes before the self, orders pass down through ranks as in an army, and one ruler at the top holds it all by force;
- **territory and tribute**: sharp borders between gangs, shopkeepers paying for protection from their protectors, and rooms where appetite is sold behind a locked door (drink, cards, sex);
- **hidden, throwaway guns**, loud new-money clothes, police who can be bought, favours that become lifelong debts, silence enforced by death;
- **no way out**: once in, in for life (Truby's *Gangster Code*).

**The broken ladder holds the rest in place** *(built)*. Show early and concretely that the honest route is blocked for this hero: who gets hired, who gets promoted, who owns what. If the honest route looks open, his crimes read as plain greed rather than a world's pressure (Gap principles 2 and 3: the world sets what people want and what it costs, and shows itself by what it does to people under strain).

**Where the departures lie** (the workshop's reading of Truby's claim that at the start the system is so vast it looks like the ordinary world, itself *asserted*). For a Gangster audience the hidden criminal kingdom is a genre default, so it costs almost nothing. The basic form spends its *departures* (Implied World: ways the fictional world differs from the baseline) on a place and an economy of its own (below). The higher forms depart from a real-world default instead, that respectable business plays clean (Implied World principle 1: unless told otherwise, the audience assumes the world works like theirs): the boardroom and the legislature turn out to run on the gang's rules, and because the audience assumed otherwise, this lands as a reveal about the world (Gap theory, Part 2, section 3). Truby's test: if the society around the gang is clean and only the gang is dirty, you are writing the basic form.

**Generic Gangster rides on these defaults**: pinstripes, fedoras, one immigrant community, Prohibition. Truby warns against shrinking the genre to professional criminals or to one ethnic group *(asserted)*. Several titles on his list show the way out (the workshop's reading): a French prison (*Un Prophète*), a Rio favela (*City of God*), Birmingham after the First World War (*Peaky Blinders*), each a departure of its own whose economy produces the gang (Implied World principle 3: a departure convinces in proportion to its knock-on effects). For the repair, hand to `.claude/skills/story-world/references/diagnosing-a-world.md` (section 3).

## 4. Guarantees, and which to break

A *guarantee* is a protection the audience assumes the genre gives without being told; while it trusts one, some part of it relaxes (Anticipation principle 5: fear needs the audience to believe these protections are gone). Gangster's protect people: the man at the top is untouchable, the family at the table is safe. Truby does not discuss guarantees directly; the table is the workshop's, drawn from his examples.

| Guarantee the audience leans on | Broken by | What breaking it buys |
|---|---|---|
| The man at the top is untouchable | *The Godfather*: the old don is shot in the street, early | No one is safe from then on |
| The family at the table is safe | *The Godfather*: Carlo, a brother-in-law, sets up Sonny's killing; a bomb meant for Michael kills his wife | Lost refuge: no family scene is safe again |

- **Which one to break.** The one worth most (the workshop's reading) is the safety of the family: the family table is the only refuge a gangster story has. Break it once, early and convincingly, and every later wedding, christening and dinner carries threat. Test by removal: restore it (the brother-in-law stays loyal) and ask whether the later family scenes lose their tension. If they do not, the break was doing no work.
- **The price is a promise, not a guarantee.** "He is punished at the end, by a bullet or the law" looks like a guarantee but protects nobody: it is the usual form of the price promised in section 2. By the cheated-or-unsafe test (`.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`, section 2), a hero who keeps everything with no price shown leaves the audience cheated, not unsafe. Changing the price's form is a twist (section 11); dropping it breaks the promise.

## 5. Hero and opponent

**Hero: the killer at the head of a gang.** Truby's line of descent among the genres makes him the Western hero's bad son *(asserted)*: the lone gunman moved into a crowded city where one man with a gun can no longer fix anything, so he can win only as part of an organisation *(fitted)*. **Test:** remove the gang. If he could still do it with his own fists and gun, he is an Action hero in a suit (`action.md`). He is no mastermind: his intelligence is practical, his ambition crude, his trade the forbidden appetites (drink, drugs, betting, sex) *(fitted)*. The contrast is the gentleman thief of *The Thomas Crown Affair*: if he commits crime for the pleasure of the game, the draft has drifted into the caper (`crime.md`).

**Flaw.** Truby's name for it is a *moral contradiction* *(built)*. The workshop's picture: two ledgers kept in one head, the family on one page and the killings on the other, never set side by side, so he can be tender at the christening and order a death the same afternoon. (For the general kinds of flaw and how values turn dark: `.claude/skills/character/references/building-a-character.md`, sections 4 and 7.) Two things keep the ledgers apart *(built)*. One is the gang's rule of silence, backed by death. The other is his own hunger to look strong and honoured, which he polices in himself and which turns each killing into something he can live with. **Test:** give the gang both, and show one character who could slip the rule but is held by his pride. A third strand *(built)*: he mistakes money for freedom, and since no sum is ever enough, the freedom never arrives. **Need and desire.** The need is to see the two ledgers, and the money trap; he almost never does, and that is the point (the beat *The blind spot*). The desire is money, then power, then empire, taken one deal at a time; every deal leaves someone owing someone, and those debts are where the later betrayals come from *(built)*.

**Opponents: all at once.** Truby's *six-point opposition* *(built: many fronts at once give density)*, grouped the workshop's way: above him, the boss he means to replace and the gang's hold on him through the debt; beside him, rivals inside the gang and the fake allies who change sides; outside, the police and the other gangs; and at home, his own blood family. Give each opponent a want of his own, not just a place in the plot (Bond principle 6: a function, not a person, leaves the audience indifferent). **Test:** list the fronts you have, what each wants from him and when each strikes. If the middle's pressure comes from only one or two, ask whether another front would add density or only noise. Truby's rule of thumb that two empty fronts make the story thin is *asserted*, with no examples, a pattern to question; six fronts at once can also go numb (section 9). See `.claude/skills/character/references/opponents.md` (section 10). In the higher forms the hero is an everyman climber (a bond seller, a salesman, an ad man) whose flaw is a false self-image that each success confirms instead of correcting, so he stays a child in an adult's body *(built)*.

## 6. The beats, as jobs

The beats are fitted, drawn from three films of 1931 and 1932 (*The Public Enemy*, *Little Caesar*, *Scarface*), which Truby treats as the base every later Gangster story varies from *(asserted)*, and from the classics that varied them. A missing beat is a question, not a fault: ask what job it does and whether the draft gets that job done another way. Names and grouping are the workshop's.

| Beat | The job it does | If the draft lacks it, ask... |
|---|---|---|
| The broken ladder | Shows honest work cannot lift him, so crime reads as pressure, not plain greed | Could he have climbed honestly? Who gets hired, promoted, paid? |
| The kingdom | Sets the trap before he walks in: the group over the self, ranks, and a king who rules by force | Who is king here, what does his power rest on, and what does the gang demand above the self? |
| The first small crime | Starts the chain: someone now owns a little of him (a shoplifted item, a hold-up at a petrol station, errands for the local boss) | What is his first crime, and whom does it put him in debt to? |
| The code at the table | Puts the value he would die for beside his crimes, in one scene | Where do family love and an order to kill share a scene? |
| Deal by deal | Turns desire into a ladder he can count; every deal leaves a debt behind | What does each deal give him, and who owes whom afterwards? |
| Money made visible | Delivers the promised thrill in concrete excess, to be turned sour later | Which image of wealth comes back spoiled, or at his death? |
| The ranked gang | Multiplies his strength, and supplies the men who will betray him | What does each ally's rank give him reason to do: stay, or switch? |
| Six fronts | Packs the story with attacks from every side | Which fronts are empty, and would filling one add pressure or only noise? When does each strike? |
| Turncoats | Each switch is a reveal and a new conflict, rooted in the brittleness of power held by force | Who switches, when, and was it in him from the start? |
| Pulled back | Makes the no-exit rule felt: he tries to leave and is dragged back | Does he ever try to get out, and what hauls him back? |
| Tactics, not ethics | Keeps moral debate out of the middle; the judgement is left to the audience or an ignored outsider | Is he agonising where he should be planning? Who outside the gang says it is wrong? |
| Quarrel to war | Escalation: each killing answers the last, until a business grudge has become war between gangs | Is each step caused by the one before, and where does pressure ease before the next? |
| The reckoning | The climax as his method pushed to its limit: mass killing or ruin; if he wins, a sign it will not last | If he wins, what tells the audience it will not hold? |
| The blind spot | No self-revelation: he excuses everything as protecting his family; the insight goes to someone else or to the audience | Who learns what he cannot, and how does his last act show his blindness? |
| The successor | He dies, or wins with his soul gone, and someone as bad waits; an image planted early returns | What early image comes back at the end, and who is next? |

**The shape that holds them** *(built)*: two lines going opposite ways, wealth and rank rising, humanity falling. Choose early which of Truby's three endings you are planting towards (death, a win so costly it is a loss, or bondage). In *Scarface* the hero dies under the sign that once seemed to promise him the world.

## 7. The genre's question

Truby's picture of life here, his *Mind-Action story view* *(asserted)*: the old American hope joined spiritual freedom to material plenty; the gangster drops the first half and keeps the money, won by organised force. His *thematic recipe* (a genre's plan for living well), the *Way of Destruction* in his term, in gist: money mistaken for freedom ends in ruin. He pushes it further, to a distrust of wanting as such *(asserted; section 13)*.

**As a question for a draft:** Truby's key question sets the city against the soul. Write it as "how does he win *this* without losing *that*?", with both blanks concrete (the city and his brother; the business and his daughter's trust), and check that the ending answers it (Gap principle 7: a theme is the story's answer to the question its world poses). By Implied World principle 8 the main departure should embody the question; in the higher forms, the respectable world revealed as a gang is the question made into a place.

**Where the moral argument lives.** In the knowledge gap: what the audience sees against what he admits *(built)*. He settled his conscience before the story began, so when he and his men meet, they weigh what will work, and the case against him has to come from elsewhere. Cut the scenes of agonising; let an outsider (a wife, a priest) make the case and be ignored; let the audience judge.

## 8. Where the care comes from

This is the genre where the Bond theory's **alignment beats virtue** (principle 2) does the most work: the audience sides with whoever it spends time with and understands, often whatever they have done.

**Case.** Tony Soprano beats people and has them killed, and *The Sopranos* never asks the audience to approve. It gives them something else: years in his company, a therapist's room where they hear how he thinks, and a household he cannot rule. Viewers stayed. Patricia Highsmith does the same in prose with Tom Ripley, a small-time con man who kills a rich young man and takes his identity: kept close to Ripley's mind, readers want him to get away with it. **Point.** In Gangster, allegiance is built from alignment, not approval. Truby's own two reasons why audiences root for the gangster (he drives the story, and he is superb at getting what everyone wants) are alignment and Competence under other names *(built: each can be removed and its effect watched)*. Decide how far you want the audience on his side, then set those two, and Interiority, to match.

The levers:
- **Competence, Agency, Desire** run high: he is good at the thing everyone wants, he always acts, and his want is concrete and escalating (Bond principle 3: his wanting gives the audience something to want on his behalf).
- **Connection** does the work **Decency** cannot. Decency is kept near zero; his love for his family gives him stakes, and their love for him vouches for him (Bond principle 4: turn one lever down, turn another up). Hence the genre's signature pairing, the christening or the family dinner beside the killing.
- **Interiority**, the most powerful lever and the most dangerous (therapy, voice-over, a close narrator), is paired in this genre with no insight. Henry Hill's narration in *Goodfellas* is candid about the life and blind about himself (the workshop's reading): access to his mind builds allegiance, and the missing self-revelation leaves the judgement with the audience.
- **Vulnerability** comes from enemies on every side and certain betrayal, and, in what Truby calls the *king of everyday*, from a ruler who cannot govern his own house: Tony can hit his son but cannot make him turn out well.
- **Specificity**: clothes, rooms, flaunted money. **Humour** and charm offset cruelty, in doses; constant jokes undercut the stakes.

**Care through others** (Bond principle 5). How he treats those below him in the ranks tells us who he is; who loves him (a wife, a brother) vouches for him, and withdraws the vouching when they turn away. When the central figure cannot change, Truby's *observer hero* can carry the care: Nick in *The Great Gatsby* admires Gatsby, and we come to care through him, as we do for Holmes through Watson *(built)*.

**The line.** Some acts end allegiance for most audiences; time and interiority stretch that line a long way, not for ever (Bond theory, the limits of care). The higher forms' moral staircase, each act worse and each with a business excuse (*Mad Men*), walks the audience towards it. Know where you want each step to land. Long series build the **Miss** rung deepest, one reason the Gangster Epic has thrived on television (the workshop's reading).

## 9. Suspense and knowledge

**Uncertainty is the weak term.** Suspense = Care × Threat × Uncertainty × Time. The debt and the closing vice (Truby likens it to Film Noir's) make the fall feel fated, so "whether" is nearly settled. The Anticipation theory allows this: uncertainty can move from whether to how (Part 2, section 1; *Chronicle of a Death Foretold*). Supply it: how, when, at whose hand, and who goes down with him. Make the price of leaving explicit and fatal early, then let him try once and be dragged back, so the vice is felt rather than told *(built)*. **Threat inside the refuge** (Anticipation principle 4): the family, the wedding, the table, the car. The genre's safe places are where the hit comes, which is why their safety is the guarantee worth breaking (section 4).

**The ratchet** (Anticipation principle 7). Truby's middle is violence that only speeds up, from six fronts at once. Tension that only rises goes numb, so build partial releases between the steps: a truce, a wedding, a deal, a meal. The release is also the classic moment for the hit: *The Godfather* cuts between a christening and the killings Michael has ordered (see `.claude/skills/plot/references/scenes.md` on crosscutting).

**Knowledge position** (Anticipation principle 6). Truby's betrayal reveal hides a rival's real strength from hero and audience alike until it is exposed *(built, but offering only one position)*. That buys surprise and gives up suspense; the owner theory makes it a choice, scene by scene. A middle way is to show the rule and hide the traitor (Anticipation principle 3): in *The Godfather* the old don tells Michael that whoever proposes a meeting with the rival boss will be the traitor, and from then on every lieutenant is watched through that rule (the workshop's example). Each switch of side opens a gap the audience did not know was there (Gap principle 4); by Bond principle 7 it lands best when the treachery was in the man all along, so plant it.

**Settlement and residue.** The ending leaves the audience knowing what the hero will not admit, dramatic irony as meaning (Gap principle 7). The successor and a system that outlives the man keep the threat alive after the story (Anticipation principle 8); in the higher forms the threat is the audience's own economy, so residue comes built in. An ending that leaves his fate open, as *The Sopranos* did, is kept or broken by emphasis (Gap principle 5): the harder the story pressed the question, the more an answer never given reads as betrayal. More: `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md` and `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`.

## 10. Subgenres and mixes

**Truby's three higher forms** *(a classification, asserted)*:
- **The Gangster Epic**: its job is to let one gang's history carry a country's, so that each big turn in the family's fortunes echoes one in the nation's (*The Godfather* I and II, *Boardwalk Empire*, *Peaky Blinders*).
- **The *Eastern*** (his term): the climb told through a legal climber, where the cost is the self, not a bullet (*The Great Gatsby*, *Mad Men*). Truby pairs it with the Western as the same national story told falling instead of rising *(asserted)*. *The Talented Mr Ripley* is a near relation (the workshop's reading): a self invented through a stolen identity, as with Don Draper.
- **The Economic-Political Epic**: here the machine is the subject, and its job is to make it felt through one person caught in the place where money buys power and power protects money (*The Big Short*, *Network*). Truby's warning *(built)*: never split business from politics. In a business story, find where the law or the politician comes in; in a political story, find who is paying.

**Neighbours.** Truby groups Gangster with Crime, Detective and Thriller. It is Crime scaled up *(asserted)*, and the sorting question tells the two apart: if the middle hunts a criminal already known, Crime leads (`crime.md`); if it follows a rise and fall inside a criminal organisation, Gangster does. In Truby's picture the detective and the gangster are the Western hero's good and bad sons, working the same city with opposite answers, truth and money (`detective-and-thriller.md`; *The Departed* plants a policeman and a gangster each inside the other's side). The Western is the parent: its motion is outward across open land, Gangster's is upward through a city *(asserted; a lens more than an argument)* (`western.md`).

**Mixes.** *Myth and Fairy Tale*: *The Godfather* borrows the fairy tale's three brothers, the youngest the hero, so each son tests a way to lead (the brawler fails, the adopted negotiator standing in for the gentle middle brother fails, and only Michael joins toughness to cunning) *(built: siblings facing one task compare strategies side by side)*. *Horror for one scene*: the hospital, where Michael realises killers are coming for his father (`horror.md`). *Love inside the money climb*: in *Gatsby*, setting a love story within a chase for money makes Daisy the spoiled promise itself *(built)* (`love-story.md`). *Fantasy*: Truby reads *Game of Thrones* as a mob drama in a royal court (`fantasy.md`). *Comedy* (*Get Shorty*) and *Black Comedy* (*Network*; the self-revelation denied) (`comedy.md`). For choosing which genre leads, see `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`.

## 11. Twisting it

Truby's general routes above a genre are in `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`. For Gangster his main route is to stop treating gangsters as a sideline and show business and politics as the same gangsterism (section 10) *(asserted)*. He calls it the hardest form to write, because a vast system has to be felt through one life. **Test:** if the system is explained rather than felt through one person's choices, the story reads as an essay. His chapter's own moves, restated:
- **One move at two sizes** *(built, from one film)*: cut between a small version and a large one, so the audience sees the small as the seed of the large. *The Godfather Part II* sets Vito's neighbourhood rise against Michael's dealings with corporations and a dictator.
- **Freedom into slavery** *(built)*: the usual story runs from bondage towards freedom; *Goodfellas* starts with a hero who adores the life, so it runs the other way.
- **Flip the reckoning, hand on the revelation**: keep the price promised in section 2 and change its form: a win that costs him his soul (*The Godfather*), testimony against his friends and a dull suburban exile (*Goodfellas*), a sacrament or a courtroom in place of the gunfight; give the insight to a witness who can see (Kay), or let the system swallow it (*Mad Men* ends with the hero's retreat turned into an advert). See `.claude/skills/character/references/change-and-growth.md`, sections 7 and 9.
- **The king of everyday**: a ruler whose power cannot fix his own home (*The Sopranos*), or an everyman whose job reaches the whole nation (the salesman, the ad man).
- **Everyone trapped, nobody the villain** *(built)*: a prisoner's dilemma, where each side's sensible self-interest ruins both, as with owners and miners in *How Green Was My Valley*. Show the land blackening as the cost piles up, and make the characters depend on the thing that will kill them.
- **Give the argument a body** *(built)*: Truby's counter-images to Adam Smith's invisible hand (a fist that beats the worker, a mouth that eats and leaves waste) turn an economic case into something the audience can see, as the valley of ashes does in *Gatsby*. Business avoids open battle, so also find where its violence lands, often as a death at one remove that the powerful walk away from (Gatsby is shot; Tom and Daisy go abroad), and put it on the page.

**One idea from the owner theories.** The successor beat is Implied World principle 4 (indifference) turned into an ending. Run the theory's test on the draft: if the gangster died in chapter one, what would the city be doing next week? In Gangster the answer should be: the same business, under someone else. Build that business before the hero needs it (rackets, rivals, payments that run with or without him), and the last image of the successor stops being a moral tag and becomes proof that the world was never about him. **Swap test:** replace the successor with a clean sweep (the police break the gang for good). If the ending loses its chill, the successor was doing the work.

## 12. Examples

- *Scarface* (1932), *The Public Enemy*, *Little Caesar*: the founding beats; a small first crime; a sign planted early that returns at the death.
- *The Godfather* and *Part II*: three brothers as ways to lead; turncoats; killings cut against a christening; the insight passed to Kay; one move at two sizes, father and son across decades.
- *Goodfellas*: freedom into slavery; narration candid about the life and blind about the self; testimony in place of a gunfight.
- *The Sopranos* and *The Talented Mr Ripley*: alignment without approval, carried by access to a killer's mind; the king of everyday; an open ending.
- *Peaky Blinders*, *City of God*, *Un Prophète*: the genre moved to places of their own.
- *The Great Gatsby* and *Mad Men*: the Eastern; the observer hero; the valley of ashes; a moral staircase; a revelation turned into product.
- *Network*, *Glengarry Glen Ross*, *The Big Short*, *How Green Was My Valley*: the system as the gang.

## 13. Rivals and doubts

- **Contempt, or grief earned by alignment.** *Truby:* the audience should end in contempt, not pity, whatever it enjoyed on the way, because a crowned man in a republic is a usurper and his fall carries no tragic weight *(asserted)*. *Bond theory:* the time and access that made the audience root for him (principles 1 and 2) will make his fall hurt, whatever it is supposed to feel. *Test:* keep the same fall; in one version give the audience long access to his mind first, in the other watch him only from outside. Truby's claim predicts scorn in both; the theory predicts grief in the first. In practice treat "not tragic" as a choice: for satisfaction, hollow him out and show the harm before the end; for grief, give him something real to lose.
- **Give up desire, or share it.** Truby's recipe ends by warning against desire itself; Bond principle 3 says wanting drives attention. The warning is a theme, not a craft rule: the genre works by making the audience share a want the ending condemns. *Test:* cool the hero's want to reduce the audience's complicity; the theory predicts a flatter story, not a wiser one.
- **Speeding violence, or the ratchet.** Truby's middle only accelerates; Anticipation principle 7 says tension that only rises goes numb. *Test:* in a middle of five escalating hits, put a truce or a wedding before the fourth. The theory predicts the fourth lands harder.
- **Hidden betrayer, or shown.** Truby hides the rival's strength from hero and audience; Anticipation principle 6 makes it a choice. *Test:* show the audience the traitor one scene early. The theory predicts suspense in every scene he shares with the hero, where Truby's version buys one surprise.
- **A cast the size of a society.** The higher forms want a large cast that stands for the whole society; Bond theory warns that too many characters dilute care, so keep a small inner circle (`.claude/skills/character/references/building-a-cast.md`). *Test:* keep the large cast and spread the audience's time evenly across it, or keep it and give most of the time to an inner circle of three or four. Both versions stand for the society; the theory predicts care only in the second.
- **Doubts.** Calling every bad ruler, monopolist and dictator a gangster makes the category wide enough to swallow any story, which by `hard-to-vary`'s standard means it explains little. Much of the chapter's second half is economic and political argument (capitalism as a faith, the free market as a utopia that never existed): Truby's opinions, outside craft. Take the story tools (the broken ladder, the debt, the six fronts, the turncoats, the death at one remove, the swallowed revelation), not the politics. The basic beats are fitted from a small set of American films, and the split between immigrant Catholics and a Protestant middle class belongs to their period. "The hardest genre to write" and "the darkest view of life of any genre" are rankings with no test.
