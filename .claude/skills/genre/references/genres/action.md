# Action

*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Genres, chapter 3, in the workshop's own words; framed by the owner theories (Implied World principles 2, 4 and 8; Gap principles 2, 4, 5 and 7; Anticipation principles 1, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7; Bond levers, chiefly Agency, Competence, Flaw and Connection, principles 5 and 6, question 4 and the limits of care).*

Contents: 1 In one line. 2 The promise. 3 The baseline. 4 Guarantees, and which to break. 5 Hero and opponent. 6 The beats, as jobs. 7 The genre's question. 8 Where the care comes from. 9 Suspense and knowledge. 10 Subgenres and mixes. 11 Twisting it. 12 Examples. 13 Rivals and doubts.

## 1. In one line

A skilled fighter beats an outside opponent by force and cunning, and the story counts what the win cost: in Truby's view, Action is the genre that says a person is what they do (his *Existential Code*).

## 2. The promise

The audience comes for:
- **Standing to fight.** The hero fights rather than runs; they may run in order to fight later, never to give up. This is close to the genre's definition: drop it for good and the story has left Action.
- **Mastery under pressure.** Watching someone very good at fighting work when it matters, above all when the plan falls apart and they improvise.
- **Blow answered by blow.** Two near-matched sides trading attacks, each move forcing a reply (Truby's *punch-counterpunch*).
- **Speed.** One urgent goal against constant resistance. Truby calls Action the straightest line of any story shape (*asserted*).
- **A final battle** that is the biggest, fastest and tightest fight in the story, ending in a win the audience can see and that buys the hero freedom to act as they choose.

In Gap theory terms the breach (what breaks the world's normal) is bodily danger to a community, and the open question the story presses hardest is *how* the hero will win. That question is a mystery (Gap principle 5: an open question the story promises to answer): the audience has been promised a reckoning with the main opponent, in person. A draft that dodges it (the opponent dies offstage, or by accident) breaks the promise. If someone else lands the last blow, they need to carry a hero's line of their own, as Furiosa does in *Mad Max: Fury Road*. Standing to fight, a win the audience can see and the freedom it buys are promises of the same kind, things the genre undertakes to deliver: breaking one reads as betrayal (Gap principle 5), not as a broken guarantee (section 4). They can be twisted instead (section 11).

## 3. The baseline

Before anything is said, an Action audience assumes: a community in bodily danger that only force can meet; violence as a normal, legitimate tool; clear sides; weapons as the world's key technology; no justice in the courtroom sense, only winners and losers (bringing someone to account is Crime's business, see `crime.md`); status won by performance; and a hero whose body, however stretched, obeys this world's physics (the last is the workshop's inference, not Truby's). Truby notes that as guns grew deadlier, the Action hero shifted from protector of the weak to professional killer (*fitted*, a trend).

Riding on these defaults lets an Action story spend its attention budget (Implied World principle 2: the audience's limited attention) on the arena (where the fighting happens), the plan and the opponents instead of on why force is needed. It is also where Action most often goes wrong.

**Case.** A city has gangs because the hero needs gangs to shoot. Remove the hero and the gangs have no reason to exist: no trade they run, no street they grew up on, nothing they want next week. Truby calls Action built this way a *false form*: the world is shaped backwards from the violent fix (*built*). Set that beside his note on the American Civil War, where the side with the better generals lost to an economy and a blockade: that world had business of its own, and it settled things.

**Point.** Test the danger with Implied World principle 4 (indifference): if the hero died in chapter one, what would the danger be doing next week? Give it roots (money, politics, history), and let at least one force the hero cannot fight (supply, weather, terrain) decide something. For the full repair of a generic world, hand to `.claude/skills/story-world/references/diagnosing-a-world.md`.

**Arena.** Truby splits Action arenas into the *enclosure* and the *chase* (*built*, if a little tidy). An enclosure makes pressure by itself (his *pressure cooker*). A chase gives that up for speed, so it has to find pressure elsewhere: a clock, a closing distance, a vehicle coming apart.

## 4. Guarantees, and which to break

A guarantee is an unspoken protection the audience trusts the genre to honour (Anticipation principle 5); while they trust it, some part of them relaxes. Action's protect people: the hero comes through alive, so does the team's inner circle, and the hero's side wins the final battle. Truby does not discuss guarantees directly; the list is the workshop's, drawn from his examples.

Trusted, these leave the audience asking *how* the hero wins rather than *whether* anyone they care about is at risk (Anticipation, Part 2, section 1), and, in the workshop's extension from the Gap theory's settlement, what the win will cost.

**Case.** In *Seven Samurai* the defenders win, but most of the samurai die, and Truby puts much of the film's power down to that. *Mad Max: Fury Road* orders its deaths upward, from minor members of the team to the tyrant's lieutenants to the tyrant: early losses on the hero's side make later threats believable, and each death matters more than the one before.

**Point.** The guarantees Truby's examples break: the team survives (it does not); the hero survives (a noble self-sacrifice for the group, Truby's *beau geste*). Two moves that look like breaks are twists on a promise instead (section 11): the win that costs the hero their goodness, and the sports story where the hero loses the match and wins inwardly. Truby's first route to lifting the genre, showing loss, is Anticipation principle 5 at work: break one convincingly and keep the rest. The workshop adds, from the Anticipation theory's advice to break a guarantee early: a loss in the middle, not only in the final battle, makes every later threat real.

## 5. Hero and opponent

**Hero.** The warrior: a master fighter who acts fast and needs to be the best (Truby's *will to greatness*). Variants: in the war epic, often an ordinary person who does not want the job and must grow into it; in sports, the athlete; in the caper (a heist story), the leader whose plan is the real draw. Truby finds that female-led Action keeps the same beats and changes how each one plays (*fitted*).

**Flaw and need.** The flaw (Truby's *weakness*) grows out of the hero's strength (*built*: each gift casts a shadow); in Action, self-reliance hardens into refusing help. Typical forms (*fitted*, and bound to the culture of the films): pride and contempt for the weak; force as the first answer (Truby's *will to violence*); living by others' judgement, where failure means disgrace (his *shame culture*); and winning each fight while losing the war (tactics over strategy, in the ordinary military sense). The need points away from success and towards other people. A strong story knows whether the hero gets there.

**Desire.** Success, glory and freedom, aimed at one finish line the audience can see crossed. Truby calls it the sharpest goal of any genre hero (*asserted*); why it matters is *built*: a visible end point lets the audience measure progress.

**Opponent.** Someone the hero can fight in person, strong and dangerous, who wants the hero dead or caged. The best is a *nemesis* (Truby's term): the opponent best placed to strike at this hero's deepest flaw, often the second-best fighter in the story (*built*: the outer fight then tests the inner flaw). Better still is a web of opponents, which Truby builds with his *four-point opposition* (*built*, though the number four is only a convenient shape): opponents who strike from different sides, and whose hidden links come out as reveals. What to check: does each attack a different part of the hero (body, plan, friends, beliefs)? Swap two opponents' attacks; if nothing in the story changes, one of them is doing no work. For opponents in general, see `.claude/skills/character/references/opponents.md`.

## 6. The beats, as jobs

A beat here is a recurring story event that does a particular job. These are *fitted*: drawn from past stories, mostly Hollywood and Kurosawa. A missing beat is a question, not a fault: ask whether the draft gets the job done another way. The rows are the workshop's own grouping, not Truby's list. Test any beat with `hard-to-vary`: remove it, and see whether another scene still does its job.

| Beat | The job it does | If the draft lacks it, ask... |
|---|---|---|
| Crisis first | Shows who the hero is by what they do under threat, before anything is explained (Competence and Agency; Bond question 4, when do we first see them act?); built as a chain of narrow escapes, it previews the story's rhythm. Truby lets it stand apart from the main plot, which is what lets care come first: the normal, and what could be lost, still come before the main breach (section 13) | Where does the audience first see the hero act in danger, and what exactly did they learn from it? Is this crisis the main breach, and if so, where did we see what could be lost? |
| Force is needed | Puts a community in bodily danger, which justifies a fighter and sets the price of losing | Why can only force meet this danger, and would the danger exist without the hero? |
| Stand, not run | Poses the genre's question and shows the hero's answer | Is there a moment when running is the sensible choice, and does staying cost something visible? |
| The costly strength | Plants the flaw that grows from the hero's gift, and the need | What does the hero's main strength cost them with other people? |
| A finish line | Gives a goal whose winning can be seen | Can you write the moment of winning as one observable event, and what freedom does it buy? |
| Opponents in layers | Brings attacks from several sides, and reveals when hidden links come out | Does each opponent hit a different part of the hero, and which link between them does the audience learn late? |
| Where skill comes from | Training shows the source of the expertise the audience will enjoy and teaches them what the hero can do; in team stories each recruit adds a skill the plan needs | If the hero's skill is a key pleasure, where did it come from, and what friction (a hated teacher, an inner block) does learning it carry? |
| The plan on show | Gives the audience a model to predict against, so every slip registers | Who holds the intricate plan, hero or opponent, and does the audience see enough of it to notice when it goes wrong? |
| Strike and decide | The middle's cycle: a surprise attack (or an ally unmasked as an opponent), then a decision | Does each surprise force a choice, and does the opponent ever make a clever move of their own, such as a feint? |
| The plan breaks | Improvisation, which Truby ranks above every other beat as the clearest proof of mastery (*built*) | Where does the plan fail, and does the fix use skills already shown in a way nobody predicted? |
| The line crossed | Shows what the hero does wrong to win: being great against being good | Is there a point where winning costs the hero their decency, and does the ending count it? |
| The funnel | The final battle: every line meets in the tightest space, the pace is the fastest in the story, and it narrows to hero against main opponent, won by wit as well as force | Does every earlier thread meet here, does the pace rise, and does the hero out-think as well as out-fight? |
| What they see | The self-revelation, the moment the hero sees the truth about themselves, paying off their inner line (a track of inner change set beside the outer fight) | What does the hero realise about themselves, and was it planted in the first act? |
| Stay or go | Answers "stand or run" with "join or leave": does the loner enter the community, leave it, or stay to lead it? | Does the hero's choice about belonging answer their flaw? |

## 7. The genre's question

Truby's view, restated: a person is what they do, and life is a contest over scarce things in which one side wins and the other loses. Action judges people by whether they win, not by whether they are right. In his order of genres it comes after Horror: once survival is settled, what follows is the everyday contest with other people. Its moral argument runs through the hero's methods, where greatness and goodness pull apart (*built*: the theme is carried by the means of pursuit, not by speeches). **Test for a draft:** what did winning cost the hero's decency, and does the ending count it (the beat *The line crossed*)?

Truby calls Action's *thematic recipe* (a genre's plan for living well) the *Way of the Warrior*. In gist: stand, act, and watch your means. As a question for a draft:

**Will you stand and fight for what you hold, and what will winning make of you?**

The Gap theory says a theme is the story's answer to the question its world poses, and Implied World principle 8 says the world's main departure should embody that question. **Case.** *Witness* puts a police officer whose trade is force among pacifists, so the setting itself asks whether force is the answer, and every fight replies. **Point.** Build the world so that it asks "fight or run?", let the final act answer it, and make sure any tainted means are paid for in the settlement (Gap principle 7: how the ending closes the story's gaps).

## 8. Where the care comes from

Of the Bond theory's ten levers (the things a story can turn up or down to make the audience's relationship with a character easier or harder to build), the Action hero runs high on **Competence** (the pleasure of skill, peaking when they improvise) and **Agency** (they always act), with a sharp **Desire**. **Vulnerability** comes from bad odds and wounds, and in the war epic from the reluctant hero's doubt. **Humour** grounds a hero weighed down by saving the world, but in doses: Bond theory warns that constant quips undercut the stakes.

The lever deliberately left low is **Connection**: the hero is a loner, and the ending may raise it. **Interiority** is usually low as well, since the hero is stoic, which is one reason Action self-revelations so often go missing. **Flaw** is present in the strongest examples and absent in weak ones.

Two Bond principles do extra work here. *Care is social* (principle 5): the respect of allies vouches for a cold hero, as when Furiosa acknowledges Max. *Indifference is the enemy* (principle 6): a nemesis works as a person with levers of their own (competence, specificity) and fails as a function. Watch two limits. Competence pushed too far kills suspense (Bond's Superman problem; Truby notes that a superhero, good by definition, leaves only the question of method). And a team of specialists thins care unless the audience bonds with a small inner circle (see `.claude/skills/character/references/building-a-cast.md`). Action builds the **Miss** rung (the audience would grieve if they were gone) through time with the team: training, missions shared. Losing a teammate spends it, so build it first, and make the death mean something for the one who dies, not only as fuel for the hero (Bond theory, the limits of care).

## 9. Suspense and knowledge

**Suspense over curiosity.** Action's main gap is about the future: how will the hero win? Show the opponent early and put the weight on the fight. If the draft's main question has become *who* the real opponent is, curiosity has taken over and the story is a Thriller (see `detective-and-thriller.md`).

**The four terms** (Anticipation principle 1). In the workshop's reading, *threat* is Action's strongest term and *care* often its weakest, because the genre rushes to the breach: show the normal, and what could be lost, first (an opening crisis can come before that, as long as it is not the main breach; see section 13). *Uncertainty* peaks when the odds are bad but not hopeless, so near-matched sides with the hero slightly behind beat a hero who plainly outclasses everyone. *Time* pulls against the genre's love of speed: a story that never slows gives suspense no room to build. A clock, crosscutting between lines of action and a win in the last seconds all spend time well.

**Show the rules** (principle 3). Training and a plan the audience has seen give them a model to predict against, so each slip makes suspense. **Lost refuge** (principle 4). The enclosure is a refuge under siege: the fortress that should keep people safe is where the threat gets in.

**Knowledge position** (principle 6; the Gap theory's knowledge gap): whether the audience knows more than, the same as, or less than the characters, chosen scene by scene. **Case.** In *Rocky*'s meat-locker scene, the champion's trainer and the audience see the danger while the champion ignores it: the audience is ahead of the opponent. **Point.** A feint can let the audience in on the fake (ahead of its target: suspense) or fool them along with the target (behind the one faking: a surprise, in Gap terms, when it lands). In war stories, a view from the commanders tends to put the audience ahead; a view from the ranks puts them level.

**The ratchet** (principle 7). Blow answered by blow gives rise and partial release. Truby's warning about *hitting the same beat*, repeating one kind of exchange so conflict grows but plot does not, is the audience learning the rhythm. His cure (the "Opponents in layers" beat) breaks it: attacks from new directions, and hidden opponents whose links come out as scheduled reveals (the withholding gap, Gap principle 4).

For the general tools, see `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md` and `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`.

## 10. Subgenres and mixes

The subgenre names are Truby's; the promise and the job or risk given for each are the workshop's.
- **War epic.** One person or family decides, or stands for, a nation's fate (Truby's definition). It promises scale: forces no fighter can beat (supply, terrain, a society in change) shape the battles, and the choice between a view from the commanders and one from the ranks sets what the audience can know (section 9). Its risk is drift when the story travels; a destination named early is a promise (Gap principle 5) that gives the wandering a direction.
- **Suicide mission.** A trained team against one objective the war turns on, with the odds declared hopeless (see section 13 for what that does to uncertainty). Its job is to make a unit out of separate specialists, so that the plan breaking on the night hurts. Truby pairs it with the heist.
- **Caper (heist).** Truby reads it as a game played against the system. Its job is to make thinking visible: the audience follows the plan, so every departure from it registers (section 9).
- **Sports.** It promises a bounded field, a score and rules everyone can see, so progress can be measured exactly. Its risk is that the scoreboard settles everything; cheating as the moral test, and an inner win, give it a second measure (section 11).
- **Buddy story.** Action crossed with Love Story, often with Comedy. The pair's friction is the care engine: how they argue shows how well they fit.

**Mixes.** With Crime, the hero often works by gut and the opponent holds the intricate plan (`crime.md`). With Thriller, both share mortal danger, but Thriller asks who and Action asks how (`detective-and-thriller.md`). With Horror, the prey can become the fighter across a series, as Ripley and Sarah Connor do; Horror's bondage is inside, Action's is imposed from outside (`horror.md`). With Myth, Truby puts superheroes on Myth's side, and Myth frees the hero by growing rather than by fighting (`myth.md`). The Western is the parent form (`western.md`). With Love come the joining ending and the buddy pair (`love-story.md`). When genres meet, decide which question leads and which guarantees each one brings; see `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`.

## 11. Twisting it

Truby's general routes above a genre are in `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`. His Action chapter's own routes, restated:
- **Widen it** into the war epic: a nation's fate at stake, the craft of war studied, losses on both sides.
- **Shrink it** into a game (caper or sports): strip away variables until the audience can see what really makes someone win.
- **Show loss.** Push towards tragedy so the price of winning is felt; he treats this as the main route.
- **Deepen both sides.** Give the opponent a case that is partly right, so that beating them means more, and give the hero an inner line from the first act, so the self-revelation has something to pay off.
- **Twist a promise** (section 2) rather than break it: the hero wins and loses their goodness, so the win is visible but not clean; or, in sports, the hero loses the match and wins inwardly, so the win is real but the scoreboard does not show it.

**From the owner theories.** Make the danger indifferent and thematic at once (Implied World principles 4 and 8): a danger with business of its own that also embodies fight-or-run. **Case.** In *Fury Road* the tyrant rations water. His rule would go on whether or not Max had turned up, and it makes freedom a question of who controls a necessity, so the chase out and back answers the story's question in the world's own terms.

## 12. Examples

- *Seven Samurai*: the final battle as the genre in small; a win bought with most of the defenders.
- *Raiders of the Lost Ark*: an opening crisis built as a chain of escapes that sets the film's rhythm.
- *Die Hard*: a high fortress arena; weapons made from what is to hand; a hero both quick in the moment and planning the campaign.
- *Mad Max: Fury Road*: a straight line out and back; a nemesis; deaths ordered upward; the loner walking away.
- *Speed*: the chase arena, with a clock doing the fortress's job of pressure.
- *Rocky*: training that shifts who knows the danger; an inner win that outranks the judges.
- *Witness*: a man of force among pacifists, so the world poses the question.
- *The Dirty Dozen*: a team whose flaws become the skills the mission needs.
- *Saving Private Ryan*: a humane goal that costs more lives than it saves.
- *The Thomas Crown Affair* (1968): the caper as a game against the system, chosen over love.
- *The Natural*: the last-second win from behind.
- *Aliens*, *Terminator 2*: Horror's prey become Action's fighters.
- *John Wick*: the gun as the world's key technology, the hero as professional killer.

## 13. Rivals and doubts

- **What stalls a middle.** *Truby* blames delay: an elaborate set piece (a long, staged action sequence) whose result is put off freezes the plot, and knowing a final battle is coming makes earlier fights feel like stalling. *Owner theories:* a delayed outcome is the Time term, which feeds suspense; but tension that only rises goes numb, and a rhythm the audience has learned stops working (Anticipation principle 7). The workshop's reading is that what kills a middle is flat uncertainty and sameness, not delay. *Test:* lengthen one set piece twice, adding no new plot fact: once by repeating the same exchange, once in ratchet steps where the odds shift (a loss, a new route, a partial release). Truby predicts both stall; the owner frame predicts only the first does. For the final-battle half, give an earlier fight a stake that can be lost for good; the owner frame predicts it stops feeling like a stall. *Speed*'s bus sequence is a case to try it on.
- **Crisis first, or care first.** Truby opens on the hero in danger; the Anticipation theory wants care built before the threat arrives, by showing the normal before the breach. They fit when the opening crisis is not the main breach, which Truby allows. *Test:* write two openings, one whose crisis is the main threat, one with a crisis unconnected to it followed by the normal. The theory predicts the main breach lands harder in the second.
- **"Certain death."** The suicide mission declares that nobody expects to come back; Anticipation principle 1 says suspense collapses once hope is gone. *Test:* write the mission twice, once near-hopeless with one thin route, once certain with none. The owner frame predicts the second holds only if the uncertainty moves to who survives and how.
- **The epic hero and an indifferent world.** Truby defines the epic by one person deciding a nation's fate; Implied World principle 4 wants a world with business of its own. His own Civil War example sides with the theory. *Test:* remove the hero; if the war has no course of its own, the world is set dressing.
- **The purely evil opponent.** Truby praises a monstrous tyrant (Immortan Joe) as an ideal nemesis, then calls a purely evil opponent always an error in the epic. Bond principle 6 draws the line elsewhere: person against function. *Test:* keep an opponent wholly evil but make them specific and highly competent. If the audience badly wants them beaten and the story does not feel artificial, Bond's line holds, and a defensible case is one way to make a person, not the only way.
- **The joking hero.** Truby's epic hero jokes under fire; Bond theory warns that constant quips kill worry. *Test:* cut half the jokes in the tensest sequence; if fear rises, there were too many.
- **Loose claims.** "The straightest story shape" and "the sharpest goal" are *asserted* (sections 2 and 5). The beats are *fitted* to mostly Hollywood and Kurosawa films: question them for other traditions.
