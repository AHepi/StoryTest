# Horror

*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Genres, chapter 2, in the workshop's own words; framed by the owner theories (Anticipation theory throughout, above all the fear ladder and principles 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8; Implied World principles 1, 3, 4 and 8; Gap theory principles 2, 5 and 7; Bond theory's levers and principles 2, 5 and 6).*

Contents: 1 In one line. 2 The promise. 3 The baseline. 4 Guarantees, and which to break. 5 Hero and opponent. 6 The beats, as jobs. 7 The genre's question. 8 Where the care comes from. 9 Suspense and knowledge. 10 Subgenres and mixes. 11 Twisting it. 12 Examples. 13 Rivals and doubts.

## 1. In one line

Horror sets a monster on a hero whose own flaw let it in, and closes in on her until death stops being something that happens to other people.

Words kept apart in this file: *Horror*, with a capital, is the genre; *the horror rung* is the top step of the Anticipation theory's fear ladder, where the audience sees the thing. The *monster* is the genre's opponent, the *threat* of the suspense equation. Truby's own summary *(asserted)*: Horror stages the one divide with no middle ground, alive or dead, as the story of a mind that fails.

## 2. The promise

From Truby's account the audience comes for four things *(mostly asserted)*: **the squeeze** (Truby's *pressure*; the workshop keeps that word for the Gap theory's principle 3), the hero's room to move (space, allies, body, sanity) shrinking scene by scene, easing briefly and then pressing harder, until none is left; **nightmares with her name on them**, each reveal hitting one of her own worst fears in a new shape; **death made personal**, which Truby puts as the audience feeling death as its own, now, where in a Detective story it has already happened to someone else; and, in the better examples, **the monster in us**, the discovery that the inhuman thing was inside the humans all along. The Anticipation theory places the fear differently: inside the story the audience fears *for* a character it cares about (when it knows more than she does) or *with* her (when it knows the same), and fears for itself only afterwards, as residue (principle 8; section 13). In Anticipation terms the promise is anticipation itself. The audience does the frightening, so what it is owed is enough to predict with and never enough to feel sure. Gore alone does not keep the promise: a slasher whose victims nobody cares about gives spectacle, not suspense.

## 3. The baseline

The *baseline* (Implied World theory) is what the audience assumes by default: the real world plus the habits of the genre. Say "an old house in the woods" and a Horror audience has furnished it before the first scene is over. Unless told otherwise it assumes *(fitted, from Truby's beats)*:
- **a place that traps the hero**, Truby's *haunted house*, called **the house** here: a hotel, a ship, a town, a city; often a sealed society, a rotten upper class or a cheerful small town hiding rot;
- **an old wrong**, buried and unpaid, that will come up, and **a barrier** between the human world and something else, which will fail;
- **a monster far stronger than the hero**, with rules and costs that can be learned;
- **help that will be cut off**, and **a doubter** who explains everything away until he cannot.

**The real world weighs more here than in most genres** (the workshop's reading). Horror usually spends on one departure, the monster and its rules, and lets the real world carry the rest. That is what makes residue possible (the threat could exist where the audience lives), and also what the genre must beat, because the audience believes in doctors, police and phones. The doubter, Truby's *rational skeptic*, is the tool *(built)*: an ally who puts the audience's objection more strongly than the audience would, offers a sensible explanation, and is overruled by events, so the audience's doubt is spent for it. A related pattern *(fitted, from four examples)*: open somewhere remote and folk-tale old, where the baseline already allows strangeness, then carry the threat home, where it becomes a lost refuge.

**Generic Horror rides on these defaults.** If the house could be swapped for any spooky house and the doubter for any doubter, the draft is using the baseline's furniture. The fix is a departure of its own (hand its building to `story-world`: `.claude/skills/story-world/SKILL.md`, Building steps 2, 4 and 6) and a house held in place by something only this story has: this hero's flaw, or business of its own. A town or house that exists only for the hero, with nothing going on in it that does not turn on her, is a different fault: not a missing departure but a world with no business of its own (Implied World principle 4, indifference). Hand that to `.claude/skills/story-world/SKILL.md`, Building step 7, and `.claude/skills/story-world/references/diagnosing-a-world.md`, section 5.

## 4. Guarantees, and which to break

A *guarantee* is a protection the audience assumes the genre gives without being told: the star survives, the child is safe. Anticipation principle 5, the broken contract, says fear needs the audience to believe these protections are gone. Break one early and every later threat is real; break them all, so that everyone dies all the time, and care collapses, and suspense with it. Horror is where this bites hardest, because fear is what it sells. Truby does not discuss guarantees directly; the table is the workshop's, drawn from his beats and examples.

| Guarantee the audience leans on | Broken by | What breaking it buys |
|---|---|---|
| The apparent hero survives | *Psycho*, *Hereditary* | Nobody is safe from then on |
| The ally is loyal | Ash in *Alien* | The threat is inside the group |
| The refuge is safe | the last fight, in a refuge that looked safe (*Alien*) | Lost refuge: nowhere left to go |
| An everyday need is harmless | sleep (*A Nightmare on Elm Street*), sound (*A Quiet Place*) | No safe action is left |
| It is over when it seems over (an expected ending kept as a protection for the survivors) | the return (section 6) | Residue |

- **Expectations, not guarantees.** That the killer is someone other than the one the audience sided with, and that the humans are the good side, protect nobody. Overturning either makes the audience reread, not feel unsafe: a twist (section 11), not a broken guarantee.
- **Keep the monster's rules: a promise, not a guarantee.** The rules work like mysteries (Gap principle 5): stated, tested and kept to. A monster that ignores its stated rules breaks a promise, which reads as betrayal, not fear. A rule may turn out to have been misread, if the clues were fair; it may not be dropped for convenience.
- **A break used too often becomes a pattern.** The monster's return after its apparent death is now so expected that audiences wait for it, and a pattern must break before the audience learns it (Anticipation principle 7). The break today may be to withhold the return, or to bring it from a place nobody was watching.
- **Which one to break.** One, early and convincingly, and preferably the one the story's question is about (the workshop's suggestion, after Implied World principle 8): a story about a family breaks the family's safety; a story about trust breaks the ally. Test by removal: restore it (the victim lives) and ask whether later scenes lose their fear. If they do not, the break was doing no work.

## 5. Hero and opponent

**Hero: a victim beaten towards death** *(fitted)*. Two ways in, both about the length of the fall *(built: the fall is the distance from where she starts to where she ends)*: the *overreacher* at the height of success who tries to beat death and falls all the way (*Frankenstein*, *The Fly*, *Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde*), or the *decent everyday person* who stumbles in (*Jaws*, *Alien*, *Pet Sematary*), shown first with a great deal to lose.

**Flaw, need and desire.** The flaw (Truby's *weakness*) has two sides: what it does to her (psychological) and what it makes her do to others (moral). If the moral side cannot be named, the fear has no inner source. Truby's Horror flaw is a mind in chains *(asserted)*: split by basic fears (death above all; being devoured, enslaved, driven mad) and by a forbidden want she has walled off (his *unsociable desires*), and loaded with shame (falling short in other people's eyes) or guilt (breaking a duty to someone close). Both often come through the *ghost*, Truby's term for an old wrong, often a forebear's, never paid for, that keeps striking the hero now. The need: face death and the past, repair the harm, beat the monster inside. The desire: on the surface, escape, then defeat the monster; underneath, defeat death. Escape is a reaction, the weakest kind of want *(built: it holds no plan)*; a hero who only runs gives a flat, passive middle.

**Opponent: the monster.** Truby's *Other-in-the-Extreme*: the Other (the one not like us) pushed as far as it goes, a non-human intruder in human society, far stronger than the hero, working by trickery as much as force. At best it is her *double*, the outer shape of her inner flaw *(built as a way to force growth, since an opponent aimed at her flaw makes her face it; loose as a reading, since almost any opponent can be called a double afterwards)*. Beside it stands the ghost, an inner opponent, so the squeeze comes from both sides at once *(built)*. Truby sorts monsters by what they reduce people to *(fitted)*: *Animal Horror*, becoming body, appetite and lost control; *Machine Horror*, becoming a mechanism with no self, the zombie its purest figure. Use the pair to choose, not to label afterwards, and point the hero's flaw the same way. **Test:** for each trait of the monster, name the part of the hero's flaw it answers. Swap a trait for a neighbour: if nothing about her changes, the trait is decoration. For building either figure, see `.claude/skills/character/references/opponents.md`.

## 6. The beats, as jobs

Left to itself a Horror plot has one beat, the monster attacking, repeated; Truby's unusually long list of Horror beats exists to stop that going stale *(built)*. The beats are fitted, drawn from past stories: a missing beat is a question, not a fault. Ask what job it does, and whether the draft gets that job done some other way. Names and grouping are the workshop's.

| Beat | The job it does | If the draft lacks it, ask... |
|---|---|---|
| The unpaid debt | Plants the ghost: an old wrong that keeps coming back to collect, and drives the plot | What old wrong makes this monster come for this hero, and does it return and grow? |
| The house | Gives her flaw walls she cannot leave, and tightens the squeeze | Why can't she just leave? Could this house be swapped for any other? |
| What she can lose | Sets her starting height, so the fall is long and felt | Did we see what she has to lose before the threat arrived? |
| The walled-off want | Gives the monster a human source: the fear or forbidden want she has shut away | What has she walled off, and where does the wall leak before it breaks? |
| The mirror | Makes the monster her double, so the outer fight carries the inner one | Which trait of hers, pushed further, makes the monster? |
| The doubter | Voices the audience's disbelief and loses to events | What is the audience's likeliest objection, and who says it aloud? |
| The crossing | The one choice that breaches the barrier, letting the monster in and the forbidden want out; after it, costs compound | Where is the crossing, and can she step back after it? |
| The price | Every cheat on death costs something (Truby's *Necessary Cost* law), at least once at others' expense | What does the power that defies death cost, and who pays? |
| Running to scheming | She stops only fleeing and starts improvising a plan: the genre's rare route to growth | Where does she stop running? |
| The siege | The middle: each attack takes something the last did not, from her or from the audience: certainty (a scare that proves false), a safe place (rooms that hide the attacker), an ally (a friend who is secretly with the monster) | Is the middle the same attack again? What does each attack take that the last did not? |
| Nightmares that quicken | Reveals aimed at her worst fears, each in a new shape, coming faster towards the end (Truby's *cyclone effect*) | Does each reveal hit one of her named fears harder than the last? |
| The refuge falls | The last fight, where she and we felt safe (Truby's *safe haven*), fought outside her and inside | Where did she last feel safe, and which inner flaw must she beat there? |
| Learn or break | A choice: she learns (the rarer route) or breaks, and the lesson passes to the audience | Did you choose, and can the audience see what she should have learned? |
| The return | The monster seems dead, relief lands, then it comes back (Truby's *double ending*) | Is there a real beat of relief first, and what in her makes the return unavoidable? |

**The chain that holds them:** an old crime, through the hero's flaw, to a terrible crime to come *(fitted, from Psycho, Alien, The Shining and A Nightmare on Elm Street)*. A missing link makes the monster arbitrary.

## 7. The genre's question

Truby's picture of life in Horror, its *Mind-Action story view* in his term *(asserted)*: a long fight against death that everyone loses, waged by a mind that can picture its own end yet lives as if it will go on for ever. His key question: **where does the human end and the inhuman begin?** At root, how can a person be moral?

His *thematic recipe* (a genre's plan for living well) comes from turning the hero's basic action inside out *(built, and reusable in any genre)*. For Horror, in gist: face death and the unpaid past. **As a question for a draft:** what is this hero running from, and what would turning to face it cost her? Classic Horror answers in the negative *(asserted)*: the lesson is missed by the hero and carried out by the audience. The gaps still settle (Gap principle 7), and the meaning lies in how: the knowledge gap ends in the audience's favour, with the hero still blind. By Implied World principle 8, the monster, as the main departure, should embody the question: grief in *The Babadook*, racism in *Get Out*.

## 8. Where the care comes from

Care is the first term of the suspense equation and must be built before the threat arrives. Horror leans on these Bond levers:
- **Vulnerability**, turned high: she is weak beside the monster. It breaks into pity, then fatigue, unless she pushes back.
- **Decency and Connection** for the everyday hero. Truby's menu (kind, loving, about to marry or become a parent) is *fitted*; Bond theory warns that visible levers backfire, so show one decent act under strain rather than a list of virtues.
- **Interiority**, the most powerful lever, comes with the genre's focus on a split mind, shame and guilt.
- **Agency** arrives late, at the turn from running to scheming, where care renews; **Competence** carries the overreacher before the fall; **Humour** is usually kept low, since nobody fears for someone who takes nothing seriously (the workshop's reading of Bond theory, not Truby's).

**Alignment beats virtue** (Bond principle 2) does the genre's boldest work. *Psycho* keeps the audience with a gentle-seeming Norman, so the reveal lands on people who sided with him. In the flip, care moves to the monster through its mercy and the humans' cruelty to those who cannot help them (Bond principle 5). The monster needs care too (Bond principle 6): a pure killing machine is a function, not a person, and the audience grows indifferent to it.

## 9. Suspense and knowledge

**The equation.** Suspense = Care × Threat × Uncertainty × Time. Horror drives Threat and Time hard with a monster that never stops. Truby measures a Horror middle by the squeeze alone *(asserted)*; the Anticipation theory adds the ceiling: suspense peaks when the odds look bad but not hopeless, and collapses once hope is gone.

**The fear ladder in a Horror plot** (the workshop's mapping). Each rung closes an open question and uses up some of the audience's imagination, so climb slowly and linger below the top.

| Rung | Where Horror usually puts it |
|---|---|
| Dread | The house too quiet, the unpaid debt surfacing, the doubter's calm explanation |
| Suspense rung | Once the rules are shown: the audience knows what the monster does, not how it ends |
| Terror | The siege in a maze of rooms; signs before sight (the rats running past in *28 Days Later*) |
| Horror rung | Late, and it must beat what the audience imagined; Truby's beast and machine reductions are two ways to break a category (human yet animal; dead yet walking, like the zombie) |
| Shock (side rung) | False alarms: after real dread, a release; without it, a flinch the audience soon stops feeling |
| Residue | The return, a threat that could exist at home, and the theme left in the audience |

**Show the rules, hide the thing.** The audience needs enough of the monster's rules to predict danger, not enough to understand it. Truby's costs are ready-made rules: a vampire who hunts only after dark and recoils from the cross gives the audience something to predict with. The rules work like mysteries and are kept; the monster's nature and origin work better as wonders. Truby's ghost and his doubter both pull towards explaining, so keep two things apart. The hero's moral debt (her own or her family's old wrong) can be a mystery the story presses and pays. Where the monster came from stays a wonder, mentioned and never pressed, because emphasis is what turns a gap into a mystery the story then owes (Gap theory, Part 2 section 4). Where Truby's ghost is the monster's origin, as in *A Nightmare on Elm Street*, paying it off is his choice against the theory (section 13). And let the doubter's account fail.

**Lost refuge.** Fear is strongest when it invades a safe place or makes an everyday necessity dangerous. Truby's house is dangerous from the start; it frightens more if it was home first. His last fight in a refuge that looked safe is the principle at the climax. Cutting off help (the phone line, the car, the police who do not believe) and confining, isolating and wounding the hero do the same work: they take away safe actions.

**Knowledge position.** Choose scene by scene whether the audience knows more than, the same as, or less than the hero. Truby would hide the monster's full power from hero and audience alike *(asserted)*, which suits the shared terror of being level with her but gives up the suspense of helplessness that comes from being ahead. A false friend can go either way: tell the audience and every scene with him is suspense; hide it and his turn is a surprise.

**The ratchet.** Rise, ease a little, rise higher. Truby's false alarm before the real attack is the Lewton bus: a harmless scare, a laugh, the guard lowered, and the real threat arrives in that release. A false all-clear does the same ratchet work with no scare in it: in *Alien*, Kane seems recovered and the crew relaxes over a meal, and the attack comes while they are at ease. Used in the same order every time it becomes a rhythm the audience learns, and the pattern must break before they learn it.

**Residue.** Fear outlasts the story when the threat could exist in the audience's own world and something stays unresolved. Truby's endings supply both: a hero who breaks with the ghost still lodged in her, and a monster that returns. The deepest residue is the theme the monster gave a body to. More on each principle: `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`; on scheduling reveals: `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`.

## 10. Subgenres and mixes

**Subgenres Truby lists:** Gothic, Ghost Story (where a wronged spirit is literally the monster; not the ghost beat), Paranormal, Occult, Vampire, Werewolf, Slasher, Superhero, Saviour and Comedy. **Two large hybrids** *(asserted)*: *Horror with Myth*, which Truby reads as the story form of religion, how to beat death and reach endless life (*A Christmas Carol* moves the haunting from a place to time, so the visits show the hero his past, present and possible futures and make his choice urgent); and *Horror with Science Fiction and Epic*, which in Truby's reading asks whether a species can evolve or will destroy itself. **Other mixes:** with Love (*The Shape of Water*, *Ex Machina*), with Thriller (*Stranger Things*; both are about fear), with Detective and Fantasy (*Grimm*), with a Western inside Science Fiction (*Westworld*).

**Neighbours by contrast.** Detective is the opposite pole: reason wins and death has already happened to someone else. If a Horror draft reads like a puzzle, Detective has taken the lead; pull death back onto the hero's own body. Comedy is a cousin: both shrink people to beasts or machines, and Horror pushes the shrinking until it frightens. If a reduction gets laughs, the monster is not threatening enough. On choosing which genre leads, see `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`.

## 11. Twisting it

Truby's general routes above a genre are in `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`. For Horror, the larger form of life behind the genre is, in his reading, religion, and the stakes should spread beyond one house to society and politics *(asserted, fitted from admired works)*. His chapter's own moves, restated:

**The flip**, Truby's strongest move *(built)*: hero and monster change places morally, so the audience must face its own earlier judgement. Build the fear of the Other honestly first; then plan a scene where the monster shows mercy or love, and a matching one where the "normal" people are cruel to someone weak. The flip overturns an expectation, that the humans are the good side (*Frankenstein*, *Get Out*), so every earlier judgement is reread; the same kind of turn can show the killer is the one the audience sided with (*Psycho*: the gentle-seeming Norman, section 8), and every earlier scene changes meaning. Two questions for a draft follow from his cases. If the story makes a being, where does the draft spend its time: on the moment it is made, or on its struggle to become a self? Truby puts the weight on the second. If the world claims that anything goes, who pays for that freedom, and are they on screen?

**One idea from the owner theories.** Make an everyday necessity the monster's way in (Anticipation principle 4): sleep, sound, looking. It removes every safe action at once, and its ripple (Implied World principle 3) reaches every ordinary scene: once sound kills, a dropped plate is suspense. A necessity no earlier story has used also escapes the genre's defaults, so the audience cannot furnish it in advance. Test with a swap: replace the necessity with a place the hero could avoid. If the fear barely drops, the necessity was not yet doing its job.

## 12. Examples

- *Psycho*: the apparent hero killed early; the ghost is the killer's; alignment with a gentle-seeming Norman.
- *Alien*: the old crime hidden in company orders; a false friend; a false all-clear at a meal; the last fight in a shuttle that looked safe.
- *A Nightmare on Elm Street* and *The Shining*: a parent's old wrong becomes the monster, or is carried into a hotel that closes in; sleep made dangerous.
- *Pet Sematary* and *Dracula*: the price of cheating death; what comes back is worse, and endless life is fenced with rules the audience can learn.
- *Jaws*: an everyday hero; the monster kept unseen for over an hour. *A Christmas Carol*: a haunting moved from place to time.
- *Get Out*: a cheerful surface over rot; the ally as lure; racism as residue.
- *Frankenstein*, *The Shape of Water*, *Rise of the Planet of the Apes*: monster turned hero (the flip); a made being's struggle to become a self; a scientist's flaw rooted in kindness.
- *The Haunting of Hill House*, *A Quiet Place*, *It Follows*: terror with nothing seen; rules shown, the thing hidden.

## 13. Rivals and doubts

- **A squeeze without a ceiling.** Truby: the tighter the squeeze, the better the middle, against Anticipation principles 1 and 7 (no hope, no suspense; tension that only rises goes numb); the general rivals, *How low is the low point* and *A steady climb, or a ratchet*, are recorded in full in `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`, section 11. *Test for Horror:* halfway through a rising middle, remove the hero's last believable way out. Truby predicts fear keeps rising; the theory predicts the audience goes slack until some hope returns.
- **Hide everything, or hide the thing.** Truby: conceal the monster's power from hero and audience as far as possible. Anticipation principle 3: give the rules, hide the thing. *Test:* stage one attack twice, once after the audience has learned a rule (sound draws it) and once with nothing learned. The theory predicts suspense before the first and only shock at the second.
- **Attack at once, or care first.** The rival over opening on the conflict or on the normal, recorded in full in `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`, section 11; Truby has the monster press the hero almost from the start. *Test for Horror:* open with the attack, or with ten minutes of the family; the theory predicts the same attack frightens more in the second. Truby's own advice on the everyday hero sides with the theory.
- **The house as her mind, or a world with business of its own.** Truby's house as the hero's flaw given walls, dangerous from the start, against Implied World principle 4; recorded in full, with the workshop's verdict, in `.claude/skills/story-world/references/world-from-story.md`, section 11. Horror adds lost refuge, which wants a place that was safe first. *Test for Horror:* give the house a first act as a real home with routines that have nothing to do with her; the theory predicts more fear when it turns and a more real world.
- **Explained origins.** Truby's ghost often supplies the monster's origin, and his doubter explains; the Anticipation theory keeps the monster's nature and origin as wonders (section 9). *Test:* add one scene that explains where the monster came from (the old crime that made it). The theory predicts less fear from then on, as franchises find when they tell their monsters' backstories; Truby predicts a tighter chain from old crime to new.
- **Fear for oneself, or for her.** Truby makes death personal to the audience itself; the Anticipation theory builds fear for or with a character the audience cares about, and turns it on the audience only afterwards, as residue. *Test:* cut the scenes that build care for the victim and keep a threat that could reach the audience's own home. Truby predicts the fear holds; the theory predicts suspense inside the story drops, while residue may survive.
- **Doubts.** Rising above the genre is judged after the fact, and the label can be stretched over any acclaimed work. The double, the beast-or-machine reductions and "the monster in us" fit almost any story: prompts for choosing, not findings. The beats are fitted mostly from Gothic fiction and English-language film. Reading Horror with Myth as religion is a label, not a mechanism.
