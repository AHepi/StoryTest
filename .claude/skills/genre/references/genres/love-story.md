# Love Story

*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Genres, chapter 13, in the workshop's own words; framed by the owner theories (Gap theory principles 2, 4, 5 and 7; Anticipation theory principles 1, 5, 6 and 7, and its move from whether to how; Implied World theory, the baseline and principles 1, 3, 4, 5 and 8; Bond theory's ladder, levers, principles 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7, and the limits of care).*

Contents: 1 In one line. 2 The promise. 3 The baseline. 4 Guarantees, and which to break. 5 Hero and opponent. 6 The beats, as jobs. 7 The genre's question. 8 Where the care comes from. 9 Suspense and knowledge. 10 Subgenres and mixes. 11 Twisting it. 12 Examples. 13 Rivals and doubts.

## 1. In one line

A Love Story sets two people who each lack what only the other can give against each other, and brings them together only when each gives up part of a separate life for a shared one.

Words kept apart in this file: the **hero** is the lead whose desire the plot follows; the **beloved** is the other lead; **both leads** means the two as people, the **couple** the two as a unit. A **rival** is a competing suitor. **Union** is the ending in which the couple come together (Truby loosely calls it marriage); **farewell** is the ending in which they part for good. Truby's own summary *(asserted)*: this is the genre about how to be happy in ordinary daily life, and he puts it last and highest in his order of genres, a ranking the workshop treats as a value judgement (`.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`, section 7).

## 2. The promise

What the audience comes for, from Truby's account *(mostly asserted)*:
- **To feel the love grow**, not be told about it. That takes time, and buying the time is what most beats are for (section 6).
- **The sparring**: talk in which the couple test, tease and win each other. For many fans this, more than any love scene or wedding, is the chief pleasure.
- **Delayed firsts**: the first time they move as one (Truby's *first dance*) and the first kiss, each held back and each particular to these two.
- **A place to want to be**: pockets of the world, snug, closed off and a little outside time, where the couple can be close. Truby's word is *subworld*; this file says **pocket**.
- **A perfect moment** of union, and then the ache of the breakup.
- **An answer.** Before page one the genre sets up the expectation of a question: will these two end up together, and what will it cost them? The story makes it a *mystery* in Gap terms (principle 5) by pressing it, as nearly every Love Story does from the charged meeting on, and then owes an answer on the page: usually a reunion that brings happy tears; in the tragic form, love felt most keenly at the moment it is lost.

**The belief test** *(Truby's, fitted)*: the audience must believe these two love each other. His non-examples are sparring couples nobody believes in: *Gone with the Wind*, where Scarlett's heart stays with Ashley, and *The Maltese Falcon*. The fights of a Love Story without that belief are only conflict. The Bond theory names this failure: its **Connection** lever breaks on relationships that are claimed but never shown, and care can't be ordered, because audiences believe what they see (section 8). A draft that announces the love and never shows it has broken the promise, whatever its ending.

## 3. The baseline

The *baseline* (Implied World theory) is what the audience assumes by default: the real world plus the habits of the genre. Say "they meet at a wedding" and a Love Story audience has half the plot mapped. Unless told otherwise it assumes *(fitted, from Truby's beats and examples)* that the two introduced with friction are the couple, and a quarrel at first meeting means attraction; that a fiancé already attached to the beloved is the wrong one, and friends' advice about love is wrong; that both leads are equals, and suited; that at least one carries a wound from an earlier love; that there will be pockets for the love (a ballroom, a café, a foreign city) and a wedding somewhere; that in a period setting marriage means money and survival, fenced by inheritance, propriety and chaperones, while in a modern one family has little say over whom anyone loves; and, in the classic form, that the couple are a man and a woman (a default Truby notes is widening).

**Realist, but priced.** Most Love Stories spend little on departures and let the real world carry the rest. The strong ones still set one rule that prices the love. The Gap theory uses the case Truby uses: the Bennet estate can pass only to a male heir, so five daughters must marry well or face poverty, and every desire in *Pride and Prejudice* is shaped by it (Gap theory, Part 2 section 2). Truby's related claim *(built)*: each pocket needs a **dream side** and a **trap side**. The Regency looks like one long round of balls and clever talk, laid over an inheritance system that ruins women; *Four Weddings and a Funeral* puts four small paradises and one small hell in its title. The dream side gives the audience somewhere to want to return; the trap side supplies the pressure that makes the love matter. **Test:** for each place the couple meet, write what makes it lovely and what makes it cruel or confining for these two. If only the first column fills, add the second.

**Family weight** *(built, from a social mechanism)*: an opponent works only if its power is believable. A modern audience assumes weak family power, so family objection must be set up as a departure (Implied World principle 1) or tied to something the family really controls: money, belonging, safety. It still bites for pairings that defy custom across class, faith or race (*Romeo and Juliet*, *Crazy Rich Asians*, *The Big Sick*).

**Generic Love Stories ride on these defaults.** If the café could be any café, the fiancé any stiff fiancé and the wound any dead spouse, the draft is using the baseline's furniture. The fix is one departure the story owns, ideally the rule that prices this love (section 11); hand its building to `story-world`.

## 4. Guarantees, and which to break

A *guarantee* is a protection the audience assumes the genre gives. Union is the Love Story's strongest, an expected ending kept here because the audience relaxes into it as a protection for a couple it cares about: Truby warns *(asserted, from audience reaction)* that ending without it will anger readers unless the reason is very strong. Breaking a guarantee leaves the audience unsafe (or, when the break is the ending, bereft: the workshop's reading of a late break, which buys weight for the ending); dropping the question of union leaves it cheated, because that was a promise (the cheated-or-unsafe test, `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`, section 2). The table is the workshop's, drawn from Truby's endings and forms.

| Guarantee the audience leans on | Broken by | What breaking it buys |
|---|---|---|
| They end up together | farewell for a larger cause (*Titanic*, *Brief Encounter*; *Casablanca*, which gives the hero a new friendship prepared from the start) | Love felt most keenly at the moment of loss |
| Nobody dies in Romantic Comedy | *Four Weddings and a Funeral* | Weight: the comic safety was never total |

**Expectations to overturn.** Three more patterns are expectations about form or beat order, not protections: overturning one is a twist on how the promise is kept, not a broken guarantee, and the cheated-or-unsafe test gives neither answer. *The story ends at the wedding*: start there instead, and the question moves from whether to how long (section 11). *A marriage, once made, holds*: open on a failed one, the remarriage (*The Awful Truth*; section 11). *Love comes before sex*: *When Harry Met Sally* reverses it, which puts the belief promise at risk (section 6, on sex).

- **A farewell needs a cause the audience accepts** *(built)*. Truby's condition: only the largest reason may part them, best when one gives up the love for a higher cause, and a modern audience seldom believes anything outranks true love. **Test:** state the cause and ask whether this audience would call it worth more than the love. If not, find a bigger cause or choose union. The Bond theory says the same from its side: a story may hurt or part characters the audience loves, but it must mean something for them (the bond is a promise).
- **Keep the promise either way.** A farewell answers the question. A story that lets the beloved drift out of the last act does not.
- **Which one to break.** One, convincingly, and preferably the one the story's question is about (Anticipation principle 5; Implied World principle 8). Test by restoring it: let them unite after all and ask whether the scenes before lose anything. If nothing changes, the break was decoration.

## 5. Hero and opponent

**Hero: the Lover** (Truby's term), who drives the story *(asserted)*. One lead's want usually carries the plot, for a single line of drive *(built)*, but the chase runs both ways. His premise *(asserted)*: the hero lacks something only this person can supply, and the reverse. **Test:** swap the beloved for any attractive stranger; if the hero's change is the same, the love line is decoration. Truby sets the Love Story against a pattern that dresses Action up as romance, which he calls stalking: a confident man is refused and keeps pressing until she gives in. There only one person wants, the other is a prize, and nobody changes. **Test:** does the beloved have a desire, a growth and a say of their own? If refusal is an obstacle to push through, Action has taken the lead (Bond principle 6: a function rather than a person draws indifference).

**Flaw, need and desire.** The flaw (Truby's *weakness*) is an inability to love, in two forms *(built on his general model)*: psychological, hurting herself (cynicism, daydreaming, loneliness, bitterness, low self-worth), and moral, hurting someone else (selfishness, prejudice, a judging nature). **Test:** name who each lead's flaw hurts now; if nobody, add the moral side. Truby's *Mutual Need* *(built)*: give both leads variations of one need, so each change reads against the other. In *Silver Linings Playbook* he clings to a lost marriage and she uses sex in place of closeness, two ways of dodging intimacy (and a diagnosis is not a flaw). The **old wound**, usually a love that ended badly, is a cliché if it only explains; Truby's fix *(built)* is a fear alive now, which flares whenever the lead gets close. The need: to learn to love, letting go of the past and of what each hoards. **Potential** (Truby's *Character Potential*, *built*): show each lead's promise in the same stroke as the flaw, and have the other see it: energy, drive, a skill, an odd charm, fairness, a good heart. **Desire** runs in two lines *(built)*: a goal in work or life, set up first, and the love goal, which collides with it. Truby rejects the older pattern in which he achieves and she achieves through him; better that each has an ambition and love gets in its way.

**Opponent: the beloved** *(built)*. The obstacles that matter most come from the couple's own natures; a rival or a disapproving relative can be removed without anyone changing, so they are secondary opponents. Behind the beloved stands an inner opponent: love itself, feared as a loss of freedom and self. What keeps the resistance alive *(built)* comes from two places. **Inside the couple:** a difference no single conversation clears, in what they believe, where they come from or what they have (Truby's *Odd Couple*); **test:** could one honest conversation clear it up? Then deepen it. And wrongs done to each other that need forgiving, each one a measure of how far the other's love stretches (Truby's *Love Test*); if neither has anything to forgive, the love is untested. **Outside it:** family, where its power is believable (section 3), and rivals (below).

**Rivals and advisers.** Each rival stands for another way to live and marry *(built: choosing a partner is choosing an answer)*. Austen's pattern, as Truby reads it *(fitted)*: a dull match whose one asset is money, a dazzling charmer who proves hollow, and a steady, half-hidden man who is the right one. **Test:** write the life the hero would have with each; if two look alike, change one. Each lead's confidant gives confident, wrong advice about love *(fitted)*; better, make the ally the lead's flaw carried to its end *(built)*, as *Knocked Up* sets a pack of grown boys with no ties beside a married couple worn thin. More: `.claude/skills/character/references/opponents.md` and `.claude/skills/character/references/building-a-cast.md`.

## 6. The beats, as jobs

The genre's engine is delay *(built)*. Desire pulls the two together at once, so the beats are ways of keeping them apart long enough for the audience to feel the love, and for the question of union to build suspense (the Time term of the suspense equation). Nearly every beat swings between closeness and retreat, a swing Truby calls the *yes/no* *(fitted)*. The beats are fitted, drawn from past stories: a missing beat is a question, not a fault. Names and grouping are the workshop's.

| Beat | The job it does | If the draft lacks it, ask... |
|---|---|---|
| Two lives first | Sets each lead's goal in work or life, and the pocket's dream and trap, so the love has something to collide with | What is each trying to achieve before they meet, and what does this world charge for loving the wrong person? |
| Seeing worth | The first look registers the other's worth, not only their looks, with a flicker of alarm: the first yes/no | What does the hero notice that is not beauty, and what frightens her about it? |
| The charged meeting | Marks the pair as the couple and starts the conflict at once | Does the meeting mark them as the couple and start the conflict, or only charm? |
| A fight to a tie | Proves they are equals, and shows what each defends: freedom, pride, a way of life | Who wins the first fight? If one wins outright, where is the equality? |
| Longing | The ache after meeting, hidden behind composure or laid bare, with at least one retreat | Which lead hides the want and which shows it, and where does the guarded one run? |
| Kept together | A job, trip, disguise or task that stops them walking away (Truby's *Forced Togetherness*) | Could they simply leave each other? What stops them? |
| The rival | A competing suitor who has something the hero lacks, and so offers another life | What life would the beloved have with the rival, and is it tempting? |
| The con | A plan to win the beloved with a lie inside it (Truby's *Love Scam*), kept running as late as possible | What is hidden, from whom, and when does it come out? If before the climax, could it run longer? |
| Deeper each time | Talk that climbs from tastes to families to hopes and values, between fights; it can deepen the love or end it | Do their conversations climb? Does any step risk showing something the other might reject? |
| Moving as one | Love shown in deeds: they first act as partners (the first dance, literal or not), then what divides them returns | Where do they first move in step, and what reminds them straight after of what divides them? |
| The kiss held back | The point of no return: a jump in closeness, delayed and particular to these two | Is it as late as the story can bear, and what about it could only happen to them? |
| The perfect moment | The peak of union, in a place that mirrors it: a false win before the fall | Where are they happiest, and does a breakup follow soon? |
| The breakup | Costs both of them: each old self ends before the new one is ready | Does each lose something, and does each share the fault? |
| The slide | A desperate or vengeful wrong around the breakup, which the later revelation looks back on | What wrong does she do to keep or punish the beloved? |
| The fight over how to live | The climax sets their two ways of living against each other, before either owns their part of the blame | Are they arguing about how to live, or only about a misunderstanding? |
| Each gives up | Both changes land in one move: what each hoards is given up for the other (Truby's *double reversal*), so "I" becomes "us" | What does each let go, and is the cost shown, not only said? |
| Union or farewell | A reunion where feeling spills out in front of others, or a parting for a cause the audience accepts | Early desire, a separation, a reunion with witnesses: which is missing? |

**Sex** is not a major beat in Truby's account *(built)*: shown before the love has been felt, it nearly destroys the audience's belief in it, which is fragile early on; after it, a recoil and then tenderness bring the doubt back. **Talk carries most beats**: flirting is banter, not praise, and a compliment too soon tips the couple into friendship *(built)*; seduction runs cool (the desire under talk about something else, as in *The Big Sleep*) or open (a declaration that risks everything). The wording belongs to `dialogue`: `.claude/skills/dialogue/references/line-and-scene-design.md`, section 5, for comic exchanges; `.claude/skills/dialogue/references/what-dialogue-does.md`, section 5, for subtext.

## 7. The genre's question

Truby's picture of life here, his *Mind-Action story view* *(asserted)*, in one line: a person becomes most fully themselves through one particular other, at the price of some independence. His key question: **how much can you love?** His *thematic recipe* (his *Way of the Lover*) is most useful as the question below.

**As a question for a draft:** what will each lead give up for the other, and does the ending show it done rather than said? Union and farewell both answer it; a story that only illustrates the recipe preaches (`.claude/skills/plot/references/premise-and-theme.md`). The Gap theory puts the meaning in how the gaps settle and what it cost (principle 7); here the cost is independence, paid by both. Two ways to answer with nuance rather than a rule: set the world's main departure on the question (Implied World principle 8: the entail asks whether to marry for money or for love); and let plot and subplot judge the same act two ways, as *Moonstruck* excuses one affair and condemns another, because one serves love and the other puts passion above trust *(from one example)*.

## 8. Where the care comes from

The leads climb the Bond theory's ladder towards each other while the audience climbs it towards them, often in the same beats (the workshop's reading): the first look is *recognise*, the talk that goes deeper is *understand*, moving as one is *side with*, the rival and the breakup bring *worry*, and the perfect moment builds *miss*, which any farewell then tests. The levers the genre leans on:
- **Connection**, the lever the genre is built on: the love between the leads is a Connection the audience must see shown, not claimed (the lever's breaking point, and the belief test of section 2), and the beloved's regard vouches for the hero (Bond principle 5).
- **Desire**, highest of all: Truby calls it the strongest desire in any genre *(asserted)*, and the audience wants the union on the hero's behalf (Bond principle 3).
- **Vulnerability**: the person who can hurt her most is the one she loves, and love threatens her freedom. It turns to pity if she never pushes back.
- **Flaw**, a real weakness or wrong belief that costs her something. In this genre it usually also costs the beloved, which is Truby's craft preference (section 5), not the lever's condition. A severe flaw alienates unless other levers make up for it (the lever's breaking point; Bond principle 4); Truby's *potential* is one way, raising Competence, Agency, Humour or Decency in the same stroke.
- **Humour** carries the sparring, but constant quips undercut stakes; **Interiority** comes through the talk that goes deeper, where each describes themselves by describing what they love (*Sideways*, and wine).
- **Care is social** (Bond principle 5): the public reunion lets others witness the love.
- **Decency** is proved by the sacrifice at the end, not claimed; **surprise** (Bond principle 7) comes from reveals of each lead's real character, there all along.

**Runs low: Specificity**, when the classic form leans on types of man and woman, and that makes the Recognise rung harder to reach (section 13). **A trap: declarations.** Care can't be ordered: praise of the beloved, from the hero or anyone, makes audiences resist (the Bond theory's limits of care). The workshop's reading adds that a declaration which costs the speaker is an act the audience sees, and so works through Vulnerability and Desire rather than as praise.

## 9. Suspense and knowledge

**The equation.** Suspense = Care × Threat × Uncertainty × Time. A Love Story is rich in care and time, and its threat is the loss of the beloved, or of oneself. Its weak term is uncertainty, because the audience trusts the guarantee of union. So uncertainty moves from *whether* to *how* and *at what cost* (Anticipation theory, Part 2 section 1), and inside the story the audience still feels the doubt (anomalous suspense). The breakup must look near-final but not hopeless: bad odds are good, no odds are despair.

**Withholding** is the gap the genre mainly runs on: as suspense (will they unite, and how?) and through reveals. Truby's reveals in a Love Story concern real character *(built)*. For each lead, list what the other has not yet learned about them, then schedule when each item comes out (Gap principle 4). The kiss held back is the same move made with the body.

**Knowledge position** (Anticipation principle 6). The con puts the audience ahead of the beloved: it knows the lie and waits for the exposure, which the story then owes (Gap principle 5). Truby's advice to keep the con running as late as possible *(built)* follows, since once the secret is out that tension is gone. False things the beloved learns about the hero (Truby's *red herrings*) work the same way. Ensembles use the gap too: in Truby's *round* (*La Ronde*) each person wanted wants someone else, a chain only the audience sees, comic but episodic, so it does not build; several love strands converging on one time and place (*Love Actually*) restore drive *(built)*.

**The ratchet** (Anticipation principle 7). The yes/no is a ratchet of closeness: rise, partial release, rise higher. Its rule applies too: the pattern must break before the audience learns it (section 13).

**A half-answer at the end.** *Sideways* stops at a knock on a door. The pressed question, will he choose her, is settled by the knock (Gap principle 5); only what follows it stays open. More on each principle: `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`; on scheduling reveals: `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`.

## 10. Subgenres and mixes

**Subgenres Truby names**, grouped here: by tone (Romantic Comedy, Tragedy, Epic Love); marriage forms (Comedy of Remarriage, Tragedy of Traditional Marriage, True Love; section 11); heat (Erotica, Clean and Wholesome); period and place (Contemporary, Historical, Regency and the other market shelves sold by era); identity (LGBTQ+, Black and African American); readership (Young Adult); and blends sold as romance (Paranormal, Gothic, Romantic Suspense). **Mixes:** with Thriller (*Rebecca*), Coming-of-Age (*Moonlight*, *Say Anything*), Sports (*Rocky*, *Bull Durham*), Crime (*Gilda*), the Caper (*The Thomas Crown Affair*, where the game beats love), Fantasy (*Splash*, *The Night Circus*), Horror (*The Shape of Water*), Detective (*The Thin Man*), Comedy (*Bridget Jones's Diary*), and the Buddy Story (Action joined to Love Story). The first dance works even where Love Story is only a guest genre *(Truby, asserted)*.

**Neighbours by contrast.** Action pursues and wins; a Love Story changes both people. If only one lead wants and the other is won, Action leads (section 5). Romantic Comedy is a Love Story played for laughs (`comedy.md`); if neither lead ever risks anything real, Comedy has taken the lead. **Ensembles** *(built)*: strands that converge (*Love Actually*) or the round (section 9); ensembles thin care (Bond theory: too many characters dilute it), so keep a small inner circle. **Series** *(fitted, from one strong case)*: a family of siblings who each see the world's rules from a different angle, one in front per book and the rest behind (*Bridgerton*, grown from *Pride and Prejudice*'s five daughters); known siblings carry the Miss rung into the next book. On choosing which genre leads: `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`, section 8.

## 11. Twisting it

Truby's general routes above a genre are in `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`, section 9. For Love Story, the field of life he assigns is happiness itself, and his main route runs **from courting to marriage**: the classic story ends with a wedding, the lifted one starts there *(built: closeness raises both what each can gain and the risk of domination and collapse)*. Three forms:
- **The old contract, followed to its end** (Truby's *Tragedy of Traditional Marriage*). Write the couple's unspoken deal, what each gives and expects, and follow its terms for years. In Truby's reading the old deal (he provides; she is beautiful and raises the children) turns him into a machine and her into a child *(built)*: *Madame Bovary*, *A Doll's House*, *Revolutionary Road*.
- **The remarriage** (Stanley Cavell's *Comedy of Remarriage*). Open on a failed marriage. **Test:** what does each find in the ex that the marriage had buried? If it is only the old attraction rekindled, the second marriage will fail like the first. *His Girl Friday*, *The Awful Truth*.
- **Lasting love** (Truby's *True Love*), the hardest and rarest, because past courtship no single goal drives it. Truby's fix *(built)*, his *Opposition Before Desire*: start from what presses on the couple (parents and children, work against love, social forces) and let that show what they must want. The shape falls instead of rising, from the most passionate union towards a steadier, deeper balance, reached through several small realisations rather than one. Happy couples are hard to dramatise, so set one beside less happy ones (Austen's couples, ranked by likely future), or hand them a problem from outside (*The Thin Man*, a marriage that solves crimes; *from one example*).

**Smaller twists from the chapter:** turn the first look into hearing, so the beloved is met first as a person (*Sleepless in Seattle*, a voice on the radio); play the meeting awkward rather than charming (*Silver Linings Playbook*); let the woman drive, run the con (*The Lady Eve*) or kiss first (*To Have and Have Not*); save early firsts for the climax (*Heartbreaker* holds back the first outing and the first dance until then); end on a farewell and a new bond prepared all along (*Casablanca*), or on a half-union (*Sideways*).

**One idea from the owner theories: let the world price this love.** Give the story one rule of its world that makes loving *this* person cost something specific, and follow it everywhere (Gap principle 2: the world decides what things cost; Implied World principles 3 and 8). The entail does it for *Pride and Prejudice*; a law, a debt, a faith, a caste or an illness could do it elsewhere. It gives a realist backdrop business of its own (principle 4), and it makes union a real question without making either lead worse. The rule does not replace the beloved as opponent; it sets what their difference costs, as Darcy's pride and Elizabeth's prejudice matter because marriage decides a woman's whole future. **Test with a swap:** move the couple to a world without the rule. If none of their obstacles changes, the world was not pricing the love.

## 12. Examples

- *The Philadelphia Story*: suitors as ways to live; a heroine choosing a marriage, and remarrying.
- *Pride and Prejudice* (and *Bridgerton*): a rule that prices every desire; a proud man and a righteous woman; couples ranked by likely happiness.
- *Casablanca*: a bar that is both trap and haven; farewell for a larger cause, and a friendship prepared from the start.
- *When Harry Met Sally*: rival theories of the sexes; a slow dance where each takes fright in turn; sex, then recoil.
- *Sleepless in Seattle*: the beloved heard before seen; a heroine who crosses the country and then runs. *Silver Linings Playbook*: one need worn two ways; a meeting played awkward; a forged letter as the slide.
- *Sideways*: falling in love by talking about wine; a breakup from a slip; a half-union. *Heartbreaker*: a con run to the end; early firsts saved for the climax.
- *Moonstruck*: plot and subplot judging the same act two ways. *Out of Sight*: a thief and an agent; potential seen at once; a chance closing fast. *Knocked Up*: allies as the leads' flaws carried to their ends.
- *Madame Bovary*: the old contract's slide. *His Girl Friday*, *The Awful Truth*: remarriage. *The Thin Man*: a happy marriage given a case to solve.
- *Gone with the Wind*: sparring without belief, a non-example in Truby's reading.

## 13. Rivals and doubts

- **Keep union, or break the contract.** Truby: union is the genre's great payoff, not to be withheld lightly. Anticipation theory: suspense needs real uncertainty (principle 1), and fear needs the audience to believe the usual protections are gone (principle 5). They agree on the ending and differ on the middle. *Test:* add one early scene in which another couple in the same world parts for good, for a reason close to the leads' own. The theory predicts the leads' breakup grows tenser; Truby's account, which puts the pull in the promised payoff, predicts little change.
- **The yes/no every time.** Truby: after every peak of closeness (dance, kiss, sex), bring back at once what divides them. Anticipation principle 7: a pattern must break before the audience learns it. *Test:* let one peak stand unanswered, and bring the next retreat later and from outside. Truby predicts the story sags after the unanswered peak; the theory predicts the later retreat hits harder.
- **Every place serves the love, or has business of its own.** Truby: a pocket should express the couple, draw them together and draw the reader in. Implied World principles 4 and 5: a world feels real when it has business of its own and details that serve no one. *Test:* give one pocket people with concerns of their own who ignore the couple. The theory predicts the place, and the risk to the love, feel more real; Truby predicts the love loses focus. *Casablanca*'s bar, full of refugees, does both.
- **Declare without shame, or care can't be ordered.** Truby *(asserted, from wish fulfilment)*: the audience wants to hear a passion it rarely meets in life, so write it in full. The Bond theory speaks to part of this: praise of the beloved, meant to make the audience value her, is resisted; it says nothing against the speaker's feeling as such. *Test:* write the declaration twice, once when saying it risks nothing and once when it risks humiliation or loss. The theory predicts the praise in it is resisted in both; the workshop's reading (section 8) predicts only the second moves, because the risk makes it an act the audience sees; Truby predicts both do, if the words are strong enough.
- **Types of man and woman, or particular people.** Truby grounds the classic form's conflict in broad sex differences (seeing against hearing, winning against connecting) *(asserted)*. By the `hard-to-vary` test it is loose: it explains any couple after the fact, and does not cover the same-sex couples he includes elsewhere. The workshop's rival, which no owner theory settles: the conflict comes from these two people's flaws and desires (section 5). *Test:* swap the leads' roles. Truby's account predicts the conflict changes; if it survives unchanged, it came from these people, not from the categories.
- **The falling shape.** Truby: a marriage story falls from passion to a steady balance, and that change of kind is dramatic in its own right (he admits it has no ready spine). The suspense equation: with no real threat to the bond, one term is zero. *Test:* tell the same marriage story with and without one pressure that could really end it (a move, an illness, a job). The theory predicts only the second has suspense; whether the first holds by other means (curiosity, the Miss rung) is what the test shows. Truby predicts the change of kind can hold it alone.
