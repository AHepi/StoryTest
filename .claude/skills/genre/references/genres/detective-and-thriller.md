# Detective and Thriller

*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Genres, chapter 12, in the workshop's own words; framed by the owner theories (Gap theory: the withholding, perspective and knowledge gaps, principles 1, 4, 5, 6 and 7, Part 2 section 5 and the fourth writer's question; Anticipation theory: the suspense equation, the fear ladder and principles 1, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7; Implied World theory: the baseline, surface and implication, principles 5 and 8; Bond theory: the levers, principles 2, 5 and 6, and the limits of care).*

Contents: 1 In one line. 2 The promise. 3 The baseline. 4 Guarantees, and which to break. 5 Hero and opponent. 6 The beats, as jobs. 7 The genre's question. 8 Where the care comes from. 9 Suspense and knowledge. 10 Subgenres and mixes. 11 Twisting it. 12 Examples. 13 Rivals and doubts.

## 1. In one line

Detective hides the killer until the end, so the plot is made of the puzzle he left behind, which the investigator and the audience take apart together; Thriller keeps the investigation but puts the investigator's own life at stake, so fear takes over from the puzzle.

Words kept apart in this file: *Detective* and *Thriller*, with capitals, are the genres, which Truby treats together as the two genres of finding out. (Thriller is also called Suspense; this file avoids that name, because *the suspense rung* is a step on the Anticipation theory's fear ladder.) The *investigator* is whoever works the case: in Detective, *the detective*, usually a professional; in Thriller, an amateur, called *the Thriller hero*. The *opponent* is the role against her: in Detective, *the killer*, hidden; in Thriller, whoever is really attacking her. *Suspects* are the people who could have done it; Thriller usually has one, *the main suspect*, whose guilt stays in doubt. A *clue* is evidence that tells the investigator something new; a *false trail* (the usual word is *red herring*) is any clue that points the wrong way, whoever or whatever laid it. The killer's *plan* is the crime together with its cover-up. *Fair play* is the promise that the solution rests on clues the audience was given.

## 2. The promise

**Detective** *(mostly fitted)*: a puzzle built by a clever, hidden killer and worked out alongside a first-rate mind; clues and reveals, some false, rising to an answer that shocks and still fits looking back; a showdown between two versions of what happened; a payment that fits the crime. Its expected mystery, *who did it?*, is the purest mystery in the Gap theory's sense (principle 5): the telling lingers on it, a character makes answering it her whole goal, and it returns in almost every scene. The story owes an answer. Fair play is a promise too, not a guarantee: an answer resting on something the audience was never shown leaves it cheated, not unsafe (`.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`, section 2).

**Thriller**: fear for an ordinary person in a trap that keeps tightening (*the squeeze*, here), set by someone who may be a killer and is often someone she loves; evidence swinging between his guilt and his innocence; a last attack she survives by inventing a way to fight. Its expected mystery is closer to home, *is she right to suspect him?*, and it too must be answered.

**Four switches** (Truby's contrasts; *fitted, and loose at the edges*: plenty of detectives are amateurs). Each Thriller setting raises a term of the suspense equation (the workshop's reading). **Test:** set each switch for your premise and say why; mixed settings are legitimate, but if the investigator is never plausibly at risk, the story is Detective, and its fear scenes will ring hollow.

| Switch | Detective default | Thriller default | Term it raises |
|---|---|---|---|
| The killing | already done | usually still to come | Time |
| The investigator | trained and paid | untrained, drawn in by something odd | Threat, Uncertainty |
| Can she fight? | yes, often armed | no, and some frailty exposes her | Threat |
| Suspects | many | one main suspect, often close to her | Care and Threat, on one person |

## 3. The baseline

The *baseline* (Implied World theory) is what the audience assumes by default: the real world plus the genre's habits. A Detective audience assumes *(fitted)*: a death has already happened; a closed circle of suspects, one of whom did it; clues it could spot for itself; police who are competent but blinkered or bound by procedure; and a setting of facades, usually a city, where public faces hide private truths, power sits in few hands, rich and poor live side by side, and one crime will expose something wider. The cosy branch swaps the city for a pretty village where murders happen improbably often and nobody moves away, a genre habit accepted without a thought (Truby's joke about *Murder, She Wrote*). A Thriller audience assumes an ordinary home, marriage or neighbourhood that looks safe and hides rot; the Political Thriller, a city of layered official corruption.

**The main departure is hidden** (the workshop's reading). Both genres spend little of the attention budget on visible departures. The real departure is a lie under the surface, which the plot exists to expose, and by Implied World principle 8 that lie should embody the story's question. **Case.** *L.A. Confidential* opens by selling a sunny city of new homes and easy living, which the investigation strips down to a police force running its own crimes: Truby's *fake utopia*, where the promise of the good life is itself the cover story. **Point.** List the facades: which public face hides which private truth? Each should be one the investigator has to get behind, and one should hide the central truth. For a Thriller, make the home look safe first, then crack it (lost refuge, Anticipation principle 4).

**Practised audiences bring more.** Regulars know the tricks, so the tricks are part of their baseline: the obvious suspect is innocent, every stray detail may be a clue. A writer can turn those defaults against them (section 11; the *Knives Out* example in the genre skill's `SKILL.md`). **Generic** Detective rides on the defaults: the rain-soaked city, the weary investigator, the gathering in the library. Swap the city for any other: if nothing in the crime or its cover-up changes, the setting is furniture. Find the facade only this story has, and hand its building to `story-world` (`.claude/skills/story-world/SKILL.md`).

## 4. Guarantees, and which to break

A *guarantee* is a protection the audience assumes the genre gives without being told. Anticipation principle 5: break one early and convincingly and every later threat is real; break all of them and care collapses, and suspense with it. The two genres part here. Detective protects its investigator; Thriller withholds that protection from the start (section 2's switches), so her danger is Thriller's baseline, not a break it can spend. Truby does not use the word; the table is the workshop's, drawn from his examples and from well-known works.

| Guarantee the audience leans on | Broken by | What breaking it buys |
|---|---|---|
| The investigator is safe (Detective) | Jack Vincennes, one of three detective leads, shot by his own captain (*L.A. Confidential*) | No badge protects the two left; every later scene with the captain carries threat |
| The one who helps her is safe (Thriller) | Hutch in *Rosemary's Baby* | She is alone, and the audience knows help can die |
| The child or bystander near the danger is safe (Thriller) | the boy carrying the bomb in *Sabotage* (Hitchcock later called it a mistake) | Every later threat to anyone near her is real |
| The Thriller hero survives the last attack (the hero coming through: an expected ending kept as a protection for her) | almost never: *The Vanishing* (1988), whose searcher ends buried alive | Late, it gives the ending weight: the search was the trap |

- **Expectations to overturn.** Four more patterns are expectations, habits of how the genre keeps its promise (most sit in the baseline, section 3), not protections. They are usually overturned late, and overturning one is a twist, not a broken guarantee: what it buys is surprise or a split settlement, not the fear principle 5 describes (section 11 uses them). *The detective gets her quarry* (*Chinatown*, so the ending weighs what knowing cost, not what it won). *There is one killer* (twelve in *Murder on the Orient Express*, so the circle of suspects becomes the answer). *The investigator is innocent* (*Oedipus Rex*, *Shutter Island*, so the question moves from who did it to who she is). *The truth, once found, goes on the record* (*Murder on the Orient Express*, *Chinatown*, so the audience knows and the world in the story does not). Thriller's safe home is not a protection either: its audience assumes a home that looks safe and hides rot (section 3). Taking it away, down to her trust in her own perception, is lost refuge (Anticipation principle 4), the genre's own tool (*Shadow of a Doubt*, *Suspicion*, *Gaslight*).
- **Promises, not guarantees.** Fair play, and a teller who hides what he knows only where the hiding is part of who he is (Gap principle 6). Break either and the audience feels cheated, not unsafe. Neither is a twist (section 9). Whether the Thriller's beloved is guilty is a promise too: it is the Thriller's expected mystery (section 2), owed either way, and its answer bears on the genre's question (section 7).
- **Which one to break.** In Thriller, break early, and on someone near her, the helper or a bystander, so every later threat to her is real. In Detective the protection to spend is the investigator's own safety, most easily through a second investigator killed mid-case (*L.A. Confidential*), which makes every later scene dangerous for the rest. A quarry who escapes is not a broken guarantee but a twist on the payment (section 11). To test an early break, restore it and ask whether later scenes lose their danger. For a late break, which gives the ending weight rather than making later threats real (`how-genres-work.md`, section 2), ask instead what the ending would mean with it restored: if the searcher in *The Vanishing* lived, would the search still read as the trap? And break one: if everyone near her dies, care collapses, and suspense with it.

## 5. Hero and opponent

**The detective: a seeker with a method** *(fitted; Truby builds her from two archetypes, the seeker and the magician)*. The plot needs two things from her: a method the reader can follow, and an outsider's licence to go where the police will not. Her desire, to solve it, points at the past and needs a present stake to pull forward (section 9). Two tests. *Method made visible* (the Holmes stories): can a reader trace the steps to each conclusion? If not, brilliance reads as the author's say-so. *A gift the plot uses* (Truby's *Unique Detective*): finish "only she could have solved this because..." with a trait the turning clue needs. A gift the plot never uses is a quirk, the Specificity lever's breaking point.

**Her flaw.** Past the limits of her position (section 9, *The limited teller*), the flaw that matters is usually a strength pushed too far: suspicion into paranoia, justice into an obsession that hurts others, precision into coldness. A *ghost* (Truby's term: a past event still haunting the hero), often guilt over an old failure, makes the case personal and ties her to the genre's question of guilt; check that it both drives her and gets in her way (*Mare of Easttown*, *The Silence of the Lambs*). Truby's *Weakness Story* *(built, from a psychological model)*: the flaw comes wrapped in a self-justifying account that blames others. Write it in her voice in three sentences, then write what is true. The distance between them is her need, and the case should make her read her own story as hard as a suspect's (*The Trespasser*). A condition such as Monk's compulsions is an obstacle, not a flaw *(asserted, Truby's distinction)*; the Bond theory would ask instead whether it makes change possible, the Flaw lever's second job, and a fixed condition usually does not. A series sleuth, Truby's *traveling angel*, may have no flaw and never change.

**The Thriller hero: exposed** *(built: fear needs a credible chance of harm)*. Untrained, unarmed, frightened, with one thing that makes her easy prey: a broken leg (*Rear Window*), childhood (*The Sixth Sense*), being hunted by the law (*The Fugitive*). Truby notes she is usually a woman *(fitted)*. Name what makes her prey, and check the opponent is placed to use it. Her desire is to find out while escaping attack, and each discovery should make her more of a target (*Michael Clayton*); if learning leaves her safer, the engine is idling. Name his crime in one word, then give her a milder version of it, and let the case make her face it: the likeness ties her change to the hunt, since she cannot condemn him without judging herself (Truby's *Weakness Matches Crime*, *built*). *Shutter Island* takes this to the end, where hunter and hunted are one man.

**The killer: an illusionist** *(built: without a false trail, the first competent look solves the case)*. Pleasant outside, ruthless inside, hidden among the suspects. His plan has one job: make himself look clear and at least one innocent look guilty. Check it for both halves. The best plan counts on this detective's weakness: *Vertigo*'s scheme is built around the detective's fear of heights. If it would work as well against any detective, it is not personal enough. His own flaw is often arrogance: he underrates her (*Columbo*). A plan too clever for any but an exceptional mind is the genre's engine (Truby's *Ingenious Opponent*), with a ceiling (section 13).

**The Thriller opponent attacks**, usually an intimate (a husband, a lover, an uncle), and strikes above all at her guilt and fear rather than her body (Truby's *Emotional Attack*): in *Basic Instinct* the main suspect goads the detective about his wife's suicide. The extreme is *gaslighting*, named after the 1944 film in which a husband makes his wife doubt her own sanity. For building either figure: `.claude/skills/character/references/opponents.md`.

## 6. The beats, as jobs

The beats are fitted, drawn from past stories: a missing beat is a question, not a fault. Ask what job it does, and whether the draft gets that job done some other way. Names and grouping are the workshop's; rows marked (T) belong to Thriller.

| Beat | The job it does | If the draft lacks it, ask... |
|---|---|---|
| The killer's plan | Generates every event of the investigation; designed first, it is the hidden part of the story, which the investigation's order reveals (Truby's *Opponent's Plan First*) | Can you write his moves in time order, each with the trace it leaves? |
| The facade | Gives the investigator a world to get behind, one that pushes back | Which public face hides the central truth, and who guards it? |
| Why her | Makes the case personal: a ghost tying her to the question of guilt, or a stake the Thriller hero cannot walk away from | Why does she keep looking when stopping would be easier? |
| The gift and the blind spot | What only she can see, and the one kind of evidence she will misread | Where does her gift turn the case, and where does her blind spot cost someone? |
| The circle | Suspects with motive, opportunity and no alibi, one at least apparently cleared (Truby's *More Suspects*) | Who fails one of the three tests, and is the failure only apparent? |
| False trails | False trails, many from the killer's plan, and clues that fit two answers (Truby's *Double Clue*), keep two solutions alive | What laid each false trail, and where is it explained? Can each clue be read two ways? |
| The working mind | The middle as thinking made visible: finding, pressing, rethinking (below) | Which kind of scene does the middle lack, and which does it repeat? |
| Reveals that build | Each weightier than the last and nearer someone she is close to, the intervals between them shrinking | Do they rise? Where does the rhythm break before the audience learns it? |
| The scales (T) | Keeps her doubt alive: the case for and against the main suspect near even until late, when a false clearing leaves her lost just before the proof that damns him | Are the two columns close to even? Is there a false clearing before the proof? |
| The squeeze (T) | Each discovery makes her more of a target, and she keeps investigating under attack | In every attack scene, what does she also do for the case? |
| The slip | A mistake by the killer's side, one the audience could have seen, breaks his account from inside | Is it an error of theirs rather than luck, and does it re-explain earlier clues? |
| The battle of accounts | Detective: two coherent accounts clash and the killer cracks; Thriller: his last attack, beaten by something she improvises; the procedural: the interrogation room | Can a listener state both accounts, each coherent? What does she have that the audience saw but never took for a weapon? |
| Seeing herself | None for the series sleuth; in ambitious versions, her own part in the harm | Did you choose? If ambitious, where is her part planted? |
| The fitting payment | A punishment that mirrors the crime (Truby's *poetic justice*), and her decision about what to do with the truth | Put the crime in one image: does the payment echo it? What does she do once she knows? |

**The working mind** (the workshop's grouping of Truby's investigation steps). Middle scenes come in three kinds. *Finding*: a trace examined until it yields a new fact; evidence that yields nothing is set dressing. *Pressing*: questioning with a hidden aim (a handwriting sample, a provoked outburst), so the suspect answers the question asked and gives away the one not asked *(built: hostile witnesses call for tactics)*. *Rethinking*: a hunch tested against the facts, a memory revisited from a new angle or at a new scale (a recording, a second witness, a detail enlarged), the facts reordered into a new account and tried on the suspects. A missing kind is a hole; a middle of nothing but interviews is monotony. **No insight from nowhere** *(asserted, well motivated)*: name the object or fact behind each breakthrough, and check the audience saw it earlier.

**The two-column test** *(built; one of the chapter's most usable tools)*. Left: each move of the killer, in time order. Right: the trace each move leaves and the scene where it is found. A move with no trace cannot be solved fairly; a found trace with no move is a clue only the author planted. This is the Gap theory's split between story (what happened, in order) and plot (the order in which the audience learns it), turned into a worksheet for the hidden part of the story.

## 7. The genre's question

Truby's picture of life in Detective, its *Mind-Action story view* in his term *(asserted)*, in one line: knowing is choosing between rival accounts of what happened, and a mind that stops choosing goes blind. His key question, restated: **which person is to blame, and which is not?** **As a question for a draft:** once she knows, what does she do with the truth, and what does that choice cost her? End on a decision, not only a discovery. **Give the other side its best case** (`how-genres-work.md`, section 4): a rival investigator who bends the facts to a pet theory born of prejudice shows her weighing by contrast. At his best he is nearly right.

Thriller *(asserted)* turns the same search on the people she loves: can she trust those closest to her? **As a question for a draft:** what will she risk to find out, and what does trusting him, or doubting him, cost her? Keep both answers live until the late reveals. In Gap terms, for both genres, the question lives in the world (Part 2 section 6): a world of facades asks what is behind them, and the settlement answers through what it exposes and at what cost (principle 7).

## 8. Where the care comes from

Care is the first term of the suspense equation and is built before the threat arrives.
- **The detective** runs on **Competence** (the pleasure of watching a method work) and **Specificity** (the gift, the manner), which can carry a hero low on warmth. The Bond theory's own case is Holmes, with Watson supplying the **Connection** and the admiring eyes through which we learn to value him. First person or close third adds **Interiority** and aligns the audience with her confusion. **Desire** is always plain. Ambitious versions add a real **Flaw** and a ghost (**Vulnerability**). The social route (Bond principle 5): the snubbed outsider more moral than the respectable people around her borrows care from the audience's sense of unfairness. Series detectives reach the Miss rung through time rather than change: the audience returns for the company, not for an arc.
- **The Thriller hero** runs on **Vulnerability**, with **Desire** (stop the killing, find out) and **Connection** (the love at risk). **Agency** must stay high: a hero who only reacts under attack turns pity into fatigue, and the plot stalls with her *(built)*. Time with her and access to her mind keep the audience with her even when her flaw echoes the crime (Bond principle 2, alignment beats virtue); the **Flaw** lever makes her human and her change possible.
- **The opponent** earns care through Competence (the ingenious plan) and an arrogance that makes the audience long for his fall: it cares what happens to him, pointing the other way (Bond principle 6).

## 9. Suspense and knowledge

This is where the two genres part. Detective runs mainly on the Gap theory's withholding gap, as curiosity; Thriller runs on the Anticipation theory's suspense.

**Detective: curiosity, and the plan as story.** In the Gap theory's terms the killer's plan is the hidden, past part of the *story* (what happened, in order), and the investigation is its present part, driven by her desire and changing her. The *plot* is the order in which the audience learns both. In most Detective stories that order follows the investigation, so the investigation becomes the schedule for revealing the crime. Truby's advice to design the plan first fits the theory's logical order (story before its schedule), as a writing method. Curiosity is an open question about the past, and the detective does inside the story what every audience does with every plot, rebuilding what happened from fragments (Gap principle 1); that is why the rethinking scenes carry so much. **Curiosity needs present stakes** *(Truby, argued)*: the goal points backwards, so something must pull forwards. The genre protects the classic detective, so Threat to her is low and suspense must attach to someone else: the next victim, an innocent about to hang. **Test:** remove every present threat from the middle. If it sags, give someone the audience cares about something to lose now.

**Fair play: two checks.** *(1) The teller's limits.* The teller sets what can be known, and so what the plot can fairly hold back (Gap principle 6). A fact may fairly be withheld when the teller cannot know it yet, or when not saying is part of who the teller is. The genre's main source is the first: the killer hides the truth from the investigator, so a teller who stands with her cannot know it either. *(2) Shown before it is used*: every fact the solution uses was on the page before the reveal. That is the genre's own promise (section 2; *No insight from nowhere*, section 6), not the theory's: principle 6 alone would pass a solution resting on a fact the teller learned in the last chapter. **Once she knows**, a teller who stands with her knows too. When she has the answer and keeps it for the final scene, hide it only through a teller who cannot know (a Watson), or make her silence part of who she is and show it on the page, as when she tests the answer before naming it (the workshop's reading); otherwise it is the *neither* case below. **False trails.** Truby wants every one to come from the killer's plan *(built: such a trail re-explains itself when the case is solved, so it pays twice)*; treat that as a pattern to prefer, not a law. A coincidence, or an innocent suspect's own secret, can lay one fairly. The owner theories ask two things of each, whatever laid it: the teller reported it as she could see it (principle 6), and if the plot stresses it, it is explained by the end (principle 5: a question the plot presses is a mystery). **Test:** for each fact the solution uses, name the scene where the audience first saw it. For each false trail, say what laid it and where the story explains it; one stressed and never explained is a broken promise.

**The limited teller.** The detective's gravest weakness, starting ignorant and seeing from one angle, is also the genre's best telling position *(built)*. First person or close third keeps the audience level with her, sharing her bafflement; never tell the reader what she could not know unless you mean to change knowledge position. Stage events just outside her view, so that learning what she missed is a reveal (Truby's *Events Outside the POV*): in *Atonement* a girl misreads what she sees at a fountain, and a life follows from it. A narrator who knows less than the detective, as Watson does, keeps the audience a step behind her and makes withholding fair from the start: he cannot tell what he has not understood (the workshop's reading of principle 6).

**The narrator who is the killer.** *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd* is still argued over a century on because its narrator hides the one thing he knows best. The Gap theory does not settle the case; it names what the argument turns on: a teller who hides what he knows is cheating, unless the hiding is part of who he is. A writer who takes this road must answer that on the page: give the teller a reason inside the story to tell it this way, let his silences have the shape of his character, and plant seams a rereader can find. *Defending Jacob* stands on safer ground by that test (the workshop's reading): its narrator's late admission about his own father reads as shame carried all along. **Test**, for every teller and not only a guilty one (`.claude/skills/plot/references/telling-and-perspective.md`, section 4): mark each withheld fact *could not know*, *would not say because of who he is*, or *neither*. Every *neither* is the cheat.

**Surprise, and the least likely killer.** Surprise is an open question the audience did not know was there. Truby's *Least Likely Killer* *(fitted and built)* needs the killer to seem cleared, one of motive, opportunity or alibi apparently failing, and a solution that shocks yet follows. Least likely means least likely in the audience's model, and for a practised audience that can be the most obvious suspect. Two checks for the final reveal: does a second reading find the signs, and does the slip re-explain earlier clues rather than add new ones?

**Thriller: suspense, an open question about the future.** The crime is usually still to come, so put a clock on the opponent's plan: when does it strike, and what must she learn before then? Each Thriller switch raises a term (section 2), and loving the main suspect puts Care and Threat on one person, so both rise together (Truby's *Faith vs. Skepticism*: trust him and risk her life, or doubt him and risk losing him). The dilemma works only if the evidence about him is truly uncertain and both choices cost something. Uncertainty has a sweet spot, odds that look bad but not hopeless, and the Thriller climax sits there: she fights back from extreme weakness, with an improvised weapon. On the fear ladder, Detective keeps the killing offstage and stays mostly below it, at curiosity; Thriller works dread (something is wrong in the house) and the suspense rung (the audience knows what he may be and what he could do, not how it ends), and may climb higher in the final attack; Horror climbs to the horror rung, the killer seen. Truby's contrast *(asserted)*: Detective pursues the killer, Horror flees him, Thriller does both.

**Knowledge position** (Anticipation principle 6). Detective usually holds the audience level with the detective while she searches, and behind her once she has the answer and keeps it; a Watson keeps it a step behind her throughout. Thriller moves: level while she doubts; ahead when the audience sees the suspect at work and she does not (gaslighting scenes give the suspense of helplessness); behind when she has seen something the audience has not. Moving a Detective audience ahead changes the engine: *Columbo* shows the murder first, and curiosity becomes suspense about how the killer will be caught. Same story, different schedule (Gap principle 4). *Oedipus Rex* shows the structure working with no mystery at all for an audience that knows the myth. **The ratchet** (principle 7): Truby's scales are one, since the late reveal that seems to clear the suspect is a partial release, like the Lewton bus, before the real threat; his steadily accelerating reveals are a pattern the audience can learn (section 13). More: `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`, `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md` and `.claude/skills/plot/references/telling-and-perspective.md`.

## 10. Subgenres and mixes

**Detective subgenres Truby names**, grouped here by what defines them. Method: Police Procedural (the hero a team; the showdown in the interrogation room), Scientific Investigation, Locked Room, Supernatural. Tone: Hard Boiled, Cozy Mystery, Gentleman Mystery, Comedy Detective. Also by who investigates (a reporter, a private eye) and by period or country. **Thriller subgenres.** Arena: Political, Legal, Medical, Military, Technothriller, Historical. Inner focus: Psychological Suspense, and Woman in Jeopardy, where the danger comes from an intimate and, at best, opens onto a wider social one *(fitted)*: in *Shadow of a Doubt* a girl finds her adored uncle is a murderer, then sees how little power the women around her have.

**Neighbours by contrast** (Truby's crime family; sorting questions in `how-genres-work.md`, section 6). **Crime** knows its culprit early and asks whether he will be caught, a suspense question; if most middle scenes are moves between a known wrongdoer and a pursuer, Crime leads (`crime.md`). **Horror** shows the mind failing and the killing in full; Detective shows the mind reasoning and keeps the killing offstage (`horror.md`). **Action** asks how to beat a known opponent, Thriller who the opponent really is (`action.md`). **Gangster** works the same city with money as its answer where the detective's is truth (`gangster.md`). **Test:** is the murder seen or hidden, and is the killer's identity known? Those two choices place the story among Horror, Thriller, Detective and Crime. When the hands and the mind are different people (the hired killer known from the first chapter, the one who hired him hidden), apply the test to the hidden one, since the story sorts by what it keeps back (the workshop's reading). A Thriller whose beloved is never suspected is running Detective's least likely killer inside a Thriller, and must pass Detective's fair-play checks and the two checks for the final reveal (section 9).

**Mixes named.** With Love, above all in Thriller (*Suspicion*, *Rebecca*, *Notorious*, *Rear Window*): fear and love pull against each other, the Thriller gains feeling and the Love Story gains events *(built: care times threat)*; if a Thriller feels cold, ask whose love the truth endangers. With Social Drama (*Spotlight*); Crime (*The Wire*, *Gone Girl*); Comedy and Myth (*Zootopia*); Satire (*American Vandal*); Fantasy (*The Dresden Files*); Horror (*Stranger Things*); Science Fiction (*Lost*, *Dark*). Memoir borrows a Detective or Thriller strand for drive (`memoir-and-coming-of-age.md`). On which genre leads: `how-genres-work.md`, section 8.

## 11. Twisting it

Truby's general routes above a genre are in `how-genres-work.md`, section 9. For these genres the large field of life is truth and the mind. His two higher forms *(asserted; broad enough to take in almost any serious work with an investigation)*, restated as jobs:
- **Lead the trail home** (Truby's *Cosmic Detective*). The hunt for the killer ends at the investigator's own flaw, and the blame spreads past him. **Test:** at the end, whose guilt besides the killer's has the case exposed: hers, a family's, a town's? If only his, the trail has not come home. *Oedipus Rex*, where investigator and culprit are one man, is the first case; *Vertigo* ends with the detective seeing that he could not love and helped cause a death; *Hamlet* turns the hunt for a culprit into a struggle over what to do. Tie her fate to a community (a town, a firm, a family) so her guilt has public weight.
- **Investigate knowing itself** (Truby's *Story of the Mind*). Two stories run together: the crime, and how the mind got to the truth. Make the second as dramatic as the first, with its own reversals and climax. Shapes that follow a mind rather than a clock *(fitted)*: the *spiral*, circling back to one moment from new angles, each pass deeper into the world and into her (*The Conversation*, *Blow-Up*); rival tellers who contradict each other (*Rashomon*: give each something to gain from his version); a mind re-examining itself, scenes ordered by what she realises (*Memento*); the unreliable narrator whose split self is the secret (*Fight Club*: plant moments that only make sense on a second reading).

**Moves from his cases:** poetic justice turned on the investigator (*Vertigo*, *The Conversation*); the quarry left free (*Chinatown*), an expectation overturned rather than a guarantee broken (section 4), so the ending weighs what knowing cost, not what it won, and the promised payment falls elsewhere; more than one killer, and a detective who lets a false account stand for a higher justice (*Murder on the Orient Express*, whose twelve stab wounds match a jury); the double bluff of the obvious suspect (*Knives Out*); the detective's self-story set beside the suspects' so that all read as excuses (*The Trespasser*).

**One idea from the owner theories: hide the clue in the surplus.** In most stories a detail with no purpose makes the world feel real (Implied World principle 5), and a Detective audience inspects every detail as a possible clue (section 3). Give the world real surplus, and let the one true clue sit among it looking like the rest. The world feels lived in, and the clue is fair because it was on the page. It uses the Implied World theory's split between surface and implication: the clue's surface is ordinary, and only the solution shows its implication, as with the salt water in a drowned man's lungs in *Chinatown*, which seems to point to the sea. **Test:** remove the surplus around the clue. If the audience now guesses early, the surplus was hiding it; if nothing changes, the clue was never hidden, only unmentioned.

## 12. Examples

- *Murder on the Orient Express*: reveals that build; twelve killers; the false account left standing.
- *Vertigo*: a plan built on the detective's fear; the scheme betrayed by a necklace; the payment falling on the investigator.
- *Chinatown* and *L.A. Confidential*: facades over a corrupt city; a clue that points two ways; a killer's slip over a name only the dying man spoke.
- *Knives Out* and *Columbo*: the audience moved ahead, curiosity turned to suspense; the obvious suspect as double bluff; the arrogant killer who underrates a rumpled investigator.
- *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd* and *Defending Jacob*: tellers who withhold, and the argument over when that is fair.
- *Rear Window* and *The Conversation*: an exposed amateur; a spiral around one moment; one improvised win, one loss.
- *Shadow of a Doubt*, *Suspicion*, *Gaslight*: the home as trap; trust in the beloved as the dilemma; perception attacked.
- *Shutter Island* and *Oedipus Rex*: the investigator is the guilty one. *Michael Clayton*: each thing learned makes him the next target.
- *Rashomon*, *Memento*, *Fight Club*, *Atonement*: the mind itself as the case.

## 13. Rivals and doubts

- **Trick the reader, or withhold fairly.** Truby calls plot a scheme by which author and opponent mislead the reader, and treats a narrator lying to the reader as simple fun. Gap principle 6 allows hiding only where it is part of who the teller is. *Test:* write the same lying narrator twice, once with a reason inside the story to tell it this way and signs a rereader can find, once with neither. The theory predicts the second reads as a cheat on rereading; Truby predicts both play.
- **More deception, or rules to predict with.** Truby's more deception, the stronger the plot, against the theories' limit on what is hidden from the audience; recorded in full in `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 8. *Test for Detective:* add a third layer of false trail to a working mystery. Truby predicts more pleasure; the theories predict readers stop trying to solve it and the reveal lands as arbitrary.
- **One suspect: add more, or move the uncertainty.** Truby: a single-suspect Thriller shrinks to yes or no, so borrow Detective's field of suspects. The Anticipation theory offers a second cure: move uncertainty from *whether* to *how* (Part 2 section 1): how he will strike, how she can prove it. *Test:* confirm his guilt at the midpoint and keep one suspect. The theory predicts suspense holds while the danger to her stays open; Truby predicts it sags.
- **Reveals that only speed up.** Truby wants each reveal stronger and faster towards the end. Anticipation principle 7 agrees on rising steps but says a pattern must break before the audience learns it. *Test:* leave a long quiet stretch before the last reveal; the theory predicts it hits harder than one more step in the rhythm. (The general rival, a steady climb against the ratchet: `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`, section 11.)
- **The truth as relative.** Truby praises forms whose final truth is relative, thin or absent. Gap principle 5: a pressed question is a mystery and must be settled, but the settlement can be that the truth cannot be had (principle 7). *Test:* two endings for a rival-tellers story, one that presents not knowing as the answer, one that simply stops pressing the question. The theory predicts betrayal only in the second; *Rashomon* works because not knowing becomes its answer.
- **The only fitting payment.** Truby: legal justice rarely satisfies, and poetic justice is the way to repay a planned murder *(argued, and overstated)*. *Test:* end one draft with a trial, one with a payment that echoes the crime. If the trial settles the story's question as well, the "only" does not hold.
- **Induction first.** Truby: good detectives reason from facts to theory, bad ones from a theory to the facts. The workshop's `hard-to-vary` skill, after Deutsch, holds that knowledge grows by guesses tested against evidence, which fits Truby's own account of the hunch as a first hypothesis better than his label does. Treat his induction and deduction language as informal; the craft point (avoid early certainty, reorder the facts) survives either way.
- **Doubts.** Truby's self-aware forms puncture the sense of a world going on past the page, which the Implied World theory aims for: a different goal, not a contradiction, so choose which effect the story wants. The four switches are defaults fitted from typical cases. That the Thriller hero is usually a woman, and that the Thriller grew out of Woman in Jeopardy, are fitted and speculative. Oedipus as the first detective and *Hamlet* as the model higher form are readings of single cases. Calling Detective and Love the highest genres is a value judgement, and "human nature never changes" has no test.
