# Myth

*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Genres, chapter 4, in the workshop's own words; framed by the owner theories (Implied World principles 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8 and the expansion paradox; Gap principles 2, 5, 6 and 7 and question 4; Anticipation principles 1, 3, 5 and 7; Bond levers, chiefly Desire, Vulnerability, Flaw, Competence, Connection, Agency and Specificity, the ladder of care, and principles 5 and 7).*

## 1. In one line

A hero sets out across a whole world after one concrete goal, each place on the journey strips away a layer, and at the end they learn their destiny and come home, or to a new home, reborn.

Words kept apart in this file: *Myth*, with a capital, is the genre; *myths* are the old stories it grew from. *Departure* keeps its Implied World meaning (a way the world differs from the baseline), so a Myth hero *sets out*. *Wonder* and *mystery* keep their Gap meanings (an open question the story promises to leave open, or to answer). *Destiny* is Truby's word for the task a person came into life to do, which he says comes from their own abilities, not from the gods. *Male Myth* and *Female Myth* are his names for two stances, conquest in a line and growth in a cycle (section 10). Truby's own summary *(asserted)*: Myth is the story of a self growing across a whole life, from birth through death to rebirth, and its deep subject is immortality.

## 2. The promise

The audience comes for *(fitted, from Truby's profile of the genre)*:
- **A world to get lost in**, and a long journey through it. A large place tied to nature (forest, river, desert, ice, sea, underworld), with more in it than the story visits, crossed region by region past many opponents met in turn.
- **Change made visible.** A person growing into what they could always have been. Truby counts this Myth's great invention *(built)*: change inside a person is rare and hard to see, so the genre lines up the outer journey with the inner one, and each place the hero reaches stands for a step of inner change.
- **A brush with death and a return**, and at the top, **a vision** of how a whole people should live.

In Gap terms, the breach (what breaks the world's normal) often arrives with an object: in Truby's account the inciting event in Myth is usually the hero getting a *talisman*, an object of unknown power (section 6). The open question the genre presses hardest is who this hero really is and what their destiny is. Characters chase it and the story keeps coming back to it, so by Gap principle 5 it is a mystery, which the story promises to answer. A Myth that ends with nothing new known about who the hero is has broken that promise; even a tragedy pays it, only too late for the hero to use.

## 3. The baseline

The *baseline* (Implied World theory) is what the audience assumes by default: the real world plus the habits of the genre. Before being told, a Myth audience assumes *(fitted, from Truby's beats and examples)*:
- **an ordered world**: a ranking of beings from gods and spirits through rulers and commoners down to animals, plants and stones (the old *Great Chain of Being*, which Truby says makers of Myth, Fantasy and Science Fiction worlds still lean on), where nothing on the journey happens by chance;
- **a home that confines**, often conformist or ruled by a tyrant, with family pushing the hero down an absent father's path; **two cultures opposed** on the values that matter most; and a kingdom whose health mirrors its ruler;
- **magic, or a key technology, with rules**, and **nature alive with meaning**: forest, labyrinth, underworld, garden and tree each carry an old symbolic sense;
- **destiny as real**, and **familiar archetypes** (section 5): the warrior, the wise teacher, the trickster.

**Case.** Say "a quest to destroy a dark lord's weapon" and the audience has furnished the wise teacher, the fellowship, the dark tower and the final mountain before the first scene ends. That is cheap, which is the baseline's use: the attention budget (Implied World principle 2) can go on a few departures that matter. It is also Myth's usual failure. The Implied World theory names it: a fantasy that is medieval Europe with dragons has spent almost nothing, and feels generic because it is riding on the genre's defaults.

**Point.** Truby's cure *(built)* is to design from values rather than from the look of the place: split the world along its *value conflict* (the two sets of values its cultures fight over) and let each region (his *subworld*) take a side. The Implied World theory agrees on the main departures (principle 8, thematic fit) and adds what Truby leaves out: business and surplus detail that serve neither side (section 13). **Test:** swap a region for any other of its kind (any enchanted forest, any mine under a mountain). If nothing in the story changes, it is baseline furniture. For the repair, hand to `.claude/skills/story-world/references/world-from-story.md` and `.claude/skills/story-world/references/diagnosing-a-world.md`.

## 4. Guarantees, and which to break

A *guarantee* is a protection the audience trusts the genre to give without being told (Anticipation principle 5). Truby does not discuss guarantees; the table is the workshop's, drawn from his beats and examples and from well-known works. Some of what a Myth audience expects of its ending is a promise, not a protection (the destined hero does the destined deed; the climax is a battle the right side wins); turning those is a twist (section 11).

| Guarantee the audience leans on | Broken by | What breaking it buys |
|---|---|---|
| The hero comes home | Frodo saves the Shire but cannot stay in it (*The Lord of the Rings*); Jake makes a new home (*Avatar*) | The journey's cost stays visible after it ends |
| The hero is reborn | Achilles dies unchanged; what lasts of him is his shame, not a new self | Tragedy: a self-revelation that comes too late to use |
| The hero grows into the role | Adam in Genesis; Anakin Skywalker, who fails to change and brings down the order he was meant to save | A whole society falls with the hero |
| The teacher is wise, the strongest ally real | Glinda holds back the answer and the Wizard is a fraud whose test still works (*The Wizard of Oz*); Gandalf falls in Moria | The hero must act without a guide |

**Which to break.** One, early and convincingly, not all of them: if everyone falls, care collapses and suspense with it. Myth's journey is long, so a single early loss on the hero's side keeps the whole middle dangerous: once the teacher falls in the mines, every later threat is real. A guarantee broken and then restored (Gandalf returns) teaches the audience that losses can be undone, which weakens later threats; Myth can afford it only because rebirth is its own pattern, and only if the return brings change, as his does (the workshop's reading). The deepest guarantee is rebirth. Truby's contrast *(built)*: Myth grants some form of immortality, if only through the tale, while tragedy cuts life short with no rebirth. Break it only if you mean to write a tragedy. Gilgamesh does not break it: he fails to win the literal immortality he hunts, but lives on through the tale (section 7), which is Myth's own answer. **Test by removal:** restore the broken guarantee and ask whether the scenes after it lose their danger. If not, the break was doing no work. For a break that comes at the end (home, rebirth), ask instead what the ending would mean with it restored. Destiny is itself a guarantee, and it costs uncertainty (section 9).

## 5. Hero and opponent

**Hero: a seeker on a journey** *(fitted)*. Like a detective, the Myth hero looks for something, but not a culprit: a fleece, a way home, a wizard, and on the way finds what their own life means. The search almost always forces a dangerous journey through hostile ground. Most Myth heroes also carry an *archetype*, Truby's term here for a broad kind of person defined by a social role and the flaw built into it; this is chosen separately from the hero type, since every Myth hero seeks. Each archetype has a strength and a *shadow* (Truby's term), the flaw that grows from the same root, so the flaw is organic, not bolted on *(built)*. He names five: warrior, searcher, teacher, rebel and trickster. **Case.** Dorothy's strength is her longing for something beyond the farm. Push it too far and it becomes her flaw: she cannot see the worth of the home and the people she already has, which is exactly what the journey has to teach her. **Test:** name the hero's archetype and its shadow. If the flaw is not that shadow, ask whether it reads as arbitrary. Twisting an archetype makes the heroes Truby admires *(fitted)*: turn over one defining quality, as with Frodo, the small hero whose strength is heart and stubbornness, and Aragorn, the warrior-king who doubts he deserves the throne.

**Flaw, need and desire.** The flaw (Truby's *weakness*) is usually the archetype's shadow; often an effort to live up to a lost or idealised father *(fitted, from Campbell's pattern of a hard birth and a lost father)*; and best of all, the home world's organising principle in personal form (section 6). The need: to grow into the role, know oneself and take up a duty to others; in the Female Myth, to connect and belong, as Dorothy needs to learn to love the family she has. The desire: a concrete goal at a fixed end point (Mount Doom, the Emerald City, home), secretly tied to the need, so that getting there forces the hero to face themselves *(built)*. Myth's circle means that arrival reveals what was packed at the start, which Truby calls the genre's central irony. At the end the first goal gives way to the true one, knowing their destiny.

**Opponent.** A journey breeds strangers met one after another, and that is the genre's biggest plot problem (section 6). Truby's cures *(built)*: each opponent stands for a part of the hero's flaw and attacks it, so each defeat gives a small self-revelation; one main opponent stays in play from first to last, with a personal tie to the hero; and, his favourite, the family comes on the journey (his *Family Trip*), borrowing drama's habit of making the opponents relatives. *Little Miss Sunshine* sends a whole family to a child's pageant, and the father's impossible standards are the steady opposition while strangers supply the episodes. Common forms: the tyrant king, the dark lord after total power, the witch, the invading machine. An opponent needs a want that means something: Truby faults *Avatar* for a mineral named as a joke (a *MacGuffin*, Hitchcock's word for a goal that means nothing in itself) and for villains with no inner life. For building either figure: `.claude/skills/character/references/opponents.md` and `.claude/skills/character/references/building-a-cast.md`.

## 6. The beats, as jobs

These beats are *fitted*: drawn from past stories (Greek and Near Eastern epic, Tolkien, Oz, Hollywood). A missing beat is a question, not a fault: ask what job it does and whether the draft gets that job done another way. Names and grouping are the workshop's. Test any beat with `hard-to-vary`: remove it and see which later scene loses its footing.

| Beat | The job it does | If the draft lacks it, ask... |
|---|---|---|
| A world built from values | One *arena* (Truby's term for the single bounded place the story happens in: Middle-earth, or Oz ringed by desert), its regions built from the value conflict, and a home whose rule is the hero's flaw at large, so that solving one works on the other *(built; Truby's story-world method, in brief)* | Where does this world end? Which side of the value conflict does each region stand for? Is home's rule the hero's flaw, believed? For the building work, see `.claude/skills/story-world/references/world-from-story.md`. |
| Wound or fresh start | A hard birth and a lost father plant the need; a modern Myth may open at a rebirth (a new body, name or role) to reach desire fast *(built: desire drives the plot)* | How soon does the hero want something, and have we seen the normal that could be lost (the Anticipation theory's care term)? |
| The object of unknown power | The talisman (Excalibur, the ruby slippers): an object whose power nobody has measured, not even the hero, and which stands in for part of who the hero is; getting it starts the goal *(fitted, specific enough to test)* | What does the hero carry whose power is unknown, and does its reveal land with the self-revelation? |
| A fixed end | A named destination, tied to the need, so progress can be measured and arrival forces a reckoning *(built)* | Can you name the exact place or act that ends the journey? |
| Each stop a step | Every place the journey stops at stands for a stage of inner change *(built; the one-to-one match is a strong prescription, not proven necessary)* | Beside each stop, what inner step? A stop with none is scenery; a step with no stop is invisible. |
| The long meander | A winding journey through many levels of society, so the plot maps the whole world (Truby's *meander*) *(built)* | Do the encounters come from several levels of this society, or all from one? |
| Allies who lack | Allies, often with a wise teacher; the best each believe they lack something, as the hero does, and find they had it all along | What does each ally think they lack, and does the journey show they had it? |
| Opponents that build | Each opponent attacks part of the flaw; the hero reads each method and counters it; small self-revelations stack into an inner arc | Could two encounters swap places with no loss? Then the story repeats itself (Truby's *hitting the same beat*). |
| Among the dead | A descent to the dead, or a *gauntlet* (a run past terrors, like the long walk to the Wizard), often the apparent defeat: the hero sees an unexamined part of themselves and is pushed to act while time remains *(fitted)* | Where does the hero face death, and what does it show them about themselves? |
| The deciding act | A final battle in the warrior's Myth; the Female Myth may replace the battle with another kind of act | If the climax is not a fight, what act decides it, and is it the hero's own? |
| Revelation for the kingdom | The self-revelation goes *public* (a duty to lead others) and may go *cosmic* (a moral vision of how a whole people should live); Truby insists it be detailed *(built)* | What does the hero's change mean for others, in rules or acts rather than a slogan? |
| Home, reborn | The hero comes back, or reaches a new home, having left the old code behind rather than adjusting it, often in a new bond *(fitted and built)* | Has the hero outgrown the old rules, and what new act or bond shows it? |

**One scene, many jobs.** Beats are jobs, not scenes. When Dorothy lands among the Munchkins, one scene hands her the talisman, the main opponent, a teacher and a road to follow. **A short myth first.** *The Wizard of Oz* runs a quick version of the whole shape on the farm (a flaw, a wish to escape, a clash over the dog, a short trip, a return) before the long one in Oz, so the long journey echoes it.

**Symbols.** Truby ranks Myth with Horror as the most symbolic genres *(asserted)*: through the middle, each creature and object the hero confronts should stand for something inside the hero. Tradition supplies meanings (the labyrinth for confusion before understanding, the underworld for death and the unexplored self, the garden for living by nature's law), but he also holds that no symbol's meaning is fixed. Decide whether you use the old meaning or turn it over, and make the context say which. More: `.claude/skills/plot/references/symbols.md`.

## 7. The genre's question

Truby's root question *(asserted)*: what does it mean to be an animal that knows it is itself, and knows it will die? His picture of life in Myth, its *Mind-Action story view* (a genre's view of the world joined to its advice for living): a self is made by hard, active searching rather than by "just being yourself", and to be is to become. The theme is immortality, sought literally and then found in humbler forms. **Case.** Gilgamesh loses his friend, hunts for a way to live for ever and fails; the last reveal is that the storyteller is Gilgamesh himself, and the tale is what lasts. Truby calls this *natural immortality*: living on in other people's memories and retellings.

His *thematic recipe* (a genre's plan for living well) grows from the hero's basic action, seeking *(built)*. In gist: seek your destiny, keep checking what your acts do to others, and hold your ambition below the web of life. The plot gives the theme a body, so test a draft there: which scene shows the old self dying, and what does the hero do differently afterwards?

**As a question for a draft:** what is this hero really looking for, what will they find their destiny to be, and what of them will last? Truby then undercuts his own recipe *(built)*: people are born able to do many things, so one inborn destiny cannot be the whole answer; the harder question is which abilities to grow, and how. A hero who chooses among real possibilities, rather than uncovering a fixed destiny, gives the self-revelation more weight, and the Bond theory's Agency lever agrees. In Gap terms, a theme is the story's answer to the question its world poses, and by Implied World principle 8 the world's main departure should embody that question. **Case.** In *Black Panther* the departure is a rich, advanced nation posing as poor to avoid being plundered. Its flaw, keeping its gifts from others, is the hero's flaw too, and his self-revelation is the nation's turn: to share. Truby's lesson *(built)*: when hero and nation share one flaw at two scales, one change serves both.

## 8. Where the care comes from

In the Bond theory, care is a relationship the story builds, not a verdict on traits. Myth leans on these levers:
- **Desire and Vulnerability**, which together reach the Worry rung: a concrete goal with an end point the audience can see, carried by a hero who can be hurt, through twisted archetypes (Frodo's smallness, Dorothy as an orphan child) and the apparent defeat among the dead.
- **Connection**, the genre's strongest lever: allies, teacher, family on the journey, the return, a marriage. Care is social (Bond principle 5): Sam's devotion makes Frodo matter; three devoted friends make Dorothy matter.
- **Flaw**, from the archetype's shadow. A hero who can be hurt only in the body has no real flaw: Truby traces how superheroes, from Superman and kryptonite, gained moral and mental flaws (the Hulk's rage, Thor's arrogance, Stark's vanity), and good plots test those *(built)*.
- **Competence** for the warrior, capped by the limits on power (section 9): the Bond theory's Superman problem. **Humour** for the trickster, whom Truby calls the audience's favourite archetype, since watching the powerful conned is a pleasure. **Surprise** (Bond principle 7) is built into the central irony: the hero does something unexpected that was in them all along.

**Run low: Specificity.** An archetype is recognised at once as a kind of person; the Bond theory's Recognise rung needs a particular person. The archetype sets the role, and habits, voice and history must make the person. Myth trades in archetypes, so this is where its drafts go flat. **The Miss rung** comes from the length of the journey, and from origin stories that launch a series: audiences often love these most, Truby notes, because they watch the hero being formed *(asserted)*.

## 9. Suspense and knowledge

**The equation.** Suspense = Care × Threat × Uncertainty × Time. Myth's weak term is uncertainty. A prophecy or a Chosen One tells the audience the hero is meant to win, and Truby does not weigh the cost. The Anticipation theory's answer is to move the uncertainty from *whether* to *how*; the workshop adds, from the Gap theory's settlement, *what it costs*. *The Lord of the Rings* does both: the quest succeeds, but the hero fails at the last step, and he and the Shire pay.

**Threat and time on a long journey.** A parade of strangers resets the threat each episode. The ratchet (Anticipation principle 7) wants tension to rise in steps with partial releases, and the pattern to break before the audience learns it: that is the mechanism under Truby's warning about hitting the same beat. An ongoing main opponent keeps the threat in view between the first clash and the last; *The Wizard of Oz* has its Witch mainly at the two ends of the journey and patches the middle with threats along the way. The fixed end point gives time a shape, and a clock can close in, like the hourglass in the Witch's castle.

**Show the rules, hide the thing.** Truby's *Magic System* asks for the rules of any power in writing, above all its limits and whether it can be lost *(built)*. The owner theories say why. Stated limits let the audience predict danger (Anticipation principle 3), and a power with no limit leaves nothing to fear for, which drains the uncertainty term (principle 1; the Bond theory's Superman problem). The power's nature and source can stay hidden.

**Mysteries and wonders.** The talisman's unknown power is a mystery: pressed, and paid by the end. The world's deep history, the songs never sung in full, the figure never explained, are wonders, and much of a Myth world's depth lives in them (Implied World principles 6 and 7; Tolkien is the owner theory's own model). Keep each on its side: a wonder that characters start to chase becomes a mystery the story then owes.

**Knowledge position and the teller.** Myth often puts the audience ahead of the hero about the hero: we suspect the heritage, the prophecy or the talisman's power before they do, which gives dramatic irony. Gap question 4 is a test of the teller (who or what delivers the plot): is anything held back only for a twist, rather than because the teller couldn't or wouldn't say? Glinda knows all along that the slippers could take Dorothy home. The film's telling stays close to Dorothy and never enters Glinda's mind, so holding the answer back until Dorothy learns it is fair. Whether Glinda has a good reason to keep quiet is a separate question, of character rather than of the plot's fairness: hand it to `character`. The Gilgamesh frame uses the teller itself (Gap principle 6) as the last reveal: the hero turns out to have been telling the tale, which recasts it as the immortality he failed to win.

**Settlement.** The ending turns a sequence into a shape (Gap principle 7), and Myth's shape is the circle: the return recasts the journey as the way back to what was always there. Decide whether the ending brings a rebirth; rebirth makes Myth, a self-revelation that comes too late makes tragedy. More on each principle: `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md` and `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`.

## 10. Subgenres and mixes

**Two stances** *(asserted, from a history of hunting and farming peoples)*: the Male Myth, a straight line through a physical journey to a violent battle (Truby's "divide and conquer"); and the Female Myth, a cycle with repeated rebirths (his "combine and grow"), with power often held by women. The workshop keeps the stances and doubts the gender labels (section 13). Truby limits Campbell's monomyth to the warrior's Myth rather than the one story beneath all stories *(built, from the stories Campbell sampled)*. The workshop's test: if an outline was built from hero's-journey steps and the hero is not a warrior learning to lead (a child, a carer, a community), drop the steps that only make sense for a fighter.

**Subgenres** (names Truby's unless noted): the warrior's Myth; the Female Myth (*The Wizard of Oz*, *Arrival*, *Inside Out*); the *Ecological Myth*, whose job is to reconcile technology and nature in one hero, and its dark twin the *Machine in the Garden* (Leo Marx's title), where that reconciliation fails; the *Creation Myth*, a people's story of where its own values came from; the *Origin Story*, how one hero begins, the superhero's usual form; the Myth/Science Fiction epic, which follows a species or a culture across a vast span (*2001: A Space Odyssey*); and the comic Myth (*Little Miss Sunshine*).

**Mixes named:** with Fantasy (*The Wizard of Oz* is mainly Fantasy); with Science Fiction (*The Matrix*, *Dune*, *Star Wars*); with Crime (*The Dark Knight*); with Action (the *Iliad*); with Horror (Genesis, which Truby reads as a paradise-lost Horror that turns into Crime). **Neighbours:** Fantasy shares the built world and the passage into a magic land (`fantasy.md`); Science Fiction borrows Myth's long arc for stories of whole species (`science-fiction.md`); Memoir and Coming-of-Age take up how a self is defined, where Myth's single destiny falls short (`memoir-and-coming-of-age.md`). On which genre leads in a mix, see `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`.

## 11. Twisting it

Truby's general routes above a genre are in `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`. Besides twisting an archetype (section 5) and letting the hero choose among real abilities (section 7), his Myth chapter names one route above all: **follow a culture, not only a self** *(asserted)*. Transcendent Myths track the birth and growth of a people or an age alongside one hero: Creation Myths and Myth/Science Fiction epics, the Female Myth, the Ecological Myth. Concrete moves from the chapter:
- **Match the hero's growth to the nation's**: one flaw at two scales (*Black Panther*).
- **Stage change as several rebirths.** *Avatar* gives Jake a new role (his dead twin's place), a new body, a new belonging (the clan joined in a ring about him) and a new self at the sacred tree. Pair a talisman of who the hero is (a weapon) with one of who they will become (seeds that settle on him), and let the two meet in one image.
- **Make the cosmic vision moral and detailed.** Truby's case against *2001* *(built, apart from calling it dull, which is taste)*: stages of society without a lived-in culture feel like a lecture; a vision made only of images offers no way to live; and a feature film may be too small for the arc, where a serial (*Westworld*'s first season) has room.
- **Move from shame to guilt.** Achilles fears disgrace in others' eyes (Truby's *shame culture*); Odysseus, choosing a mortal life with Penelope over an undying name, moves towards judging himself by his own conscience (*guilt culture*). Show the shift through a choice between glory and an ordinary shared life.
- **Turn the ending's promises** (section 4) rather than break a guarantee. The destined hero need not do the destined deed: Frodo claims the Ring at the fire, and another's greed destroys it, so the goal is reached without the hero's strength. The climax need not be a battle the right side wins: Dorothy never fights, and in the *Machine in the Garden* the defender of nature loses, a loss the audience carries out.

**One idea from the owner theories: keep the chosen-ness a wonder.** Say *that* the hero is chosen and what it demands, never *why*. The Implied World theory's expansion paradox has the case: the midi-chlorians turned a Force that worked because it was unexplained into a blood test, and made being chosen a count. The Gap theory calls this deflation. **Test:** add a scene that explains the mechanism of the hero's destiny. The theory predicts the world feels smaller and the hero less, because a gap the audience's imagination was filling has been made canon.

## 12. Examples

- *Epic of Gilgamesh*, the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey*: a failed hunt for immortality and the hero as teller; shame giving way to guilt; the return to a marriage.
- *The Lord of the Rings*: a bounded arena with seemingly endless regions; regions built from values; two twisted heroes cut together; a quest won through the hero's failure at the last step.
- *The Wizard of Oz* (film and novel): a Female Myth; a short myth in Kansas first; a talisman whose power the opponent's desire reveals; allies who already have what they seek; a fraud whose test still works; a climax with no battle.
- *Avatar*: a warrior turned leader through four rebirths; the Ecological Myth; a meaningless goal and flat villains as warnings.
- *Black Panther*: an origin story that is also a nation's founding; one flaw at two scales.
- *Little Miss Sunshine*: the family on the journey as steady opposition, in a comic Myth.
- *2001: A Space Odyssey*: evolution staged as a line of characters; the most human mind in the machine; a vision without a moral.
- *Star Wars*: the Chosen One who rises and the one who falls; a wonder explained into a count.

## 13. Rivals and doubts

- **The world serves the hero, or has business of its own.** Truby designs the world from the characters' value fight. Implied World principles 4 and 5: a world feels real when it has business of its own and details that serve no purpose. Applied strictly, Truby's rule builds the set-dressing world the theory warns against. *Test:* give one region a concern that has nothing to do with the value conflict (a market day, a feud, a festival), mentioned in passing; the theory predicts the region feels more real and the value contrast loses nothing. The workshop's reconciliation: values design the main departures, and business and surplus go on top.
- **A rich world shown, or a deep world implied.** Truby wants a world rich enough to live in. That fits the owner theory if the richness sits in the reserve (what the creator knows but never shows), and pulls against the attention budget and the iceberg ratio if it is laid out on the surface. *Test:* replace a chapter of lore with three passing mentions; the theory predicts the depth holds or grows.
- **Destiny and uncertainty.** Truby's Chosen One and destiny tell the audience the hero is meant to win, and he does not weigh the cost; the Anticipation theory's uncertainty term says suspense needs the audience never to feel sure (section 9). *Test:* reveal the prophecy early in one version and late in another; the theory predicts the early version keeps its suspense only if how stays open (and, adding the Gap theory's settlement, at what cost).
- **A climax by accident.** Truby offers the Witch's melting as the Female Myth's alternative to battle. By the Bond theory's Agency lever (acts rather than reacts), a win decided by chance gives the hero little credit. Dorothy's act, throwing water to save the burning Scarecrow, is her own; its effect on the Witch is luck. *Test:* keep the climax without a battle, but make the deciding act a choice to save a friend that achieves what she meant it to; the theory predicts the ending lands harder. (Truby's own quibble: a villain whom water kills would not keep a bucket of it near.)
- **Archetypes or persons.** Truby builds the cast from archetypes, recognised at once; the Bond theory's Recognise rung needs a particular person (section 8). *Test:* swap the hero for any other figure of the same archetype; if no scene changes, there is a role but not yet a person.
- **Doubts.** The Male and Female labels tie two structural stances (conquest and a line; growth and a cycle) to gender; the owner theories are gender-neutral, and the stances work without the labels for heroes of any gender. The critique of Campbell rests on the sample he drew from, and the history behind it is broad and second-hand. "Myth is the mind's first story shape", "drama is refined Myth" and "religion is formalised myth" are asserted, not mechanisms. The lists of transcendent Myths stretch the word until it covers almost any large work. Truby says Myth uses symbols with settled meanings and also that no symbol's meaning is fixed, and reconciles the two only through context. Truby's fast start at a rebirth pulls mildly against showing the normal before the breach, so that care comes first (see the beat *Wound or fresh start*). The beats are fitted from a narrow set of stories: ancient epic, Tolkien, Oz and Hollywood.
