# Fantasy

*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Genres, chapter 11, in the workshop's own words; framed by the owner theories (the Implied World theory throughout: baseline, departure, surface, reserve, gap, principles 1 to 8, the Tolkien case, the guide for worlds with many departures and the expansion paradox; Gap principles 2, 5 and 7 and Part 2 sections 4 and 6; Anticipation principles 2 to 7; Bond levers, chiefly Flaw, Desire, Vulnerability, Competence, Agency, Connection and Humour, and principles 5 and 6).*

Contents: 1 In one line. 2 The promise. 3 The baseline. 4 Guarantees, and which to break. 5 Hero and opponent. 6 The beats, as jobs. 7 The genre's question. 8 Where the care comes from. 9 Suspense and knowledge. 10 Subgenres and mixes. 11 Twisting it. 12 Examples. 13 Rivals and doubts.

## 1. In one line

Fantasy takes a hero held back by her ordinary world, sends her across into a world that rewards what she lacks, and brings her home able to see possibility where she saw only routine.

Words kept apart in this file: *Fantasy*, with a capital, is the genre. *Wonder* keeps only its Gap meaning, an open question the story promises to leave open; the feeling the genre sells, delight at the impossible made real, is *enchantment* here, though the Gap theory's table also calls it wonder. *Magic* is the other world's powers and their rules; Truby's larger sense, the untapped potential in every person and place, is *hidden possibility*. A *guide* keeps its Implied World meaning (section 3); Truby's rescuer from outside is *the visiting helper*, never a guide. A *gap* (Implied World sense) is something left unfilled on purpose. *Home* is the ordinary world where the hero starts, *the other world* the fantastic one, *the crossing* the way between (Truby's *passageway*), and a *subworld* (his term) one region of the other world with values of its own. Truby's framing *(asserted)*: the genre is about growth in quality, not quantity; the hero wants to become more of herself, not to get more or to become someone else.

## 2. The promise

The audience comes for *(fitted, from Truby's profile of the genre)*:
- **A world to live in**, somewhere to sink into and want to return to (Truby says the genre stands or falls on its world, *asserted*), and **the crossing**, the moment the ordinary rules switch off.
- **A run of enchanting places and playful creatures** (Truby's advice to enjoy the creatures, *asserted*, serves this promise), and at least one moment where the other world works perfectly (Truby's *super magical moment*): the ceiling tea party in *Mary Poppins*, the game with long-dead players in *Field of Dreams*.
- **Secrets about how the world works**, uncovered, and **a hopeful return**: home looks changed, and the audience senses the same change is open to them. In the darker forms, a society gone wrong set beside one that works.

In Gap terms the genre runs mainly on the departure gap (between reality and a world), and it has two: home departs little, the other world a great deal. What sets off the crossing is the *breach*, the event that breaks home's own normal (Gap principle 2): the letters that flood the Dursleys' house. *The Wizard of Oz* breaches twice, the cyclone breaking the farm's normal and Dorothy's falling house breaking Oz's, killing a witch and freeing a people. The changed home is the *settlement* (Gap principle 7): how the opening normal differs at the end, and what that cost. Every open question carries a promise (Gap principle 5), and a Fantasy story usually makes both kinds by where it puts its emphasis: the rules and the opponent's grip, once the plot presses them, are mysteries, owed an answer; where the magic comes from is usually a wonder, never pressed and promised to stay open.

## 3. The baseline

The *baseline* (Implied World theory) is what the audience assumes by default: the real world plus the habits of the genre. Say "elven city" and a Fantasy audience has furnished it from a hundred earlier ones. Unless told otherwise it assumes *(fitted, from Truby's beats and examples)*:
- **magic exists and keeps rules whose source is never explained**; animals and objects may talk or have a spirit; there is good magic and dark, and the opponent holds the dark;
- **two worlds and a way between them**, and the hero will come home; the other world sits at an extreme, a dream or a nightmare, and even a dream has a catch; **someone local will explain the rules on arrival**;
- **pre-industrial furniture**: kingdoms, castles, wands, witches, doors into elsewhere. Truby calls the "back to nature, no technology" reading a mistake *(asserted)*: magic and tools drawn from nature are this world's technology, and deserve the same rigour.

**Case: the generic draft.** The Implied World theory's own example of a generic world is a Fantasy that is medieval Europe with dragons: it has spent almost nothing, and feels generic because it rides on the genre's defaults. The cure is not to invent everything but to choose a few *departures* (ways the world differs from the baseline) that matter and let the baseline carry the rest (principles 1 and 2). *Game of Thrones* takes the medieval furniture almost whole, which is cheap, and spends on a few departures: seasons that last for years, and above all one from the genre's habits, that honour and innocence protect nobody. The genre's habits are part of the baseline, so departing from one counts. Diagnosis in full: `.claude/skills/story-world/references/diagnosing-a-world.md`, section 3.

**Point: build the iceberg, show the tip.** Tolkien is the owner theory's model, and the genre's. Songs about stories we never hear, a Bombadil nobody explains, the Entwives lost offstage: the *surface* (what is shown) is small, the *reserve* (what the creator knows but never shows) is vast, and the appendices hold it. Depth comes from what the audience senses below (principle 6); the deliberate gaps are where the reader's imagination works and grows attached (principle 7). A good gap has edges: the audience knows what kind of thing is missing (a war, a lost people, an older magic). Truby's method pulls the other way at points (section 13).

**What to spend on** (Truby's world method, regrouped as jobs; *built*, except where marked):
- **One image for the whole world.** A single metaphor holds character, plot and theme in one picture and makes knock-on effects predictable (ripple, principle 3): Wonderland, a world of inverted logic, set against a girl who sorts everything, herself included, into fixed boxes. **Test:** say the image in five words, then check a few places, rules and creatures against it; each extends it or dilutes it.
- **Rules to predict with, and magic with limits.** Vague rules are the commonest large mistake *(asserted)*: an audience that cannot predict feels the stakes as arbitrary. For each power, settle who holds it, how it is won or lost, what it cannot do, and whether a place or a price binds it. The answers can stay in the reserve; only their effects need reach the surface.
- **The world's state as values made visible.** Truby's worlds lean to extremes, free or enslaved, each the outward form of someone's values *(built in part)*: Bedford Falls runs on George's decency, Pottersville on Potter's money and fear. Home and the other world are designed against each other in the same way (Truby's *double arena*; section 6).

**The guide.** Worlds with many departures need a guide, or the story feels hard going (the Implied World theory's prediction): an outsider who learns alongside us, or an outside aid such as a map. The theory's own outsiders are mostly this genre's: Alice, Dorothy, Harry. The crossing marks where the departures pile up; the outsider learns which ones apply, while the baseline (the real world plus the genre's habits) still carries everything the story leaves unchanged (`.claude/skills/story-world/references/world-materials.md`, section 8), and Truby's rule to show the rules on arrival *(fitted)* puts the guide to work at once: Glinda meets Dorothy on landing. A world with no crossing needs another guide: provincials who have never left home (the hobbits), a map (Tolkien's; the moving map in *Game of Thrones*' opening titles), a prologue. **Test:** count the invented rules the plot will need the audience to use in the first chapter or ten minutes (each draws on attention the plot also needs, principle 2). For each, who learns it alongside us, or what aid (a map, a prologue) carries it? If neither, it has reached the surface too soon. Names dropped in passing and taken for granted by the people who live there are surplus or gaps with edges, not a fault.

## 4. Guarantees, and which to break

A *guarantee* is a protection the audience trusts the genre to give without being told. Anticipation principle 5: fear needs the audience to believe the usual protections are gone; break one early and convincingly and every later threat is real, break them all and care collapses. Fantasy offers more comfort than most genres (a safe return, good outlasting dark), so its threats go soft easily. Truby does not discuss guarantees; the table is the workshop's, from his beats and examples. The last row can break either way: late, as in *Pan's Labyrinth*, it buys weight for the ending rather than fear for later scenes; early, by showing someone who did not come back, it makes every later threat real (below).

| Guarantee the audience leans on | Broken by | What breaking it buys |
|---|---|---|
| The apparent lead survives | Ned Stark's execution (*Game of Thrones*) | Every lead's danger is real from then on |
| The wise protector will step in | Dumbledore's death (*Half-Blood Prince*) | No rescue is left; her acts are her own |
| The refuge is safe | enemies inside Hogwarts; Truby's *paradise lost*, a paradise ruined by its own people | Lost refuge (Anticipation principle 4): nowhere left to stand |
| The child gets home safe (an expected ending kept as a protection for the child) | *Pan's Labyrinth* | Every task was for real; the safe return was never owed |

- **The rules hold: a promise, not a guarantee.** Breaking it leaves the audience cheated, not unsafe (`.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`, section 2). The magic's rules work like mysteries: stated, tested and kept. A rule that bends when the plot needs it is a broken promise, not a twist, and a power with consequences nobody planned can break the world. The owner theory's case is the Time-Turner in *Prisoner of Azkaban*: it implied any disaster could be undone, so the series had to destroy them all.
- **The hopeful return: a promise, not a guarantee.** Home seen new is what the genre promises (section 2), and it keeps nobody safe. A draft that never settles the return at all, or brings her home unchanged, drops it, and the audience feels cheated. How she comes home is an expectation, and a return that costs is a twist that keeps the promise, not a broken guarantee: Frodo saves the Shire but cannot stay in it (*The Lord of the Rings*; `myth.md`, section 11, reads it the same way).
- **The funnel's winner earns it: also a promise.** Where many contenders narrow to one prize, the winner must have competed for it and must be the best answer to the story's question (Truby, *built* on the promise mechanism; his verdict on *Game of Thrones*' ending is opinion). A winner who never played cheats, like a whodunit whose culprit could not have done it.
- **Which one to break.** One, early, and preferably the child's safe return (the last row), the protection the genre leans on hardest (the workshop's reading of Anticipation principle 5). Show that someone before her did not come back (the ghost children in *Coraline*), and the catch becomes real: she might not return. **Test by removal:** restore the guarantee in the outline (Ned lives; the ghost children were never taken) and ask whether later scenes lose their danger. If not, the break was decoration.

## 5. Hero and opponent

**Hero: the explorer** *(fitted)*. Home keeps her small; the other world is where she finds out what she could be, so each place should tell her something about herself, not only about the world. Often a child (Alice, Dorothy, Harry), or adults who have lost the habit of play (*Big*, *Field of Dreams*).

**Flaw, need and desire.** The flaw (Truby's *weakness*) is rigidity *(fitted)*: a life run on fixed boxes, grown dull and shut to play; home's bad values make it worse. Truby's key move *(built)*: make the flaw the opposite of what the other world rewards, so the world tests it at every step. Work from the hero (name the flaw, then build a world that rewards its opposite) or from the place (name somewhere you would love to go, ask what it rewards, then build a hero who lacks exactly that). The need is mostly psychological, to open up to possibility and play; where there is a moral need, it is usually to give more and take less for granted (Scrooge) *(fitted)*. **Bond warning:** a flaw such as "doesn't know how to have fun" is cosmetic unless it costs her something real. Truby's cure *(built)*, his *slow open*, is to let the flaw do its damage at home before anyone crosses; the Anticipation theory asks for the same order for its own reason, care before the breach. **Test:** find the scene before the crossing where the flaw costs her something she wants; if the flaw is only told, write that scene.

The desire *(built)*: a goal she cannot reach without crossing, travelling through several subworlds and fighting what lives there, so that learning how the world works is part of winning. Dorothy's wish to go somewhere better lands her in Oz, then becomes the wish to go home, which drags her across the land.

**Opponent: the frozen ruler** *(fitted)*. A controlling authority who runs the system: strict rules, small wishes, money and position above dreams; a frozen society in one person, often with dark magic (the Queen of Hearts, the Wicked Witch of the West, Potter, Mr Banks). Truby's two moves *(built)*: make the hero force the ruler to use his powers, since a power never used is a threat never felt; and consider giving the ruler the change, since the system can only thaw if he does (Mr Banks ends up flying a kite with his children). In Bond terms (principle 6) a frozen ruler risks being a function rather than a person. A want of his own, something he fears to lose and habits of his own make him one (the Desire, Vulnerability and Specificity levers); the change is Truby's route, not the Bond test. In Social Fantasy the head of a whole society takes the seat. For building either figure: `.claude/skills/character/references/opponents.md` (section 11) and `.claude/skills/character/references/change-and-growth.md`.

## 6. The beats, as jobs

A *beat* is one of the signature events a genre's audience expects. Truby calls Fantasy loose, its beats landmarks on a round trip from home to the other world and back *(asserted)*. They are fitted, drawn from past stories, mostly portal stories: a missing beat is a question, not a fault. Ask what job it does and whether the draft gets that job done another way; test any beat with `hard-to-vary` by removing it and seeing which later scene loses its footing. Names and grouping are the workshop's; the world-building jobs are in section 3.

| Beat | The job it does | If the draft lacks it, ask... |
|---|---|---|
| Home as the flaw | Gives the flaw a place, so the return can be measured against it | What does home look like, and how does it keep her small? |
| The flaw costs her | Sets up the change, and builds care before the breach | Where, before the crossing, does the flaw cost her something she wants? |
| A goal that forces exploring | Hangs the world on a line of desire, so the world cannot stall the plot | Could she reach her goal without exploring? Then the world is scenery. |
| The crossing | Moves the audience from home's few departures to the other world's many, and signals which everyday defaults no longer hold; one moment of enchantment makes the new rules welcome; plants details that, seen again, turn out to have been apt | Is the crossing a bare cut? What strange thing met there foreshadows the main conflict? |
| Rules on arrival | Shows the rules that will matter, through someone local, within a scene or two | When does she (and do we) learn what she may and may not do? |
| Creatures that expose her | Each creature's power or habit lights up one of her strengths or flaws, so the world presses on her (Truby's *character web*) | Which of her traits does each creature reveal? One that reveals nothing is texture; keep it only as that. |
| The frozen ruler | Gives the frozen system a face, and a power that must be drawn out | When does her action force the ruler to use his power? |
| Places that comment | Each subworld has its own value and its own fight, and says something about the real world | Do two subworlds make the same comment? Merge them. |
| Gifts, or nightmares | Shapes the middle *(built)*: in a good world, gifts, then a reason to leave that comes from inside her (Josh in *Big* sees the childhood he is losing); in a bad world, worse and worse, fought with something brought from home | Why would she ever leave a good world? Does the reason come from her, or from a push? |
| How the world works | Reveals the world's hidden workings, or the opponent's hold on them, turning the world into plot | Which secret is about the world, and was it set up as a question the audience wanted answered? |
| The world at its best | One set piece where everything connects and works: proof of the dream, and the genre's promised enchantment | What would this world look like at its best, in motion? Can the same scene move a character? |
| Fight or test | Settles the goal, by battle or by a trial borrowed from Myth; either way it presses the flaw she started with | Does the climax press her original flaw? |
| Home seen new | The same kind of situation as the opening, now met with freedom and play; home has changed because she sees it differently | Put the first and last scenes side by side: does she act differently? |

**The chain that holds them** *(built)*: home shows the flaw, the other world is designed against it (pushing home's bad values to an extreme, or rewarding their opposite), and the return proves the world did its job. Truby's shorthand is A, B, A2, and his *double arena* is the pairing of the two worlds. If the last scene could swap places with the first, the round trip has not paid off.

## 7. The genre's question

Truby's picture of life in Fantasy, its *Mind-Action story view* in his term *(asserted)*: imagination finds the hidden possibility in any world, and shows either a life worth living (or one that only looks it) or a set of values run to ruin. Implied World principle 8 (thematic fit) turns that into a test: the other world's main departure should embody the story's question, as Wonderland's inverted logic is aimed at Alice's love of order.

His *thematic recipe* (a genre's plan for living well) *(asserted)*, in gist: stay playful while grown up. **As a question for a draft:** who could she be with the ordinary rules switched off, and can she bring it home? More sharply: what has she stopped herself from becoming, and what does the other world show her about how to become it? The exploring and the inner lesson should be one act seen twice. If the ending pays off only the outer goal and she is the same person, the draft is a quest with magic in it, not yet Fantasy.

The Gap theory puts theme in the settlement, the story's answer to the question its world poses, so treat the recipe as a claim the ending tests, not a lesson it delivers (`.claude/skills/plot/references/premise-and-theme.md`). In Social Fantasy the question widens to a community: how could it be arranged so its people flourish? Truby's caution, from *It's a Wonderful Life* *(asserted)*: judge a utopia by its conditions, not by a character's mood at the end.

## 8. Where the care comes from

Care is the first term of the suspense equation and must be built before the threat arrives. Fantasy leans on these Bond levers:
- **Flaw and Desire** do most of the work: the flaw shown costing her before the crossing, and a clear goal, often to get home, that gives the audience something to want with her.
- **Vulnerability** is easy to set up with a child hero or a lonely outsider, but the orphan in the cupboard is one of the Bond theory's visible levers: it feels cheap when it serves the tear ducts rather than her story. It builds care only when the story shows what she could lose, and it tips into pity, then fatigue, unless she pushes back.
- **Connection** carries the endings: home and family valued anew (Oz, *A Christmas Carol*), friends who become family (*Harry Potter*), a father reconciled over a game of catch (*Field of Dreams*).
- **Humour**: her own wit and play, or a creature companion's, make good company; constant jokes undercut the stakes. The playful creatures and the world at its best belong to the promise (section 2).
- **Care is social** (Bond principle 5): the creatures reveal her through how they treat her, and *It's a Wonderful Life* reaches the Miss rung directly, by showing the audience the town without George, person by person.

**Run low on purpose: Competence.** Truby's warning about magic *(built)* is the Bond theory's Superman problem in this genre's form: too much competence kills suspense, and magic is competence that can grow without limit. Give power a limit, a cost, or a way to lose it. **The visiting helper** balances levers the Bond way: high Competence and Decency, offset by a need of her own (Clarence must earn his wings), while the people she helps keep the Agency. For the full method: `.claude/skills/character/SKILL.md`.

## 9. Suspense and knowledge

**The equation.** Suspense = Care × Threat × Uncertainty × Time. Fantasy's weak terms are uncertainty, when magic makes the hero too strong or the safe return is taken for granted, and threat, in a good world where little is at risk until the catch appears. Drive can fail too, the genre's *catch-22* in Truby's term *(built)*: the world pulls sideways while the story needs to move forward (he ranks lost drive as the worst mistake, *asserted*). Hang the world on her goal, and show each part of it as it gets in her way; the Implied World theory gives the reason, since every departure draws on the attention the plot needs (principle 2). **Test:** mark each passage of world explanation in the first act; where nobody is pursuing anything, move or cut it (`.claude/skills/story-world/references/world-from-story.md`, section 10). Where the happy return is expected, uncertainty can move from *whether* to *how* and *at what cost*: late in *Big* the question is less whether Josh gets back than what he gives up to do it.

**Rules shown, source hidden.** The workshop carries Anticipation principle 3 (show the rules, hide the thing) over from threats to magic: the rules are mysteries, stated early and kept (section 4); the magic's source is a wonder. The Gap theory's case: Tom Bombadil stays a wonder because the book never makes solving him anyone's goal. Truby's strongest reveals expose the world's hidden workings, or the opponent's hold on them *(fitted)*: the stone and why the villain wants it, then the hidden anchors of the villain's life. They work because the plot pressed those questions first.

**Knowledge position.** The outsider hero keeps the audience level with her through the crossing: we learn the rules as she does, which is the guide at work, and the genre's biggest surprises are often about the world itself. Putting the audience ahead suits a bad world (the workshop's reading of Anticipation principle 6): show what the frozen ruler is preparing, and every scene of hers gains the suspense of helplessness.

**Lost refuge and the ratchet** (the workshop's readings). In a good other world the refuge is the world itself, so the catch that spoils it is the genre's lost refuge. In a bad world, experiences grow steadily worse (escalation, which the ratchet shapes); where the threat is also revealed step by step, that is a climb up the fear ladder, so hold back the full sight and linger below the horror rung. Truby's *meander*, one hero wandering past a string of opponents *(fitted)*, risks tension that resets at every new place; let each subworld bring a new opponent and a harder press on the flaw, so the wandering still rises in steps (Anticipation principle 7). For a sprawling epic, see the tournament in section 11. More: `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md` and `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`.

## 10. Subgenres and mixes

**Subgenres Truby names**, grouped here by where the fantastic sits: in another world reached and left (*Portal Fantasy*, the classic form); in magical districts of a real city (*Urban*); inside everyday life, unremarked (*Magical Realism*); in space (*Space Fantasy*); or in a secondary world with a mood or period of its own (*Sword and Sorcery*, *Dark*, *Romantic*, *Historical*, *Alternate History*). Plus the three social forms of section 11; he also names a *Traveling Devil* without discussing it.

**Portal or Urban** *(built in part)*. Urban Fantasy gives up the round trip, and with it the genre's main carrier of change and return; it gains a charge of possibility for the city where most readers live, and in Implied World terms the real city supplies nearly all the baseline, so each departure stands out. Set in a city, it usually mixes with Crime, Detective or Thriller (*The Dresden Files*, *Lucifer*). **Move:** decide which city genre supplies the spine, and find another carrier for the change, such as a hero changed by each crossing between districts.

**Mixes named:** with Myth (*The Wizard of Oz*, *The Lord of the Rings*); with Horror, where Truby places fairy tales (*Pan's Labyrinth*, *Edward Scissorhands*); with Crime (*The Dark Knight*); with Science Fiction (*The Umbrella Academy*); with Love Story (*The Night Circus*); with Action (*Game of Thrones*); with Coming-of-Age (*Harry Potter*, where the school is the baseline that makes the magic cheap: `memoir-and-coming-of-age.md`); with Comedy (the visiting helper: `comedy.md`); with political drama, which gives the Fantasy Political Epic.

**Neighbours** *(asserted, as contrasts)*, as questions to sort a draft. Against Myth (`myth.md`): a road travelled, or a leap there and back? (*Oz* does both.) Against Horror (`horror.md`): are doors closing on someone shrinking, or opening on someone growing? Against Science Fiction (`science-fiction.md`): is the other world built to test values or to model a system? Truby hands on to `detective-and-thriller.md`: Fantasy widens who a person could become but says little about the mind at its best. On which genre leads a mix: `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`.

## 11. Twisting it

Truby's general routes above a genre are in `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`, section 9; the field of life Fantasy takes apart is, in his reading, the art of living itself *(asserted)*. His Fantasy chapter adds two small moves, setting the fantastic inside a real city (section 10) and giving the self-revelation to the opponent (Mr Banks), and one large one: widen the lens from one person to a community, *Social Fantasy*, in three forms *(asserted, as a taxonomy)*:
- **The visiting helper** (Truby's *Traveling Angel*) *(built)*. **Test:** does she refuse to solve it for them? Do they, not she, change? Does she have a need of her own that the story also settles? A stranger who simply fixes things takes the change away from the people the story is about, so this form keeps her a catalyst (*Mary Poppins*, *The Bishop's Wife*).
- **Paradise on Earth** *(fitted)*: a working model of a happy society, shown in action, not described (*Coco*, *The Night Circus*); or its negative, paradise lost, where people ruin a paradise through a flaw the story has already shown. *Pleasantville*'s move *(built)*: find the hidden rule that makes a perfect-looking world a prison, then the smallest act that could break it, and let its effects spread visibly (colour, in that film): ripple (Implied World principle 3) turned into plot.
- **The Fantasy Political Epic** *(asserted)*, the grimmest of the three, weighs whole social orders against each other. *It's a Wonderful Life*'s move *(built)*: hold the town fixed and change one cause (George was never born). **Test:** write who five minor characters would be under the good order and the bad; if the difference is only scenery (neon against trees), push it into their lives. *Game of Thrones*' move, Truby's *tournament structure* *(built)*: every faction chases one prize by its own way of leading, and the field narrows to finalists who stand for clearly different answers, so the contest itself is the argument, and the winner must earn it (section 4). Shrinking the field works as a clock, and its deaths break the star-survives guarantee, within the owner theory's limit: if everyone dies all the time, care collapses.

**One idea from the owner theories: grow the world by opening gaps, not by filling them.** The expansion paradox says franchises grow by turning reserve and gaps into official canon, so worlds can feel smaller as they get bigger: the midi-chlorians explained a Force that worked because it was unexplained, the Gap theory's deflation. Fans gather at the gaps, not at the details. So when a Fantasy grows (a sequel, a second half, a prequel), let each new region answer only what the story has pressed, and bring gaps of its own, with edges: a sealed door, a people mentioned and never met, a song about a war nobody tells. *The Lord of the Rings* explains the Ring, which the plot chases, and leaves Bombadil and the Entwives alone. **Test by swap:** list each new fact a sequel adds about the world and ask whether a character chased it (a mystery, owed) or whether it explains something that worked unexplained (a wonder). Swap each of the second kind for a new gap with edges; the theory predicts the world feels larger. If the swap costs the plot nothing, the explanation was doing no work. Repairs: `.claude/skills/story-world/references/diagnosing-a-world.md`, section 7.

## 12. Examples

- *Alice in Wonderland* and *Through the Looking-Glass*: one image for a whole world; every creature aimed at the heroine's love of order; Truby's pick as the genre's greatest work.
- *The Wizard of Oz*: a cyclone as the crossing; rules on arrival; a wish to leave that becomes a wish to return; Fantasy wrapped round Myth.
- *Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone*: home as the flaw; subworlds from the pub to the school; reveals about the magic and the villain; a return to a house that is no longer home.
- *Mary Poppins*, *It's a Wonderful Life* and *A Christmas Carol*: the visiting helper; the opponent who learns; one town under two orders, compared person by person; subworlds as visits in time, and home seen new on Christmas morning.
- *Pleasantville*, *Big* and *Field of Dreams*: an apparent utopia made real by one small act that spreads; adults reclaiming play; a reason to go home that comes from inside.
- *The Lord of the Rings* and *Game of Thrones*: reserve and gaps, and hobbits as guides; medieval furniture with a departure from the genre's habits, the apparent lead killed, and a funnel whose ending, Truby argues, broke its promise.
- *The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe*, *Coraline* and *Pan's Labyrinth*: the crossing as an ordinary object; the safe return put in doubt, and then withdrawn.

## 13. Rivals and doubts

- **A world built round the hero, or a world with business of its own.** Truby makes home the flaw given a place, the other world its opposite, and every creature a light on the hero. Implied World principle 4 wants a world that goes on without her. *Test:* add one scene of the other world at its own business (a sport, a market, a quarrel between strangers). Truby's model has no job for it; the theory predicts a more real world and a weightier test for her. They agree when the main departure is aimed at her flaw *and* the world keeps routines that would carry on without her (`.claude/skills/story-world/references/world-from-story.md`, section 9).
- **All allegory, or surplus.** Truby: everything fantastic stands for something real. Implied World principle 5: details with no purpose are what make a world. *Test:* add one detail that stands for nothing, a barometer in Barthes' sense; Truby's view predicts it dilutes the allegory, the theory predicts more reality.
- **Show as much as possible, or show the tip.** Truby wants the hero to see the widest possible share of the other world. Implied World principles 6 and 7: depth comes from what is sensed below, and deliberate gaps build attachment. Truby's own catch-22 sides with the theory. *Test:* cut one subworld to a mention (a song, a far-off view); Truby predicts a smaller world, the theory a larger one.
- **Reveal the workings, or keep the wonder.** Truby's best reveals expose how the world works; the Gap theory's promise and the expansion paradox warn that explaining what worked unexplained deflates it. They agree when the reveal answers a question the plot pressed, as in Truby's own examples. *Test:* add a scene explaining where the magic comes from; the theory predicts deflation.
- **Culture from the hero, or wants from the world.** Truby draws a world's culture from its leading characters' values; Gap principle 2 says the world decides what characters want and what things cost. *Test:* write the culture first from the world's own history, then derive the hero's wants from it. If the stakes sharpen, the Gap direction was doing work the other hid.
- **Doubts.** "Every Fantasy is an allegory" can be read into any story, so it is close to unfalsifiable. "Becoming more, not someone else" is a neat line with a fuzzy border. The three-step shape (home, other world, changed home) is fitted from portal stories and does not fit Urban Fantasy, as Truby admits. His praise of the meander's easy line and of lingering in the crossing sits uneasily with his ranking of lost drive as the worst mistake. That Fantasy flourishes when a society grows uniform, that its magic works through animism, and that a female tyrant marks the loss of caring are asserted. The examples lean on English-language children's classics and Hollywood film.
