# Comedy

*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Genres, chapter 8, in the workshop's own words; framed by the owner theories (Bond theory's Humour lever and where it breaks, above all; its principles 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 and the limits of care; Anticipation theory's suspense equation and principles 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8; Gap theory's knowledge gap and principles 2, 4, 5 and 7; Implied World principles 1, 2, 3 and 8).*

## 1. In one line

Comedy sends a hero who believes her own front after a goal she wants far too much, knocks her off it again and again without real harm, and ends when her last front is stripped and she is joined to other people.

Words kept apart in this file: *Comedy*, with a capital, is the genre, and its subgenres are capitalised too (Farce, Black Comedy). The *drop* is the comic lowering, a character knocked in an instant from the height she claims to what she is (Truby's *comic drop*, after Henri Bergson); the word means nothing else here. Her *front* is the self she shows the world and half believes in; her *low* is the weakness under it. Truby calls the pair the *Comic Gap*, but *gap* keeps its owner-theory meanings in this file. A *nightmare* is a reveal in which the hero's fear comes true. The *union* is the closing wedding, reconciliation or renewed community. Truby's *Traveling Angel* is called *the visiting helper*, as in `fantasy.md`. Truby's own summary *(asserted)*: Comedy looks at society from the side of what goes wrong, how it fails to work and how people botch their lives inside it; it is the genre of everyday ethics.

## 2. The promise

From Truby's account the audience comes for four things *(mostly asserted)*: **the drop, over and over**, someone who claims a height brought down at once to what they really are, with no lasting harm *(built: the height and the ground must be seen together in the same instant, and the drop must be survivable; a pie in the face is funny, a scalding is not)*; **every front exposed**, the pompous and the deluded first; **disasters that gather speed** towards a climax of wreckage; and **a close that joins** people who spent the story at odds. Under the obsession runs a quieter assurance, which Truby hears in the tone itself: however bad it looks, it will come out all right. That is not a promise but the genre's central guarantee, the protection for a hero it cares about that lets the audience relax enough to laugh (section 4). He roots the laugh in a feeling of being above the character *(asserted)*. The Bond theory puts the pull of a whole story in care, not in a verdict; the workshop's reading is that superiority explains one laugh and care explains why a comic story holds for ninety minutes or nine seasons (sections 8 and 13). In Anticipation terms, Comedy keeps the threat term low for the body and high for the pride.

## 3. The baseline

The *baseline* (Implied World theory) is what the audience assumes by default: the real world plus the habits of the genre. Say "a wedding weekend at a country house" and a Comedy audience has cast the snob, the fool and the couple who will end up together before the first scene is over. Unless told otherwise it assumes *(fitted, from Truby's beats and examples)*:
- **a settled society** whose manners, ranks and institutions work well enough to fail in; Truby places Comedy late in his order of genres for this reason *(asserted)*, and with no working order to fall short of, there is no height to fall from;
- **everyone performing**: each person plays a role and hides something, and the rich and those in charge are pompous and due to be brought down;
- **danger that is social**: embarrassment and exclusion, not death; injuries heal between scenes;
- **small wrongs that come back**, and **an ending that reconciles**.

**The world is cheap here, on purpose** (the workshop's reading). Comedy usually spends almost nothing on departures (Implied World principle 1) and lets the audience's own sense of manners carry the world, so the attention budget (principle 2) goes on character and escalation. What it cannot do without is a *normal* exact enough to breach. The world decides what counts as an event (Gap principle 2), and in Comedy the event is an offence against a code: in *Seinfeld* the code is the loose, shifting etiquette of a big modern city, and each tiny breach is an event only because of it. When Comedy does depart, the departure tends to carry the question (Implied World principle 8), like the loop in *Groundhog Day*. Fish out of Water turns this round: the audience's own city is the baseline, seen through a stranger's eyes as if it were the departure (*Crocodile Dundee*).

**Generic Comedy rides on these defaults.** If the snob could be any snob and the code any code, the draft is using the baseline's furniture. The fix is a code particular to this world, with knock-on effects (principle 3; section 11 below), and a front held in place by this hero's desire. Hand the code's building to `story-world`.

## 4. Guarantees, and which to break

A *guarantee* is a protection the audience assumes the genre gives without being told. Anticipation principle 5, the broken contract, says fear needs the audience to believe these protections are gone: break one early and every later threat is real; break them all and care collapses, and suspense with it. Comedy is where breaking the central guarantee costs most, because safety is what lets the audience laugh. Break it and the genre changes, not just the level of suspense *(built, from the limit on the drop)*. Truby does not discuss guarantees directly; the table is the workshop's, drawn from his beats and examples.

| Guarantee the audience leans on | Broken by | What breaking it buys |
|---|---|---|
| It will come out all right (an expected ending kept as a protection for the hero and those around her) | Black Comedy, where the system can wreck everything (*Dr. Strangelove*) | Any drop might be the one she does not get up from; the laugh turns nervous |
| Nobody dies where we can see it | Black Comedy and Crime Comedy (*Dr. Strangelove*, *Goodfellas*) | Later threats are real, and the laugh turns uneasy |
| The opponent can menace, not kill | the same forms; kept in *Ghostbusters*, whose first ghost frightens everyone and harms nobody | A lethal opponent the audience can still laugh at, nervously |

- **Expectations to overturn, not guarantees.** Some things a Comedy audience expects protect nobody, and by the cheated-or-unsafe test (`.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`, section 2) overturning them leaves it neither cheated nor unsafe: a twist on how the promise is kept. Most are settlement choices (section 7), and overturned late they give the ending weight rather than making later threats real. *The hero learns*: withheld in Black Comedy always and by the visiting helper usually, so the lesson passes to the audience. *The union is real*: Chekhov (*Uncle Vanya*, *The Cherry Orchard*) leaves people together from habit, unchanged, for bittersweet weight. *The trouble ends*: in Black Comedy the survivors start again, and the system outlasts the story (residue, section 9). *The goal is worth having* is one Comedy reverses as a matter of course: the audience expects the prize to prove hollow (Truby: in Comedy the goal proves worthless, where Action and Myth heroes win something great), so a comic prize that turns out worth having is the surprise (the workshop's reading). Unlikable leads (*Seinfeld*) are a choice of levers, not a break (section 8).
- **Keep the safety the story set up: once stated, it is a promise.** Establish early the worst an opponent can do *(Truby, built)*. A stated limit is a rule the audience predicts with; switching to real harm midway with no warning reads as betrayal (Gap principle 5), not as bite.
- **Which one to break.** Comedy's guarantees are all forms of safety, so breaking one changes the genre: break safety only if you mean to write Black Comedy, and then one, convincingly, preferably the one the story's question is about (the workshop's suggestion, after Implied World principle 8). For bite without leaving the genre, overturn an expectation instead. Test by restoring it: make the hollow union real and ask whether the ending loses weight. If it does not, the turn was doing no work.

## 5. Hero and opponent

**Hero: a bungler who believes she is cool** *(built: without a front there is nothing to fall from, and a character who already knows her weakness cannot be caught out by it)*. On Northrop Frye's scale of heroes by power, which Truby borrows, she is an ordinary person or an antihero; in Comic Myth, a bungler, a holy innocent or a child *(asserted)*. Truby's first step, for her and for every other character *(built)*: one line, "believes she is X; is actually Y". If she already knows Y, give her a front she can be proven wrong about.

**Types.** Truby offers nine stock comic types ranked by social standing, each with a front, a low, an inner need and a moral need *(fitted from many examples; the ranking is loose and one person can be several)*. Grouped here by what the front rests on:
- *self-image*: the nerd or klutz who thinks she is hip (Lucy in *I Love Lucy*); the know-it-all whose jargon passes for expertise (*Frasier*); the show-business type whose gushing covers no feeling or no talent (*This Is Spinal Tap*);
- *rank or money*: the princess who thinks herself above dirt and drudgery (*Clueless*, *Legally Blonde*); the aristocrat, a snob by wealth alone (Judge Smails in *Caddyshack*);
- *effort*: the everyman, hopeful, hard-working and never respected (*Big*; Michael in *The Office*);
- *a con*: the trickster, who passes himself off as grander, wiser and kinder than he is (Venkman in *Ghostbusters*; Phil in *Groundhog Day*);
- *the setting*: the slob, whose front is nothing but the smart room he has been let into, and whose crudeness shows up everyone else's false superiority (*Animal House*); and the visiting helper, where the front belongs to the stiff world around her and she is the one who knows what matters (*Mary Poppins*, *Amélie*).

The needs, in gist: an inner one (confidence, humility, to become genuine, to see others as equals) and a moral one (stop using people, stop lying, stop making others live your way). Write both before plotting and the arc and the laughs are half built.

**Casting by drop.** Bergson's three directions of lowering, as Truby reads them *(asserted on Bergson's authority)*: to an **animal** (the body: appetite, toilet, sex), to a **child** (far more feeling than the moment calls for: tantrum, panic, tears) or to a **machine** (far less: deadpan, dry wit). The child-machine axis is Truby's own and the most usable part: set an over-reactor against an under-reactor and each makes the other funnier (in *Seinfeld*, Jerry is usually the machine against three children).

**Desire.** The goal is usually success or romance, which is broad; what sets the theme is *how* she pursues it: by lying, putting on a front, using people *(asserted)*. The job, in one line (after Truby): a want far bigger than her ability, aimed at the thing she is worst at, so that her flaw produces every nightmare *(built)*. One goal, set early, is enough, as long as each trouble it leads her into is of her own making (Truby's *Dangerous Desire*) *(built)*.

**Opponent.** Usually someone who speaks for authority or for society *(fitted)*. A drop needs a height, so he needs a front and a low of his own; with nothing to pretend to, he is only strong, and strength alone is not funny *(built)*. State his limit early, how much trouble he can make and that it stops short of serious harm, and it becomes a rule the audience predicts with (Anticipation principle 3). Never just one: Truby's *four-point opposition*, called **the web** here, turns the duel into a small society, the hero against several opponents at once, or in a series four leads against each other *(built in part: more opposed pairs give more material; that it matters most is asserted)*. Each pair needs a difference that will last the whole story, and the drop gives the handiest one: set an over-reactor against an under-reactor, as above *(fitted, from sitcoms)*. Give each side a whole way of life (a retired headmistress who prizes duty, thrift and silence against a young vlogger who prizes attention, speed and noise), so the web argues about how to live *(asserted as the source of a Comedy's thematic force)*. **Test:** draw the square. Every pair, not only hero and chief opponent, needs a conflict of its own; a pair divided by nothing will run dry. For building either figure: `.claude/skills/character/references/opponents.md`; for the web as a cast: `.claude/skills/character/references/building-a-cast.md`.

## 6. The beats, as jobs

Truby calls Comedy the one genre that turns every standard beat upside down: goals prove worthless, insight can be refused, and instead of Drama's one slow fall and rise the hero is dropped instantly, many times *(the contrast of structure is built; "every beat" is overstated, since his own chapter keeps a firm line of desire and gives most comic heroes an insight: read it as "reshapes", not "removes")*. So Comedy runs on a rhythm of set-up and drop, and a long stretch with none is playing as Drama. The beats are fitted, drawn from past stories: a missing beat is a question, not a fault. Ask what job it does, and whether the draft gets that job done some other way. Names and grouping are the workshop's.

| Beat | The job it does | If the draft lacks it, ask... |
|---|---|---|
| The front | Gives each character a height to fall from and a low she cannot see | What does each believe she is, and what is she? Does anyone already see their own low? |
| The trigger | An outside event that sets one goal | What makes her want this, now? |
| The line | A run of story scenes for the gags to hang on (Truby's *clothesline*), started straight after the trigger | Do the first pages string gags together with no line to follow? |
| Scene, gag, scene | A story scene moves events on; a gag stops them to watch a drop; alternating keeps drive between laughs (Truby's *leapfrog*) | Label each scene S or G. Where do two Gs run together? |
| Trouble she made | The goal drags her deeper, each trouble her own doing | Name three troubles, each worse. Whose fault is each? |
| The web | Four or more people in lasting opposition, so conflict and laughs multiply | Does every pair have a fight of its own? |
| The scam | Her plan as a long deception about what she really wants, over many scenes and close to the end | Does the opponent misread her goal? Is the scam still live at the climax? |
| The disguise | The front made physical *(Truby's four rules, built: each has a job)*: the audience is in on it, he cannot take it off, it lowers him, and it is the costume he would hate most | What would he lose by taking it off? |
| Small sins | Lies, cheats and thefts that worsen her problem and spread through the group | For each, who else does it hit, and how does it come back? |
| Danger she survives | Keeps the middle taut: risk to body or feelings, most often public shame; never a death seen | What is at stake here, and who will watch her fail? |
| Quickening nightmares | Reveals in which her fear comes true, each worse, at shorter intervals (Truby's *Comic Cyclone*) | Is each nightmare hers, worse than the last, and who caused it? |
| The worst nightmare | The climax: her own goal leads her into what she dreads most, often in chaos, every front stripped, or a public unmasking | Did her goal lead her there, or did chance? Is every main front stripped? |
| Seeing herself | An insight and a moral choice. Truby calls staging it as public humiliation at a ceremony a tired cliché *(his taste, as he says)* | Is it played before a crowd? Try it in private. |
| The union | Marriage, reconciliation or renewed community; or a hollow one, for a sad ending | What brought them together: change, or habit? |

**The chain that holds them:** a front, a goal wanted too much, the task she does worst, nightmares grown from her low, the worst one, where the front is stripped and the union becomes possible *(built)*. Truby's working order *(asserted)*: settle the worst nightmare first and work back, building smaller ones that climb towards it. A missing link makes the laughs arbitrary: gags any hero could have had.

## 7. The genre's question

Truby's picture of life in Comedy, its *Mind-Action story view* *(asserted)*, in gist: the world does not work, and people hide behind fronts while they chase prizes worth less than they think. Strip the fronts and the group comes apart and is rebuilt better, which is why he calls the deep theme tolerance *(asserted)*. His key question, in short: **will she keep up her front, or let people see who she is?**

His *thematic recipe*, *the Way of Being Yourself* *(asserted, but it follows from the front mechanism)*: the front is the problem, not the path. She wins only when every front is stripped and she stands exposed; seeing she is as human as they are, opponents turn into allies, and she sees the prize was not worth what she did for it. **As a question for a draft:** which front does she hold hardest, and what does giving it up cost her and win her? The Gap theory puts meaning in the settlement (principle 7), and Comedy has three to choose from: real change, a hollow union, or a cycle that starts again. Truby's philosophy says people never really learn, while his beats give most heroes an insight; the book leaves the choice open, so make it on purpose. *Groundhog Day* gives a sharper version *(Truby's reading of one film)*: the trap opens only when the hero stops using people and does good for its own sake, and the rewards come as a by-product. By Implied World principle 8 the loop, the film's one departure, is the question: how to live a single day well.

## 8. Where the care comes from

Care is the first term of the suspense equation, and a comic story needs it as much as any other; it spends it differently.

**The Humour lever, and where it breaks.** The Bond theory's Humour lever is a character's own wit and charm (we like good company, and charm offsets cruelty), and it breaks where constant quips undercut the stakes, because nobody can fear for someone who takes nothing seriously. Comedy is the funniest genre, but that is not the same as turning this lever up: much of the laughter is *at* the hero, from her drop, not from her wit (the workshop's reading). So the lever is a choice for each comic hero. Tricksters and wits run it high (Venkman in *Ghostbusters*; the Bond theory's own Elizabeth Bennet); bunglers run it low and are carried by Desire and Flaw. The break bites when the hero is also the wit, and Comedy still needs its stakes (Truby insists on real danger through the middle; section 6). Truby's hero holds it off (the workshop's reading): she is *not* someone who takes nothing seriously; she takes one wrong thing far too seriously. Her obsession is what makes her funny to watch and also what keeps the stakes alive; she is the joke, not the person joking about everything. Two tests. Cut the hero's wisecracks from a key sequence and keep her desire: if worry rises, the quips were denying the stakes. Then ask who gets the wit: a hero with a quip ready for every drop of her own is standing outside her story; give the wit to someone watching her, and leave her the desire. A joke that is how a character admits pain, not how she denies it, keeps the stakes (`.claude/skills/dialogue/references/voice.md`, sections 9 and 11).

**Levers turned high.** **Desire** carries most: an intense, specific, self-damaging desire gives the audience a goal to share, and in Comedy it shares her wanting even while it expects the prize to prove hollow; the Bond warning that a want with no reason feels mechanical still holds, so give the excess a reason. Desire also explains Truby's leapfrog (section 6): a run of gags with no story scene stops the line of desire, leaving nothing to want on her behalf (Bond principle 3), and the stakes stop with it. That is a stalled Desire, a separate cause from the Humour lever's break. **Flaw** is built in (front and low), and real, because it costs her. **Agency** comes with the schemer and the trickster, who act rather than react. **Specificity** comes from the front and the kind of drop, which pile up into quirks unless they drive the plot. **Connection** pays off in the union, and runs all through a Buddy Story.

**Run low on purpose: Competence.** Desire at ten, ability at one. Bond principle 4: when you turn one lever down, turn another up; in Comedy that is Desire. **Decency** can run low too. *Seinfeld*'s four behave badly, and viewers stayed because the flaws were the show and an absurd system kept cheating them *(Truby, fitted from one show)*: alignment beats virtue (principle 2), indifference and not dislike is the enemy (principle 6), and the system's unfairness turns up **Vulnerability** for characters low on Decency. Elsewhere Vulnerability comes from social danger, the risk of shame and of being shut out.

**Care is social** (principle 5): the turn from opponents to allies when her front falls vouches for her. The visiting helper runs on Competence, Decency and Humour with Flaw near zero, so care, and the change, pass to the locals she helps (`fantasy.md`). Four equal leads keep the inner circle small (too many characters dilute care), and years of episodes build the Miss rung: audiences grieve for sitcom families. A revelation staged as public humiliation is a visible lever, and visible levers backfire: shame put on to wring feeling reads as manipulation (the workshop's reading of Truby's cliché).

## 9. Suspense and knowledge

**The equation.** Suspense = Care × Threat × Uncertainty × Time. Comedy caps Threat for the body and keeps it high for the pride; the limit on the drop (a bruise, not a break) is where the threat term stops. Truby's comparison with Horror shows the lever *(built at its core: whether the lowering can be undone)*: both genres lower people to beast or machine, but Horror takes the lowering all the way to death and Comedy only as far as she can get up again. The same lowering frightens when threat and care are high and the audience feels helpless, and gets a laugh when threat is capped and the audience feels on top; the clown sits on the line (Pennywise in *It*). **Uncertainty** sits in its sweet spot, bad odds (desire ten, ability one), while the genre's safety keeps the final outcome sure, so it moves from *whether* to *how* (Anticipation, Part 2 section 1). *Little Miss Sunshine* says at the start where the trip ends, a children's pageant, and the known end point frees attention for the jokes *(built)*: an attention-budget saving too (Implied World principle 2).

**Knowledge position.** Comedy's home is audience ahead. The front is itself a knowledge gap: the audience sees the low while she sees only the front. The scam and the disguise let the audience in on the deception, and mistaken identity runs on characters knowing different things. Audience ahead gives the suspense of helplessness; in Comedy its object is exposure, and the laugh comes when the front cracks (*Tootsie*). Level with her is rarer and suits the nightmare: the audience learns the worst at the moment she does.

**Surprise and scheduling.** Each nightmare is a surprise the plot times (Gap principle 4). *Seinfeld* ties its separate threads with a payoff that makes the audience re-read earlier scenes: in "The Marine Biologist", a golf ball Kramer drives into the sea turns up in George's story at the climax and pays back his lie *(built: linking threads gives each thin one weight, and the payback turns a wrong into a punchline)*. Something that looked like surplus becomes plot: Chekhov's gun dressed as Flaubert's barometer (Implied World, Part 2 section 3).

**The ratchet.** Set-up and drop is a fast rise and release, released through laughter rather than relief. Truby's two rhythms, strict scene-gag alternation and nightmares at shrinking intervals, are exactly the patterns an audience learns, and Anticipation principle 7 says the pattern must break before it is learned. Truby's answer is new shapes for the same fear; add breaks in timing too: two story scenes where a gag is due, a set-up with no drop, then the drop from where nobody was looking.

**Lost refuge.** The comic nightmare takes away what the hero relies on, with the safety still on. In the workshop's reading, Comedy's refuge is belonging, and shame is what threatens it; the harshest penalty the genre hands out is public disgrace and exclusion from the group *(Truby)*.

**Residue.** Black Comedy leaves the system standing and the cycle restarting, so something unresolved outlasts the story (Anticipation principle 8). More: `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md`; the set-up and payoff of a single joke: `.claude/skills/dialogue/references/line-and-scene-design.md`, section 5.

## 10. Subgenres and mixes

**Subgenres Truby names**, each with its own beats, one reason he gives for Comedy being hard to write *(asserted)*. **Romantic Comedy** is a Love Story played for laughs, crossing into seriousness and back (`love-story.md`). **Shakespearean Comedy** runs on tools this file uses elsewhere, above all disguise and mistaken identity *(fitted from the plays)*. **Farce** is the chase multiplied: a crowd after one prize at the same moment, and the frantic speed of the chase is what shows the prize worthless *(built)*; Screwball sits beside it (*Bringing Up Baby*, *Fawlty Towers*). **Parody** mocks other genres, and in Bond terms it gives the audience nobody to care about, so no care builds to carry it much past a sketch *(built; section 13)*; *Young Frankenstein* is his exception. **Black Comedy** and **Satire** are in section 11.

**Journeys and visitors.** **Comic Myth** (Epic Comedy) is an antihero's journey, descended from *Don Quixote*: the Myth half puffs the characters up and the Comedy half brings them down *(built)*. It sprawls into episodes, and *Little Miss Sunshine* mends that three ways *(built: each answers a named problem)*: the family on board as steady opposition, so conflict builds instead of resetting with each stranger met; a destination announced at the start; and a false goal the story will show to be hollow (`myth.md`). In **Fish out of Water** the hero jumps between two sharply different worlds, and his home skills are absurd and effective in the new one *(fitted)*. **The visiting helper** (Truby's *Traveling Angel*) is Comedy with Fantasy: an outsider with no flaw to mend brings the change, and the locals undergo it (`fantasy.md`). Truby calls it perhaps the most popular comic form *(asserted, no data)*. The **Buddy Story** is Action, Comedy and Love at once; treat the friendship as a Love Story (`action.md`).

**Mixes named:** with Love, Myth, Action and Love (the Buddy Story), Fantasy (the visiting helper), Detective (*The Hangover*, *Zootopia*), Crime and Gangster (*Get Shorty*, *Goodfellas*, *Parasite*) and Coming-of-Age (*Brittany Runs a Marathon*); and two comic subgenres together. *Wedding Crashers* sets up a Buddy Story, branches into two Romantic Comedy strands and rejoins them at the end, which Truby credits with doubling the beats *(from one film; the doubling asserted)*; it also takes a ritual of renewal, the wedding, and lets its heroes abuse it. **Shapes** *(fitted)*: plain Comedy runs in a line; Comic Myth meanders; Black Comedy branches across many insiders to show a whole system; *Annie Hall* is told as a storyteller's memory.

**Neighbours by contrast.** Horror: the same lowering, with no way back; if a comic sequence frightens, check whether she can get up (`horror.md`). Action is the opposite pole: its hero wins alone, Comedy's is inept and draws everyone together; if the ending is a solo triumph, Action has taken the lead (`action.md`). Crime deals with wrongdoing on a grand scale; if the wrongs grow to that size the Comedy slides, since Comedy works on the small wrongs of daily life (`crime.md`). Drama: one slow fall and rise, not many instant drops. On choosing which genre leads: `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`.

## 11. Twisting it

Truby's general routes above a genre are in `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`. For Comedy the field of life it takes apart is manners and morals, how people treat each other day to day *(asserted)*. His two higher forms widen the target from one person's front to the system that rewards fronts, and show that system to be absurd as a whole.

**Black Comedy** is about a system wrecking itself while the people inside cannot see how absurd its logic is *(fitted)*; the title often names the system (*Network*, *Catch-22*). Truby gives it a beat list of its own; the workshop keeps three jobs. *The logic as the joke*: the system's rules laid out early and plainly enough for the audience to see what its members cannot (below). *A rigged race*: several insiders after one prize, so that the system, not the hero alone, drives the escalation. *No exit*: the ending changes nobody, and something unresolved outlasts the story (Anticipation principle 8; residue, section 9).

**Satire** turns on beliefs: it mocks a society's picture of the good life and seldom offers another *(fitted)*. Truby gives it a longer beat list; the workshop keeps two jobs. *State the creed* early, so the audience can watch it fail. *Let belief misfire*: people act in good faith on what they believe and the acts go wrong, which is where Satire's laughs come from; how much of worth survives the wreck is its settlement (Gap principle 7).

**The system explained, as a joke.** Both forms have someone spell out the system, which can look like what the Implied World theory warns against: the reserve dumped on the surface, or characters telling each other what they both already know. The theory approves the other case, a newcomer who needs telling (worlds with many departures need a guide), and the joke gives a second safe route: a true believer laying out absurd logic in earnest, so the surface shows a person, not an encyclopedia. The rule that gives *Catch-22* its title, explained as if it were common sense to a hero who does not yet know it, does both.

**Other moves from the chapter.** Make the story a thought experiment: *Groundhog Day*'s loop is a journey in time instead of space, each pass a stage the hero goes through, with his inner change the only exit; each pass polishes the scam and his flaw wrecks it a new way. Tie several threads with a karmic payback (*Seinfeld*). Give an unlikable ensemble equal weight. Mix two comic subgenres. End on a hollow union for bittersweet weight (Chekhov). In Parody, keep real care alive (*Young Frankenstein*).

**One idea from the owner theories.** Make the comic breach a breach of manners, and let the world set its price (Gap principle 2). Build a code of conduct one notch stricter or stranger than the audience's own, a departure in etiquette, and follow its ripple through the web (Implied World principle 3), so a small wrong spreads because the code makes it spread. *Pride and Prejudice*, the Gap theory's own case, runs on one rule of its world, the estate that can pass only to a male heir, and the comic fronts in it, Mr Collins's first, are priced by that rule. Test with a swap: replace the code with the audience's own manners. If the same scenes get the same laughs, the code was costume.

## 12. Examples

- *Groundhog Day*: a trickster hero; the scam polished on every pass; a loop as a journey in time, left only by inner change.
- *Tootsie*: a disguise he would hate and cannot remove; unmasking on live television as the worst nightmare.
- *Little Miss Sunshine*: false goal, destination announced, family on board; a dead van the whole family must push.
- *Wedding Crashers*: a Buddy Story branching into two Romantic Comedy strands; a scam that abuses a ritual of union.
- *Seinfeld*: four equal, unlikable leads; threads cut together and repaid by karma.
- *Ghostbusters*: types spread across the web; an opponent that frightens and harms nobody.
- *Legally Blonde*, *Clueless*: princess heroines; social danger in place of physical.
- *Mary Poppins*, *Amélie*: the visiting helper.
- *Don Quixote*: the mock knight on a false quest, the hinge into Comic Myth.
- *Dr. Strangelove*, *Catch-22*: Black Comedy; a system whose logic its members cannot see.
- *Young Frankenstein*: Parody that keeps real feeling.

## 13. Rivals and doubts

- **Above them, or with them.** Truby: the laugh comes from superiority, and Comedy seats the audience above the characters, where Horror puts it beneath the threat. The Bond theory: the aim is care, not a verdict; the workshop's reading adds that looking down on a character may block the Side-with rung. Truby's own cases lean the theory's way: *Seinfeld*'s leads are liked for their flaws and for being cheated, the family on board makes *Little Miss Sunshine* funnier, and Parody fails at length because it blocks care. *Test:* in a feature-length Comedy, cut the scenes that build care for the lead and keep every gag. Superiority predicts the laughs hold throughout; the Bond theory predicts single laughs survive but the story sags by the middle, as Parody does at length.
- **A rhythm to keep, or one to break.** Truby: alternate story scene and gag, and bring the nightmares faster towards the end. Anticipation principle 7: a pattern must break before the audience learns it. *Test:* in a draft that runs strictly S, G, S, G, break it once in the middle (two story scenes, then a gag where none was due). Truby's rule predicts lost drive; the theory predicts the next drop lands harder.
- **The explained system.** Truby: Black Comedy and Satire need someone to lay the system out. The Implied World theory warns against the reserve dumped on the surface and against characters telling each other what they both already know, and approves a newcomer being told. So the rival is narrower than it looks: Truby says lay it out; the theory says lay it out only to a listener who needs it, or as a joke (section 11). *Test:* give the same logic three ways: a briefing between two officers who both know it, the same logic told to a newcomer, and a believer's earnest speech. Truby's rule, taken flat, predicts all three work; the theory predicts the first reads as an info-dump and the other two inform.
- **Break the contract, or keep the genre: a limit, not a rival.** Anticipation principle 5: break one guarantee early and every threat is real. Truby: show a death and the comic mood breaks, except in Black and Crime Comedy, where the laugh turns nervous. Both can be true in the same draft, so no test separates them; Truby adds a cost the theory does not state. In Comedy the price of the break is the genre (section 4).
- **Doubts.** "Every beat turned upside down" is overstated (section 6). Bergson's three directions of lowering can swallow almost any gag, a catch-all; only the child-machine axis, too much against too little feeling, makes a claim that can be checked. "Nightmare" is broad enough to fit any setback. The nine types overlap and their ranking by standing is loose. The popularity of the visiting helper, and the need for four equal leads (fitted from one show), are asserted, and the beats are fitted mostly from American film and sitcoms. The tone's guarantee of a happy end is Truby's reading; Chekhov's hollow unions and Black Comedy's restarting cycle are counter-cases he names himself.
