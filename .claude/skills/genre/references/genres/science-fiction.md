# Science Fiction

*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Genres, chapter 6, in the workshop's own words; framed by the owner theories (Implied World principles 1 to 8, the traffic jam, the guide for worlds with many departures and the expansion paradox; Gap principles 2, 3, 5, 6 and 7; Anticipation principles 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8; Bond levers, chiefly Desire, Flaw, Vulnerability, Agency, Connection, Interiority and Specificity, the ladder of care, and principles 5 and 6).*

Contents: 1 In one line. 2 The promise. 3 The baseline. 4 Guarantees, and which to break. 5 Hero and opponent. 6 The beats, as jobs. 7 The genre's question. 8 Where the care comes from. 9 Suspense and knowledge. 10 Subgenres and mixes. 11 Twisting it. 12 Examples. 13 Rivals and doubts.

## 1. In one line

Science Fiction magnifies one or two of today's choices into a whole society, usually hinged on a technology, and asks how a person can stay free, and stay human, inside the systems people have built.

Words kept apart in this file: *Science Fiction*, with capitals, is the genre. *Departure* keeps its Implied World meaning (a way the world differs from the baseline) and *breach* its Gap meaning (what breaks the world's own normal). A *reveal* is the moment a withheld fact reaches the audience, a character, or both (the plot skill's definition); the decisive choice Truby files under his reveal beat is called *the fork* here. *Mystery* and *wonder* keep their Gap meanings (an open question the story promises to answer, or to leave open). The *system* is who holds power and the rules for getting on; the *culture* is the story people tell themselves to accept it (Truby splits what he calls society-culture into these two). The *hinge technology* is the one or two tools the whole society leans on (Truby's *fulcrum technology*). A *subworld* (Truby's term) is one of the distinct regions a plot travels through, and the *vortex point* (his term too) is the single place and moment where every storyline meets at the end. Truby's framing *(asserted)*: the genre's real subject is designing a society worth living in rather than guessing at one, and it is the first of his genres to put society ahead of the individual.

## 2. The promise

The audience comes for *(fitted, from Truby's profile of the genre)*:
- **A world built in depth**: new physical rules, a whole society and a technology that matters, at a zoom Myth never uses (galaxies, or particles).
- **Today, pushed further.** A future grown from a habit the audience already has, so they cannot rule it out and the unease follows them home (Anticipation principle 8, residue). Most of the canon uses this as a warning *(fitted, from the dystopian bulk of the canon)*.
- **Big ideas made felt** through one hero's journey, and **a convergence**: every line meeting at one place, at speed, where a single choice decides what a society or a species becomes.
- At its best, **a vision** of how people could live together better.

In Gap terms the genre leads with the departure gap (between reality and the world), which produces wonder and strangeness. Its large forces (scarcity, crowding, machines grown too strong) are usually part of that departure: the world's normal, shown through what it does to people under strain (Gap principle 3, pressure). Truby asserts that good Science Fiction always shows them; the owner theory supplies the reason. The breach (Gap principle 2) is something else: the event that breaks that normal for the people inside. In *Children of Men* the missing births are the normal and a pregnant girl is the breach; in *The Hunger Games* the reaping is normal and Katniss taking her sister's place breaks it. A force that arrives during the story (a new regime, tools suddenly obsolete) can itself be the breach. **Test:** name the normal and the breach in one line each. If only the normal comes, the draft is what the Gap theory calls a travel guide. The question the genre presses hardest is what the system really is and what it costs. Characters chase it, so by Gap principle 5 it is a mystery the story owes an answer to.

## 3. The baseline

The *baseline* (Implied World theory) is the real world plus the habits of the genre. Before being told, a Science Fiction audience assumes *(fitted, from Truby's beats and examples)*:
- **technology, not magic or gods, explains the extraordinary**; the world obeys physical laws, perhaps altered but consistent, with a rule-bound way to cross great distances;
- **society is a system** with a ladder of power and a culture that justifies it, and it probably binds its people; large forces press on everyone;
- **robots or aliens may exist**, and may prove more human than the humans; the future is likely a warning;
- **stock furniture**: the starship, the space station, the wasteland, the megacity, the surveillance screen.

**Case.** The Implied World theory's own example: say "space station" and the audience furnishes it from a hundred earlier ones, so the attention budget (principle 2) can go on a few departures that matter. Truby's *recognisable world* technique *(built)* agrees: a future too strange to recognise leaves the audience watching from outside instead of feeling from within, so anchor it in today's objects and habits before adding what is new. That is minimal departure (principle 1) put as a warning. His other advice, to invent everything, pulls the other way (section 13).

**Point: the traffic jam, not the car.** Frederik Pohl's line (predict the traffic jam, not the car) is the Implied World theory's case for ripple (principle 3: a departure convinces in proportion to its knock-on effects), and it is this genre's working method: few departures, each traced into how people live *(built)*. *Dune* (the owner theory's case) has one shortage, of water, followed into clothing, burial and manners. The main departure should also be the question (principle 8, thematic fit), which Truby makes the genre's defining trait *(asserted)*: name the present-day habit your future magnifies; if you cannot, the future is decoration. Worlds with many departures need a guide, an outsider who learns alongside us (Neo) or an outside aid (the *Star Wars* crawl, *Dune*'s glossary). Abstraction is dear on film (the medium corollary), so keep it short and tie it to a person, as the opening narration of *Mad Max 2* ties the fall of the cities to its hero's ruin *(fitted, one example)*. Truby's patterns at several scales and his social stage are beats in section 6.

**Test** (`hard-to-vary`): swap the hinge technology for a near neighbour (a different surveillance device, a different drive). If the hero's flaw, the plot's key act and the theme all survive unchanged, the departure is costume. For the repair, hand to `.claude/skills/story-world/SKILL.md`, `.claude/skills/story-world/references/diagnosing-a-world.md` and `.claude/skills/story-world/references/world-materials.md`.

## 4. Guarantees, and which to break

A *guarantee* is a protection the audience trusts the genre to give without being told (Anticipation principle 5): broken early and convincingly, it makes every later threat real. Truby does not list them; the table is the workshop's.

| Guarantee the audience leans on | An early break | What breaking it buys |
|---|---|---|
| The system can be beaten | Let the system absorb a sympathetic rebel or ally in the first act, in plain sight (a planned move). *Nineteen Eighty-Four* breaks it only at its end, when Winston is remade: that works on the genre as a whole, so later readers stop trusting the guarantee, not on the book's own earlier scenes | Every act of resistance after the break is real suspense, because it may fail |
| The child is safe | Rue dies in the arena, well before the endgame (*The Hunger Games*) | Every alliance becomes a future loss |
| The ally is loyal | Cypher sells out the crew halfway through (*The Matrix*) | The threat is inside the group, so trust itself costs something |

**Expectations to overturn.** Four more patterns from the chapter's examples are expectations about how the story goes, not protections the audience relaxes on, and they are mostly overturned late. Principle 5 does not cover them; the workshop extends it to them, and what they buy is surprise or residue: *civilisation lasts* (the statue on the beach in *Planet of the Apes*, so the collapse follows the audience home); *humans are the measure of "human"* (the replicant who spares his hunter in *Blade Runner*, so the genre's question turns on the audience); *the hero is on the side of life* (a company killer turned rebel in *Total Recall*, so the growth has far to go); *the climax is a battle* (a war prevented in *Arrival*, where another kind of act decides, and it must still be the hero's).

**Which to break.** One protection, early and convincingly, not all: if everyone is crushed, care collapses, and suspense with it. The strongest for this genre is the first row (the workshop's reading): once the audience has seen a resister absorbed, every act of defiance counts. **Test by removal:** restore the broken guarantee in the outline and ask whether the later scenes lose their danger. If not, the break was decoration. **One guarantee to keep: the world's rules hold.** Truby's rule *(asserted, with a clear mechanism in the owner theories)*: you may overturn real physics, but the replacement law must hold throughout, and breaking a law must cost dearly. A rule that bends when the plot needs it is not a twist but a broken promise (Gap principle 5), and a rule with unplanned consequences can break the world (the Implied World theory's Time-Turner case).

## 5. Hero and opponent

**Hero.** A society is too large to show whole, so let one person stand for it: take the kind of person this stage of society tends to produce and make that kind a particular individual *(built; Truby's shortcut)*. Name a type only where it helps a choice: the *rebel* who refuses and is broken or thrown out, or the *crushed dreamer* who stays and retreats inward (section 8); the *Chosen One* picked by fate, or the rarer self-made hero whose struggle produces the higher self (Truby borrows Nietzsche's *Overman*; section 9).

**Flaw, need and desire.** The risk Truby names *(built)*: a world this big leaves an empty centre, a hero who only sightsees or only suffers. His cure is the *unevolved hero*: a serious moral flaw, often a way of being less than fully human, that mirrors a lack in the society. Deckard, who hunts androids with less mercy than they show, is the model. **Test:** name the hero's lack and point to a scene where the world shows the same lack; if you cannot, hero and world are telling different stories. The need follows from the flaw: to become fully human, which here means seeing the structure and choosing to change it. The desire *(built)* is a concrete aim that forces the hero to deal with the hinge technology: escape in a dystopia (*Logan's Run*), a quest or a rescue in an open universe (*Interstellar*). **Test:** write the goal in one sentence with a named object or place in it, and check it stays in view in every subworld.

**Opponent** *(built)*. The opponent embodies the bad system, often working through machines; in a surveillance state the system itself is the opponent. The job to get done is keeping a face of the threat in every act, since a top power that never meets the hero starves the story of conflict. Truby's answer, borrowed from Action, is his *four-point opposition*, here a linked chain of opponents from the supreme power down to someone close to the hero: the Emperor and Vader; the machines and Agent Smith. **Test:** who from the chain confronts the hero in each act? And let the opponent want a particular future too, spelled out, so the final clash is between two plans for the world. For building either figure: `.claude/skills/character/references/opponents.md` and `.claude/skills/character/references/building-a-cast.md`.

## 6. The beats, as jobs

These beats are *fitted* (drawn from past stories), and more loosely than in other genres: Truby admits this form ranges too widely to pin to a tight sequence, and offers recurring techniques instead. A missing beat is a question, not a fault: ask what job it does and whether the draft gets that job done another way. Names and grouping are the workshop's. Test any beat with `hard-to-vary`: remove it and see which later scene loses its footing.

| Beat | The job it does | If the draft lacks it, ask... |
|---|---|---|
| Three short lists | Land (physics), people (system and culture) and tools (the hinge technology), each enlarged; the genre's meaning lives in the world, so it comes first | Does every item change how someone lives? Which could go with no loss? |
| One place or many | The *arena*, the bounded space the story happens in: one tight place (*Alien*, *Blade Runner*) or a run of subworlds (*Star Trek*, *Star Wars*); a pressure cooker or a tour *(fitted)* | Which is it? Mixed without choosing, the story sprawls. |
| Rules with a price | Physics and travel rules the audience can predict with, and a severe cost for breaking one (in *The Stars My Destination*, teleport somewhere you cannot picture and you may end up inside rock) | What happens to someone who breaks each rule? Does any rule quietly solve a problem the characters struggle with? |
| Power at every scale | The system, and the culture that makes people accept their place, repeated in a household, a workplace, a city (Truby's *social fractals*). For any group he borrows four roles from William Irwin Thompson: leader, provider and defender, truth-teller, value-setter, each at odds with the others (that every group has exactly these four is *asserted*). Show one small scale and the audience infers the rest (Implied World principle 6) *(built)* | Who holds power, and how is success won? Does the same pattern show in a family and a workplace? Does it change between scales for no reason? |
| A stage, not a date | Truby's *social stage*: the world defined by how far its society has developed, not by its year; a named stage is cheap, since the audience furnishes a frontier or a surveillance state from memory *(fitted and borrowed)* | What is this society's main work, and what keeps people in line (in *Nineteen Eighty-Four*, managing information, and fear of a watcher nobody sees)? Which habit of ours leads here? |
| Anchors and texture | Present-day anchors so the audience takes part; daily life (work, love, play, money, speech) decided in the reserve and shown in passing | How many familiar things are in the opening pages? Is texture taken for granted, or explained? |
| The unevolved hero | A moral lack in the hero that mirrors the society's, so the world cannot swamp an empty centre | Where does the hero's lack show in the world too? |
| The cast as society | Minor characters build the society more cheaply than exposition and define the hero by contrast: plain opposites (Spock and McCoy), other species with social rules unlike ours (Truby's *anthropology of space*), machines that expose human flaws *(built)* | Is any group defined only by one look or one trait? What human flaw does each machine expose? |
| A sharp aim | A concrete goal that drags the hero to the hinge technology and keeps momentum across the sprawl | Can the goal be named with an object or a place? |
| Point to the end | Hint early at the vortex point, not at the outcome: the *Star Wars* crawl points to the battle station; *Inception* lays out its three levels when the job is taken *(built)* | Does the audience know, broadly, where this is heading? |
| The chain of command | Linked opponents keep conflict constant while the top power stays remote; the opponents' own plan links the episodes | Does the opposing plan move between the hero's scenes? |
| Subworlds as chapters | Each subworld has a local goal serving the main one, a structural job (flaw, opponent, training, reversal) and one dominant look; storylines branch inward until one is left *(built)* | Could two subworlds swap places? Does any branch never rejoin? |
| The fork | One choice sends the society's future one of two ways, and is also the hero's self-revelation (Luke trusts the Force over the targeting computer) *(fitted)* | What are the two futures, concretely, and what does the choice show the hero has become? |
| Convergence | Every line meets at the vortex point at speed, with space, time and options shrinking together (a tiny target, a pursuer, a clock) *(built)*; or the battle is prevented (*Arrival*) | Which of those constraints are present? If the battle is averted, is a real war still on the clock? |
| A vision with morals | The self-revelation goes public or cosmic: the hero sees a role in the society and, at best, how a new order would work *(asserted; Truby admits few stories manage it)* | Can the new order be described as practices (how people treat strangers, share goods, settle disputes), or only as an image? |

## 7. The genre's question

**As a question for a draft:** which habit or tool does everyone in this world use without thinking, and what will the hero choose once they see it for what it is? Truby calls the genre's recipe for living well the *Way of the Social Creator* *(asserted)*: its root is the hero's work of improving the world for those who come after. In Gap terms a theme is the story's answer to the question its world poses; in this genre the question is built into the world (Implied World principle 8). **Case.** The Foundation trilogy puts a theory of how societies change into its hero's hands: Seldon's psychohistory both shapes the plot and is the tool he consults, so the audience watches the theory tested against events *(fitted, one example)*.

**Freedom and bondage in one system** *(built)*. Truby's point is that every society both frees and binds its people, so the question for a writer is how the mix works, not which kind of society this is. Three moves, in the order a writer can use them:
- **Decide how the regime steers.** Crudely, by force from the top, or artfully, by letting people believe they chose their chains *(built: deception wins compliance without the resistance that force provokes)*. *Brave New World* controls by comfort, drugs and engineered birth; *Nineteen Eighty-Four* by surveillance, propaganda and a shrinking language, and its hero's rebellion is love. **Move:** show a citizen defending the system that controls them.
- **Map the choices.** What citizens can truly choose, what only looks like choice, and what is forbidden. A society dresses as much steering as it can as choice, because people who feel forced leave or slack. Truby's picture is a game laid out like a ski slope: each trail is a real pick, but the slope carries the player to exits someone else chose. **Move:** plan the moment the hero sees a choice was only apparent, and make her final act a real one outside the funnel.
- **Place each person between cut off and swallowed.** Alone, a person shrinks to bare survival; dissolved into the whole, they turn machine-like. **Move:** put hero and cast on that line, and let the arc run towards being connected but still individual.

## 8. Where the care comes from

Truby's central warning is about care *(built)*: the genre fails most often by leaving the audience cold observers, and its vast world shrinks the hero. That is why it usually hangs its ideas on a Myth spine *(built in part: cool ideas need a long arc of growth to carry feeling; "almost always" is fitted)*. The Anticipation theory's care term says scale alone produces nothing: a planet's fate has to pass through someone the audience cares about. **Test:** if the society's fate would be the same without the hero, the story has a big backdrop, not an epic (Truby's word for a story where one person's deeds decide a people's fate). In Bond theory terms:
- **Desire** carries the hero: the sharp aim the world cannot swallow.
- **Flaw** gives the vast world something inside the hero to answer. **Vulnerability** needs watching: a hero who is only the innocent victim of a system turns to pity and then fatigue (the Bond theory's warning; its cure is pushing back, so **Agency** matters here). Truby's cure is a Flaw, which also removes the "innocent".
- **Agency** peaks at the fork: the deciding moment is an act, not a discovery.
- **Connection** is where the chapter's examples find their feeling: father and daughter (*Interstellar*), love as rebellion (*Nineteen Eighty-Four*), allies made rivals by the arena (*The Hunger Games*). Care is social (Bond principle 5): the minor cast shows how others treat the hero and how she treats them, and defines her by contrast.
- **Interiority** is where machines earn care, which is how the genre asks who is human (*Blade Runner*, *Ex Machina*). **Competence** shows in climaxes of skill: the shot down the trench.

**Run low:** **Connection** in the city stages, where in Truby's account families shrink and scatter and the individual stands alone *(fitted)*: rebuild at least one bond, or care thins. **Specificity**: a stage type (the rebel, the crushed dreamer) is recognised at once as a kind, and the Recognise rung needs a particular person, so habits, voice and history must make one. **Agency** in the crushed dreamer, whose Vulnerability then tips into pure victimhood (pity, then fatigue): give him one act of pushing back, or turn Interiority or Humour up to compensate. The recognisable world does quiet work here too: motives are legible only in a world the audience can read (the Understand rung). For the full method: `.claude/skills/character/SKILL.md`.

## 9. Suspense and knowledge

**The equation.** Suspense = Care × Threat × Uncertainty × Time. The genre's weak terms are care (section 8) and threat, when the top power is remote; the chain of command keeps a face of the threat in the room. Hinting at the vortex point early does not kill suspense: it names a place, not an outcome, so it gives direction and a clock (the Time term) while *whether* stays open. The Chosen One costs uncertainty; *The Matrix* buys it back by having Neo doubt the role and prove it only through a choice that may kill him (the workshop's reading, using the Anticipation theory's uncertainty term).

**Show the rules, hide the thing.** A rule with a severe price turns every use of it into a prediction problem (Anticipation principle 3). The same principle corrects Truby's picture of the oppressive city, where the system is so vast most people do not know it exists: the characters may not see it, but the audience needs its rules, or there is nothing to anticipate. Show the system through what it does to people; keep its full shape hidden.

**Lost refuge and the ratchet.** A single tight arena removes escape (*Alien*). Arenas can also nest, with pressure rising at each level inward until allies are forced into a two-person endgame (*The Hunger Games*) *(built)*. The strongest dystopias take the last safe place: *Nineteen Eighty-Four* makes love the site of betrayal, and *Brave New World* turns a need, pleasure, into the trap (Anticipation principle 4). Near the end, cutting between storylines speeds up as they merge. It works as a ratchet (principle 7) only if each cut away eases a storyline a little before the cut back raises it higher.

**Knowledge position.** The apparent-choice funnel sets up a clean decision: let the audience see the funnel before the hero does (we fear *for* her), or find it with her (surprise, and fear *with* her). The genre's biggest reveals are often about the world itself: the Gap theory's own case is the statue on the beach in *Planet of the Apes*. *Arrival* goes further: scenes the audience took as memory turn out to be foresight, a gap nobody knew was there. Truby finds it plays better on a second viewing, and anomalous suspense explains why the rewatch keeps its tension.

**Mysteries, wonders and residue.** The hidden system is a mystery: pressed, and owed an answer. The far reaches (who made the monolith, what the Star Child becomes) can stay wonders if the story never made them anyone's goal (section 13). Residue needs a future grown from the audience's own present (section 2), which is why an abstract dystopia does not linger. More: `.claude/skills/plot/references/suspense-and-fear.md` and `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`.

## 10. Subgenres and mixes

**Subgenres Truby names**, grouped here by what each varies: the scale or setting (space opera, alien invasion, military and adventure stories); time (time travel, alternate history); the social stage (dystopian, post-apocalyptic, cyberpunk, steampunk); strictness about science (hard Science Fiction). And the *Robinsonade*, after *Robinson Crusoe*: a castaway builds a society from nothing, the island serving as a laboratory for ways of living (for this reason Truby counts *The Tempest* as the first Science Fiction story). **Move:** if you use the island, decide whether the story endorses what the castaway builds or exposes its cost. *Crusoe* approves of a society built on slavery and seized land, and a teller who approves of himself opens a perspective gap (Gap principle 6) for a modern reader.

**Mixes named:** with Myth, the usual spine; with Horror, dystopia and the made monster (*Frankenstein*, *Alien*); with the Western, the *space Western* (*Star Wars*, *The Mandalorian*), Truby's blunt advice for a popular hit *(fitted from box office)*, cheap in Implied World terms because the audience brings the Western's defaults; and with Action, Comedy, Gangster, Thriller and the Caper (*Aliens*, *Men in Black*, *Squid Game*, *Dark*, *Inception*). **Neighbours:** `myth.md` (the growth arc), `horror.md` (Science Fiction with Horror), `western.md` (the frontier), `fantasy.md`. Truby hands on to `crime.md` *(asserted)*: Science Fiction shows the big picture but says little about getting through an ordinary day among others, so if a draft feels abstract, borrow from Crime a concrete wrong and a question of justice at street level. On which genre leads a mix: `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`.

## 11. Twisting it

Truby's general routes above a genre are in `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`. His Science Fiction chapter names two, both mixes in which the lead genre changes the result *(asserted)*:
- **Science Fiction leading Myth:** the story becomes an account of a society and its culture coming into being and changing (*Dune*, the Foundation trilogy). With Myth leading, the same materials give a myth of the species evolving (*2001*).
- **Science Fiction leading Horror:** the dystopia. Most dystopias fail, in Truby's view, because the audience cannot find its own society in them *(built)*; the deeper version shows one society freeing and binding at once. **Test:** list the real freedoms your dystopia still gives and the real pleasures that keep people loyal. If the list is empty, the society is a cartoon.

Other moves from the chapter: let the plot's shape enact the theme (*Arrival*'s loop, for a heroine who learns to see time whole); break the cycle of collapse with renewal (the Ecological Myth in *Avatar*); make the hero self-made rather than chosen.

**One idea from the owner theories: ripple the departure into the place the genre's furniture never reaches.** Thematic fit (Implied World principle 8) says the main departure should embody the story's question; ripple (principle 3) says it convinces in proportion to its knock-on effects. Put together: follow the departure past the laboratory and the battlefield into private life, until it reaches what the hero loves. *Children of Men* imagines a world without births, and the ripple reaches empty schools and a despair no one can name; the question (can hope exist without a future?) and the world become the same thing. *Never Let Me Go* follows its organ farming into friendship, jealousy and a rumour that true love might buy time. **Test by removal:** cut every consequence of the departure that touches home, friendship or love, and keep only the lab, the institution and the battlefield. If the fork and the theme land as hard, the private-life ripple is doing no work. If the hero's loss no longer comes from the departure, the ripple was what held the theme in place.

## 12. Examples

- *Star Wars: A New Hope* and *The Empire Strikes Back*: a vortex point named in the crawl; subworlds as chapters; a chain of command; the trench run; a space Western.
- *Interstellar* and *Inception*: a father and daughter at the core; a Caper mixed in, its levels laid out when the job is taken.
- *Arrival*: a circular shape that enacts its theme; a war prevented.
- *The Matrix*: a comfortable illusion; a Chosen One proved by a doubting choice; an ally who sells out the crew; no picture of the new order.
- *Blade Runner* and *Ex Machina*: the hunter less humane than his prey; machines that earn care.
- *2001: A Space Odyssey*: a species as hero; the Star Child as an image without an ethic, or as a wonder.
- *Dune* and the Foundation trilogy: one scarcity rippled everywhere; a theory of history in the hero's hands.
- *Children of Men* and *The Hunger Games*: a standing normal and the breach that breaks it; nested arenas and a two-person trap.
- *Brave New World* and *Nineteen Eighty-Four*: control by pleasure, and control by fear.

## 13. Rivals and doubts

- **Invent everything, or build the differences.** Truby: the writer must create everything and detail as many rules as possible. Implied World principles 1, 2 and 6: the audience supplies the rest from the baseline, every departure costs attention, and depth comes from what is sensed below the surface. Truby's own recognisable world and hinge technology side with the theory. *Test:* replace a page of explained society with three details taken for granted in passing (the theory's "the door dilated"); the theory predicts the depth holds or grows, and attention returns to the hero.
- **An open vision: failure or wonder.** Truby faults *2001* and *The Matrix* for never spelling out the ethics of the new order: how people will live. Gap principle 5 and Implied World principle 7: a question the story never promised to answer may stay a wonder, and emphasis decides which it is. *Test:* first ask whether characters chased the question; if they did, the story owes the answer and Truby is right. If they did not, add a closing beat that shows the new order as practices (how people will treat strangers, share goods, settle disputes) and ask whether the ending grows, as Truby predicts, or shrinks, as the wonder reading predicts.
- **The system as villain.** Truby's oppressive-city opponent is the binding system itself. Bond principle 6: a villain fails by being a function rather than a person; Anticipation principle 3: an audience cannot anticipate a threat it cannot model. Truby's own cure, a face for the system and a chain of opponents with a rival vision, matches the theories; the clash comes only when a draft stops at "the system is the villain". *Test:* cut Agent Smith from an outline of *The Matrix*; the theories predict the threat term drops sharply.
- **Designed or lived-in.** Fractals and a neat match of stage, economy, culture and hero type can make a world look designed. Ripple and thematic fit want a system (*Dune* feels like one); surplus and indifference (Implied World principles 5 and 4) want details that serve no purpose and business that has nothing to do with the hero. *Test:* add a detail that fits no stage and no fractal, a barometer in Barthes' sense. Truby's model has no place for it; the theory predicts it adds reality.
- **Doubts.** A single fixed sequence of social stages is a strong historical claim that anthropologists largely reject; use it as a menu, not a law. The claim that nearly every story runs from bondage to freedom is a rhetorical figure broad enough to fit any arc. Truby's claims that science is itself storytelling and that a society's technology measures its consciousness are framings, not mechanisms. The stage model's claims about gender, cities and politics are opinion. Calling a broad society impossible to build without fractals overclaims: many convincing societies were built otherwise. The beats are looser here than in any other chapter, as Truby says, and the examples lean on Hollywood film.
