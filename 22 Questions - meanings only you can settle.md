## Q1. May a scene show the world at its own business and do nothing else?

**What came up.** A draft has a short market-day scene. Nothing in it moves the plot; it just shows how the town lives. Your Implied World theory says a world feels real when it has business of its own, and that purposeless detail makes a world. Your Gap theory measures scenes by the gaps they open and close. The plot skill had been telling writers to cut any scene that changes no gap, which would cut the market day.

**Options.**
- (a) No. The world's business rides along inside scenes that also do plot work.
- (b) Yes, if it is short and shows the world under some pressure. It then does a real job: if the business shows a departure, it changes the departure gap; if it is ordinary business, it does the Implied World theory's work of indifference and surplus.
- (c) Yes, and length is governed only by the attention budget.

**Now.** The skills use (b), marked as the workshop's reading.
**My recommendation.** (b). It keeps both theories whole: the scene is doing a job your theories name, and the attention budget keeps it short.

**Status:** open.

## Q2. What does "gap" mean?

**What came up.** In the Implied World theory a gap is something nobody fills in, left open on purpose. In the Gap theory a gap is one of five layers (departure, breach, withholding, perspective, knowledge), and also any single open question ("a gap about the future"). The skills now say which sense they mean at first use, but they don't agree on one default.

**Options.**
- (a) Keep both senses. Each skill says which it means.
- (b) Keep "gap" for the Gap theory's senses, and call the Implied World kind an "open space" or "unfilled space".
- (c) Keep "gap" for the Implied World sense, and call the Gap theory's five "layers".

**Now.** (a).
**My recommendation.** (a) for now, because both theories are frozen and use the word. If you revise either theory, pick (b) or (c) and the skills will follow.

**Status:** open.

## Q3. Does "show the rules, hide the thing" apply to human villains?

**What came up.** Your Anticipation theory says to give the audience the threat's rules but keep its nature and origin hidden, because explaining a monster's backstory makes it less frightening. The plot skill applies this to human threats too (hide what a person really is and where they came from). The character skill gives a human opponent a full case and, often, a history.

**Options.**
- (a) Monsters only. Human opponents get a full case.
- (b) Partly. For a human threat, show their present case; keep their origin and full nature hidden.
- (c) Fully, as for monsters.

**Now.** (b), in both the plot and character skills, marked as a stopgap until you decide.
**My recommendation.** (b). It fits the theory's reason (explained origins drain fear) and the Bond theory's point that a villain must be a person, not a function.

**Status:** open.

**Status:** open.

**Status:** open.

## Q7. A genre's promise: only open questions?

**Status:** open.
