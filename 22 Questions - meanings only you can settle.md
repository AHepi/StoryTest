# 22 Questions - meanings only you can settle

Building the craft skills turned up places where your theories can be read more than one way, or where two of them use one word differently. The skills can't decide these for you without changing what your theories say. So each one below records the workshop's current reading, labelled as the workshop's, and waits for your answer.

For each question: what came up, the options, and my recommendation. Answer any you like, in any order, in a sentence each. The first three change the most.

## 1. May a scene show the world at its own business and do nothing else?

**What came up.** A draft has a short market-day scene. Nothing in it moves the plot; it just shows how the town lives. Your Implied World theory says a world feels real when it has business of its own, and that purposeless detail makes a world. Your Gap theory measures scenes by the gaps they open and close. The plot skill had been telling writers to cut any scene that changes no gap, which would cut the market day.

**Options.**
- (a) No. The world's business rides along inside scenes that also do plot work.
- (b) Yes, if it is short, because showing the world changes the departure gap (the audience learns what the world is like).
- (c) Yes, and length is governed only by the attention budget.

**Now.** The skills use (b), marked as the workshop's reading.
**My recommendation.** (b). It keeps both theories whole: the scene is doing a job, the departure gap's job, and the attention budget keeps it short.

## 2. What does "gap" mean?

**What came up.** In the Implied World theory a gap is something nobody fills in, left open on purpose. In the Gap theory a gap is one of five layers (departure, breach, withholding, perspective, knowledge), and also any single open question ("a gap about the future"). The skills now say which sense they mean at first use, but they don't agree on one default.

**Options.**
- (a) Keep both senses. Each skill says which it means.
- (b) Keep "gap" for the Gap theory's senses, and call the Implied World kind an "open space" or "unfilled space".
- (c) Keep "gap" for the Implied World sense, and call the Gap theory's five "layers".

**Now.** (a).
**My recommendation.** (a) for now, because both theories are frozen and use the word. If you revise either theory, pick (b) or (c) and the skills will follow.

## 3. Does "show the rules, hide the thing" apply to human villains?

**What came up.** Your Anticipation theory says to give the audience the threat's rules but keep its nature and origin hidden, because explaining a monster's backstory makes it less frightening. The plot skill applies this to human threats too (hide what a person really is and where they came from). The character skill gives a human opponent a full case and, often, a history.

**Options.**
- (a) Monsters only. Human opponents get a full case.
- (b) Partly. For a human threat, show their present case; keep their origin and full nature hidden.
- (c) Fully, as for monsters.

**Now.** Both readings are in the skills, marked as the workshop's.
**My recommendation.** (b). It fits the theory's reason (explained origins drain fear) and the Bond theory's point that a villain must be a person, not a function.

## 4. "Price": attention or stakes?

The Implied World theory uses "price" for the attention a departure costs the audience. The Gap theory uses it for what the world makes things cost the characters. **Options:** (a) price = attention, "cost" = stakes; (b) price = stakes, "attention cost" for the other; (c) both, always qualified. **Now:** (c). **My recommendation:** (a), since the Implied World theory introduces it first and defines it most exactly.

## 5. "Wonder": a feeling or a promise?

The Gap theory's table says the departure gap produces "wonder" (a feeling). Its principle 5 says a "wonder" is an open question the story promises to leave open. **Options:** (a) keep "wonder" for the open question, and call the feeling "strangeness" or "enchantment"; (b) allow both, always qualified. **Now:** mixed. **My recommendation:** (a).

## 6. "Dread": one rung, or three things?

The fear ladder's lowest rung is Dread ("something is wrong, but not what"). The Gap theory's table says the knowledge gap produces dread (the audience ahead, as with Hitchcock's bomb). The Anticipation theory also has "dread by proxy" (the audience behind). **Options:** (a) "dread" for the rung only, and "the suspense of helplessness" (your phrase) for audience-ahead; (b) keep all three, qualified. **Now:** (a). **My recommendation:** (a).

## 7. A genre's promise: only open questions?

The Gap theory's principle 5 is about open questions. The genre skill stretches "promise" to the moments a genre owes its audience (for a Love Story, the couple's first real meeting), and the plot skill stretches it to set-ups and foreshadowing. Both are flagged as the workshop's. **Options:** (a) accept both stretches; (b) keep "promise" for open questions and say "owed moments" and "set-ups" for the rest. **My recommendation:** (a). Dropping an owed moment reads as the same betrayal the principle describes.

## 8. Which lever is Holmes's "warmth"?

The Bond theory says Holmes is low on warmth, but warmth isn't one of the ten levers. The character skill reads it as low Connection (Watson supplies it). **Options:** Connection; Decency; Humour; a mix. **My recommendation:** Connection.
