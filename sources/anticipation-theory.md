---

**Anticipation theory**, and it slots in with the other two. Its central claim is that suspense and fear don't come from what happens. They come from what the audience expects *might* happen. The audience does the frightening themselves. The writer's job is to make them care, give them enough to predict with, and never let them feel sure.

## Part 1: The theory

**The suspense equation**

> **Suspense = Care × Threat × Uncertainty × Time**

The four terms multiply rather than add, so if any one of them is zero, the suspense is zero. A huge bomb under a table of people nobody cares about produces no suspense. Neither does a beloved hero who obviously can't lose, or a threat that arrives with no warning at all.

**The fear ladder.** Each rung closes one gap, which makes the fear more intense but uses up some of the audience's imagination.

| Rung | What the audience knows | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Dread** | Something is wrong, but not what | The house is too quiet |
| **Suspense** | What the threat is, but not how it ends | The shark is in the water |
| **Terror** | It's here, but unseen | Something breathing in the dark |
| **Horror** | They see it | The creature in full light |
| *Shock* (a side rung) | Nothing: it arrives without warning | The jump scare |
| **Residue** | What stays with them afterwards | You check the back seat of your car |

**Eight principles**
1. **Multiplication.** Build care, threat, uncertainty and time together. Suspense peaks when the odds look bad but not hopeless.
2. **The ladder.** Climb slowly and linger below the top rung. When you reach horror, the thing shown must be worse or stranger than what the audience imagined.
3. **Show the rules, hide the thing.** The audience needs enough of the threat's rules to predict danger, but not enough to understand it.
4. **Lost refuge.** Fear is strongest when it invades a safe place or makes an everyday necessity dangerous.
5. **Broken contract.** Fear needs the audience to believe the genre's usual protections are gone.
6. **Knowledge position.** Choose, scene by scene, whether the audience knows more than, the same as, or less than the characters.
7. **The ratchet.** Tension should rise in steps with partial releases, and the pattern must break before the audience learns it.
8. **Residue.** Fear outlasts the story when the threat could exist in the audience's own world and something stays unresolved.

## Part 2: What the theory says

**1. Suspense is a multiplication.**
Hitchcock's bomb under the table shows the *time* term. If the bomb just goes off, you get seconds of surprise. If the audience sees it first, you get minutes of suspense.

The *care* term comes from the media psychologist Dolf Zillmann's work: we only feel suspense for characters we're invested in. That's why slasher films full of disposable victims deliver spectacle rather than suspense. Care has to be built *before* the threat arrives, and in Gap theory terms that means showing the normal before the breach. The audience has to see what could be lost.

The *uncertainty* term has a sweet spot, and it isn't fifty-fifty. Studies following Zillmann's work (Comisky and Bryant, 1982) found suspense climbs as the hero's chances drop, then collapses once hope is gone entirely. Bad odds are good; no odds are despair.

Uncertainty can also move from *whether* to *how*. *Chronicle of a Death Foretold* announces its killing in the title, and *The Secret History* tells you in its first line that Bunny is dead, yet both are tense. That's why re-watching still works. The psychologist Richard Gerrig calls it "anomalous suspense": while we're inside the story, we feel its uncertainty even when we know the ending.

**2. Fear climbs a ladder, and every rung uses up imagination.**
Edmund Burke wrote in 1757 that "to make any thing very terrible, obscurity seems in general to be necessary." Ann Radcliffe split terror, which expands the mind, from horror, which freezes it. Stephen King ranks them the same way: terror first, then horror, then the gross-out if all else fails.

The theory explains why this order holds. Each rung up closes a gap, and the audience's imagination was filling that gap with the worst thing it could build. *Jaws* learned this by accident: the mechanical shark kept breaking down, so Spielberg showed barrels and a two-note theme, and the full shark doesn't appear until more than an hour in. Shirley Jackson's *The Haunting of Hill House* reaches pure terror with nothing seen at all. Eleanor spends a night gripping Theo's hand in the dark, then finds out Theo was across the room: "whose hand was I holding?"

When you finally climb to horror, the reveal has to beat what the audience imagined. The philosopher Noël Carroll argued that effective monsters break categories: dead yet alive, human yet animal, inside the body yet coming out. The chestburster in *Alien* is frightening less for its teeth than for where it comes from. Freud's "uncanny" is the same move turned on everyday life: the familiar made strange.

**3. Show the rules, hide the thing.**
Pure obscurity has a limit. Suspense is anticipation, and an audience can't anticipate a threat it can't model at all. So the strongest modern horror gives clear rules and withholds everything else. In *A Quiet Place*, sound kills. In *It Follows*, something walks slowly towards you, always, looking like anyone. With rules like these, every scene becomes a prediction problem, and a dropped plate is suspense.

This connects to the mystery/wonder distinction from the Gap theory. The rules work like mysteries: stated, tested and kept to. The monster's nature and origin work better as wonders. That's why horror franchises tend to get less frightening as they explain their monsters' backstories.

**4. Take away a refuge.**
Fear intensifies when the threat enters somewhere that was supposed to be safe: the home, the bed, your child, your own body, broad daylight (*Midsommar*). The most effective monsters go further and make an unavoidable necessity dangerous: sleep in *A Nightmare on Elm Street*, sound in *A Quiet Place*, looking in *Bird Box*. You can't opt out of a need, so there's no safe action left.

Stripping away help does the same work: the phone line is cut, the car won't start, the police don't believe you. In Implied World terms, horror is a world whose rules turn against the people living in it.

**5. Break the contract early.**
Genres carry unspoken guarantees: the star survives, the child is safe, the dog lives. As long as the audience trusts those, some part of them relaxes. *Psycho* kills its apparent protagonist less than halfway through, and *Hereditary* does something just as unthinkable early on. After that, no guarantee holds and every threat is real.

There's a limit, though. If everyone dies all the time, care collapses and so does suspense. Break one guarantee convincingly rather than all of them.

**6. Choose who knows what.**
This is the Gap theory's knowledge gap aimed at fear, and it gives three positions:
- **Audience ahead** (we see the bomb or the killer in the closet): suspense of helplessness. We fear *for* the character and want to shout "don't open that door."
- **Audience level** (tight point of view, found footage like *The Blair Witch Project*): shared terror. We fear *with* the character.
- **Audience behind** (the character sees something we can't): dread by proxy. Carroll noted that characters' reactions cue our own, so a scream at something offscreen frightens us before we know why.

Great sequences move between these positions. The key moment is often the instant the audience realises the character isn't alone, just before the character does.

**7. Tension is a ratchet.**
Tension that only rises goes numb, and tension that fully releases resets to zero. The ratchet rises, eases off a little, then rises higher.

False alarms are a tool. The term "Lewton bus" comes from *Cat People* (1942): a woman is stalked down a dark street, the tension peaks, and a bus hisses into frame. The audience jumps, laughs and lowers its guard, and the real threat arrives in that release.

Audiences learn a film's rhythm fast (music swells, then the scare), so the rhythm has to break: a scare with no musical warning, or a long wait where nothing comes. A jump scare on its own is a reflex, not fear. After real dread it works as a release valve; without dread it's just a flinch, and audiences stop reacting to it.

**8. Leave something behind.**
The fear that follows you home comes from two things. First, the threat has to be something the audience can't rule out in their own world. *Paranormal Activity* is a house at night, and you have one of those. Second, the ending has to leave a gap open: the threat survives, or the rule is never broken.

The deepest residue comes from theme, as in the earlier theories. Good monsters give a body to a fear the audience already carries: grief in *The Babadook*, racism in *Get Out*, distrust in *The Thing*. When the scare fades, the anxiety it stood for is still there.

**Books and film**

| | Prose | Film |
|---|---|---|
| Who controls time | The reader; the writer steers with sentence length, white space and chapter breaks | The filmmaker, who can hold a shot until it's unbearable |
| Strongest rungs | Dread and terror, because the reader builds the monster | Suspense and horror, because the audience can see what characters can't |
| Sound | Only described | Ears can't close; silence and low rumbles work on the body |
| Jump scares | Nearly impossible | Easy, and easy to overuse |
| A threat in the background | Mentioning it points straight at it | It can sit unnoticed in the frame (*It Follows*) |
| Signature tool | The narrator who knows the ending ("Had I known then…") | Offscreen sound, the held shot, the reaction shot |

**Questions for a scene**
1. Do we care before the threat arrives, and what exactly could be lost?
2. Which rung is this scene on, and am I climbing too fast?
3. What are the threat's rules, and what about it stays hidden?
4. Which refuge have I taken away?
5. Is the audience ahead of, level with, or behind the characters?
6. Has the audience learned my rhythm yet, and where do I break it?

**In one line:** make them care, give them the rules, hide the thing, take away the safe places, and never let them be sure. Their imagination will do the rest.

**Where the parts come from:**
- The bomb is Hitchcock's.
- The case for obscurity comes from Burke.
- The terror/horror split is Radcliffe's and King's.
- The role of caring is Zillmann's.
- Anomalous suspense is Gerrig's.
- Category-breaking monsters and characters' reactions cueing ours are Carroll's.

What's new here is the multiplication, the ladder as a series of closing gaps, "show the rules, hide the thing," the principle of making a necessity dangerous, and the three knowledge positions as a tool for each scene.
