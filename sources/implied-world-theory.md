# The Implied World theory

*Supplied by the repository owner on 23 September 2026. Frozen source: the text below is reproduced as supplied; only this heading and note were added. Craft skills derive from it and do not change it; a revised version is added as a new revision, not edited in place.*

---

I've called it the **Implied World theory**. Its central claim is that a fictional world is never actually on the page or screen. The audience infers it from what they're shown. It feels real when it seems to go on past what's shown, to keep working when nobody's looking, and to exist for reasons other than the hero.

## Part 1: The theory

**Key terms**
- **Baseline:** what the audience assumes by default. That's the real world plus the habits of the genre.
- **Departure:** any way the fictional world differs from the baseline, such as a magic system, a political order, a ruined climate or a slang.
- **Surface:** what's actually shown or stated.
- **Implication:** what the surface logically requires. If there are border guards, someone pays them.
- **Reserve:** what the creator knows but never shows.
- **Gap:** what nobody fills in, left open on purpose for the audience.

**Eight principles**
1. **Minimal departure.** Audiences assume the world works like theirs unless told otherwise. So worldbuilding means choosing and managing departures, not inventing everything.
2. **Attention budget.** Every departure takes effort to learn. That effort comes from the same limited attention the audience needs for plot and character.
3. **Ripple.** A departure convinces in proportion to its knock-on effects.
4. **Indifference.** A world feels real when it has business of its own that has nothing to do with the protagonist.
5. **Surplus.** Details that serve the plot make a plot. Details that serve no purpose make a world.
6. **Iceberg ratio.** How deep a world feels depends on how much the audience senses beneath the surface, not on how much is shown.
7. **Productive gaps.** Incompleteness is a feature. Gaps are where the audience's imagination does its work, and that work turns into attachment.
8. **Thematic fit.** The world's main departure should embody the story's main question.

**The medium corollary:** Prose builds by adding and film builds by taking away. In a book, nothing exists until it's named. In a film, everything in the frame exists at once.

## Part 2: What the theory says

**1. You don't build a world; you build the differences.**
Audiences arrive with a complete world already in their heads, the real one. They fill in anything you don't mention from it. The literary theorist Marie-Laure Ryan called this the principle of minimal departure: if the story doesn't say gravity works differently, it works normally. Genre adds a second set of defaults. Say "space station" or "elven city" and the audience furnishes it from a hundred earlier examples.

So what you're really designing is the list of departures. Each one has a price, because every new rule, name or custom uses attention the plot also needs. A story with forty invented terms on page one has overspent. A fantasy that's just medieval Europe with dragons has spent almost nothing, and it feels generic because it is: it's riding on the genre's defaults. The skill is to spend on a few departures that matter and let the baseline carry the rest.

**2. Every difference needs consequences, and the biggest one should be the theme.**
Frederik Pohl's famous line was that science fiction should predict not the car but the traffic jam. That's the ripple principle. In *Dune* the departure is a shortage of water, and Herbert follows it everywhere: suits recycle sweat, the dead are rendered down for their water, and spitting on a table is a mark of respect. None of these needs explaining, because they all follow from one rule. That's why the world feels like a system rather than decoration.

A departure without consequences reads as costume. A departure with consequences the author didn't plan can break the world. The Time-Turner in *Prisoner of Azkaban* implied that any disaster could be undone, so the series had to destroy every one of them two books later.

The best worlds go one step further and make the main departure the story's question. *The Handmaid's Tale* builds a state around controlling fertility because the book is about who owns a body. *Children of Men* imagines a world without births because it asks whether hope can exist without a future. At that point the world and the theme are the same thing.

**3. The world shouldn't care about the hero.**
Set-dressing worlds exist only where the camera points: the town is there to be saved and the tavern is there to hand out a quest. Convincing worlds have their own business. *Alien* opens with the crew grumbling about their bonus shares before anything alien appears. From that we learn that this future has employers, contracts and resentment, and that the Company has its own agenda, which turns out to be the plot. A useful test: if your protagonist died in chapter one, what would the world be doing next week?

Surplus detail works the same way. Roland Barthes pointed to a barometer in a Flaubert story that serves no purpose at all. He argued that its uselessness is its job: it signals "this is real," because real life is full of things that don't matter. Chekhov's gun makes a plot; Flaubert's barometer makes a world.

How you deliver surplus matters. It works best mentioned in passing, taken for granted by characters who've lived with it all their lives. Heinlein's sentence "The door dilated" tells you more about a society than a paragraph on door technology would, because nobody in it finds the door remarkable.

**4. Show less, imply more, and leave gaps on purpose.**
Tolkien is the model here. There are songs about stories we never hear, Tom Bombadil is never explained, and the Entwives are lost somewhere offstage. Tolkien himself said part of the book's appeal was the glimpses of a large history in the background, like seeing an unvisited island far off. The appendices are the reserve. The novel just lets you feel their weight.

Gaps are where the audience's imagination goes to work. Le Guin makes this explicit in "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas," where the narrator invites the reader to add whatever details would make the city believable. A good gap has edges: you know what *kind* of thing is missing, like a war, a lost people or an older magic, even without the specifics. A gap without edges is just vagueness.

This answers worldbuilding's best-known critic, M. John Harrison, who called it "the great clomping foot of nerdism." The theory says he's right about showing, not about building. The problem is dumping the reserve onto the surface: the encyclopedia chapter, or two characters explaining to each other things they both already know. Build as much as you like. Just show the tip.

**Why books and films differ**

| | Prose | Film |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Empty: nothing exists until it's named | Full: everything in the frame exists at once |
| Cheap to do | History, law, belief, inner life | Texture, crowds, objects, sound |
| Expensive to do | Sensory density (every detail costs a sentence) | Abstraction (institutions, rules, history) |
| Where surplus lives | A clause in passing | The background of the frame |
| Where gaps live | Whatever goes unnamed | Offscreen space and sound |
| Typical failure | The info-dump | Exposition dialogue, title crawls, clutter |

Each medium fails when it tries to do what the other does cheaply. *Children of Men* and *Mad Max: Fury Road* put their worldbuilding in the background and explain almost nothing, which is film at its strongest.

This also explains why adaptations disappoint. Filming a book forces a decision on every gap the author left open, so adaptations often feel smaller than the book even when they're lavish.

**What it predicts**
- **The expansion paradox.** Franchises grow by turning reserve and gaps into official canon, so worlds can feel smaller as they get bigger. The midi-chlorians in *The Phantom Menace* are the classic case: the Force went from mystery to blood test.
- **Worlds with many departures need a guide.** Either an outsider learns alongside us (Harry, Alice, Dorothy, Neo), or there's an outside aid, like Star Wars' opening crawl, *Dune*'s glossary or Tolkien's maps. Without one, the story feels hard going.
- **Fans gather at the gaps.** Fan fiction and theorising cluster where the official story is silent, not where it's detailed.

**In one line:** build the whole iceberg, show the tip, make the audience feel what's underneath, and make every difference cost something.

**Where the parts come from:** minimal departure is Ryan's idea, surplus detail is Barthes' "reality effect," and incompleteness as a feature draws on Tolkien's "On Fairy-Stories" and Lubomír Doležel's work on fictional worlds. What I've added is joining them into one account of how the audience infers a world, plus the attention budget, indifference, the book-versus-film split and the expansion paradox.
