I've called it the **Bond theory**. Its central claim is that audiences don't care about characters because of their traits. They care because of the relationship the story builds with them: time spent together, access to their mind, understanding what they want and fearing what they could lose. Traits make that relationship easier or harder to build. The aim isn't that the audience *likes* a character; it's that they can't stop caring what happens to them.

## Part 1: The theory

**The ladder of care**

| Rung | The audience thinks… | Built by |
|---|---|---|
| **Recognise** | "That's a particular person" | Specific details, a distinct voice or face |
| **Understand** | "I see why they do that" | Access to their mind, a clear motive |
| **Side with** | "I want them to get it" | A goal we share, sympathy, fairness |
| **Worry** | "I'm afraid for them" | Vulnerability and stakes |
| **Miss** | "I'd grieve if they were gone" | Time, shared history, relationships |

The **Worry** rung is the "care" term in the suspense equation from the Anticipation theory. The **Miss** rung is what makes the audience return for a sequel.

**Seven principles**
1. **Care is a relationship, not a verdict.** It builds through time and shared experience, as it does with real people.
2. **Alignment beats virtue.** We tend to side with whoever we spend time with and understand, often whatever they've done.
3. **Wanting drives attention.** A character who wants something gives the audience something to want on their behalf.
4. **Balance the levers.** No character needs every lever. When you turn one down, turn another up.
5. **Care is social.** We learn to value a character partly through how others treat them, and how they treat others.
6. **Indifference is the enemy, not dislike.** Villains need care too: we care what happens to them.
7. **Surprise deepens the bond.** Care grows when a character does something unexpected that still makes sense for them.

**The ten levers**

| Lever | What it does | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| **Desire:** wants something specific | Gives the audience a goal to share | A want with no reason behind it feels mechanical |
| **Vulnerability:** can be hurt, has something to lose | Makes worry possible | Pure victimhood turns to pity, then fatigue; we need them to push back |
| **Agency:** acts rather than reacts | Earns respect and attention | A character who's always right and never pays a price becomes a power fantasy |
| **Competence:** good at something | The pleasure of watching skill; can carry a cold or cruel character | Too much kills suspense (the Superman problem) and reads as wish-fulfilment |
| **Decency:** kind, fair, principled | We root for good people | Goodness with no flaw is bland; kindness put on for the audience's benefit (the "save the cat" moment) looks manipulative |
| **Flaw:** a real weakness or wrong belief | Makes them human and makes change possible | Cosmetic flaws ("clumsy," "cares too much") don't count; severe flaws alienate unless other levers compensate |
| **Interiority:** we see how they think | Understanding, and allegiance even with bad people | Morally neutral and very powerful; over-narrating every thought removes mystery |
| **Specificity:** particular habits, voice, history | Makes them feel real | Piling on quirks; details should reveal something, not decorate |
| **Humour:** wit, charm | We like good company, and charm offsets cruelty | Constant quips undercut stakes; you can't fear for someone who takes nothing seriously |
| **Connection:** loves and is loved | Gives them stakes, and others' love vouches for them | Relationships that are claimed but never shown, or loved ones who exist only to be used as leverage |

## Part 2: What the theory says

**1. Care is a relationship, not a verdict.**
In the 1950s, the researchers Horton and Wohl described "parasocial" relationships: the one-sided bonds audiences form with people they only know through media. Fictional characters get the same treatment. That's why TV characters can feel like friends, and why losing one can hurt like a real loss.

The time involved is felt time, not clock time, and it can be compressed. The opening montage of *Up* covers a whole marriage in about four minutes and leaves audiences in tears. It works because it climbs every rung of the ladder in order: specific details, a shared dream, small sacrifices, and then the loss.

**2. We side with whoever we're with.**
The film theorist Murray Smith separated *alignment* (whose company we keep and whose mind we can see into) from *allegiance* (whose side we're on). His insight was that alignment tends to produce allegiance even when morality says it shouldn't.

Hitchcock noticed this in *Psycho*. When the car Norman Bates is sinking in the swamp stops halfway, the audience wants it to go down, even though they know he's covering up a murder. Patricia Highsmith's Ripley novels get readers rooting for a killer to get away with it. *The Sopranos* put Tony in therapy, which gave viewers direct access to the mind of a man who beats people to death, and they stayed.

This is why interiority is the most powerful lever and the most dangerous one. It works regardless of whether the character deserves it. Research following Zillmann suggests audiences often decide who they like first and then adjust their moral judgements to fit.

**3. Wanting and wounding.**
Aristotle said tragedy works through pity and fear: pity for suffering that isn't deserved, and fear because the sufferer is like us. That maps onto two levers. **Desire** gives us something to hope for, and **vulnerability** gives us something to fear. Together they get the audience to the Worry rung.

*WALL-E* reaches it with almost no dialogue. A robot who collects small treasures and can't decide whether a spork belongs with the spoons or the forks is recognisable in seconds. He wants company, he's small in a vast empty world, and we're worried for him within minutes.

**4. The balancing act.**
Brandon Sanderson describes three sliders (likability, competence and proactivity), and if one is low you raise another. The Bond theory extends that to all ten levers.

- **Sherlock Holmes** is low on warmth but very high on competence and specificity. Watson supplies the connection and the admiring point of view, so we learn to care about Holmes through someone who does.
- **Elizabeth Bennet** runs on wit, desire and a real flaw: the prejudice of the title.
- **Walter White** tests the limits on purpose. His creator described the arc as turning Mr Chips into Scarface. As Walt's decency drains away, competence, interiority and his family keep viewers attached, until for many they don't. A lot of viewers can name the exact moment they gave up on him.

**5. Care is social.**
We read characters the way we read people: by who loves them and how they treat those who can't help them. Sam's devotion makes Frodo matter more, and it makes us love Sam.

This also runs in reverse. Carroll noted in horror that characters' reactions cue the audience's, and care works the same way. A character everyone else trusts carries a borrowed warmth. A character who's gentle with a child or rude to a waiter tells us more than any description could.

**6. Indifference is the enemy.**
The opposite of care isn't hate; it's indifference. Audiences cheered Joffrey's death in *Game of Thrones* because they were invested in his downfall, and that investment is care pointing the other way. A villain works when we badly want something to happen to them.

Villains use the same levers as heroes. Hannibal Lecter has famously little screen time but maximum competence, specificity and wit. What makes a villain fail is not being disliked. It's being a function rather than a person.

**7. Surprise deepens the bond.**
E. M. Forster's test of a rounded character was whether it's "capable of surprising in a convincing way." Care deepens when a character does something unexpected that turns out to have been in them all along. It breaks when they do something that contradicts who they've been without the story earning it. That's the audience's complaint that something was "out of character," which really means their bond was broken.

**The limits of care**
- **Care can't be ordered.** Telling us a character is brilliant or kind, or having others praise them, makes audiences resist. They believe what they see.
- **Some acts end allegiance for most audiences.** Time and interiority stretch that line a long way, but not forever, and where it sits varies from reader to reader.
- **Visible levers backfire.** The orphan, the dead spouse and the rescued dog feel cheap when they serve the audience's tear ducts rather than the character's story.
- **Suffering without response numbs.** Audiences need the character to fight back, adapt, or at least feel it in a specific way.
- **Too many characters dilute care.** Deep bonds are expensive, so ensembles need a small inner circle.
- **The bond is a promise.** You can hurt, change or kill a beloved character, but it has to mean something for *them*. The comics writer Gail Simone named the common abuse "fridging": killing a character only to motivate someone else.

**Books and film**

| | Prose | Film and TV |
|---|---|---|
| Fastest route to recognition | A voice | A face |
| Interiority | Built in: first person, a narrator slipping into a character's thoughts | Has to be borrowed: close-ups, voiceover, confidants, therapy (*The Sopranos*), talking to camera (*Fleabag*) |
| What the audience brings | Their imagination builds the face | The actor's charisma and their history with that star |
| Time | Many hours of reading | Two hours for a film; dozens for a series, which is why TV bonds run deepest |
| Typical risk | Narrating every thought | Charisma covering for an empty character |

**Questions for a character**
1. What do they want, specifically, and what could they lose?
2. What's their real flaw, one that costs them something?
3. Which three or four levers carry them, and which are deliberately low?
4. When do we first get inside their head, and when do we first see them act?
5. Who loves them, and how do they treat people who can't help them?
6. How could they surprise us and still be themselves?

**In one line:** give them a want, a wound and a way of seeing, let the audience spend time inside it, and never tell anyone to care. Show them why they should.

**How it fits with the others:**
- In the Anticipation theory, care is the first term of the suspense equation.
- In the Gap theory, it's what makes the gaps worth closing.
- It flips the Implied World theory's indifference principle: the world shouldn't care about the hero, but the audience must.

**Where the parts come from:**
- Recognition, alignment and allegiance are Murray Smith's.
- Rooting for the good, and wanting bad things for the bad, comes from Zillmann.
- Parasocial bonds are Horton and Wohl's.
- The sliders are Sanderson's.
- Pity and fear are Aristotle's.
- The round-character test is Forster's.

Blakey Vermeule's *Why Do We Care About Literary Characters?* is the best book-length treatment of the question if you want to go deeper. What's new here is the ladder, the ten levers with where each one breaks, the balancing rule, and the idea that alignment beats virtue as the central mechanism.
