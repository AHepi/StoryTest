# The Gap theory of narrative

I've called this the **Gap theory of narrative**, and it builds on the Implied World theory from before. Its central claim is that a narrative is a stack of gaps. There's a gap between the real world and the fictional one, between the fictional world's normal and what happens in it, between what happened and what you're told, and between what you know and what the characters know. Worldbuilding, story, plot and narration each manage one of those gaps. Narrative is what the audience experiences as all of them open and close over time.

## Part 1: The theory

**Terms** (people use these words in different ways, so here's how this theory uses them)
- **World:** what's possible, normal and valued. The rules, the places and people, the everyday.
- **Story:** what happens, in the order it happened. Agents with desires cause and suffer change. That includes inner change as well as outer events.
- **Plot:** the order, selection and emphasis in which the audience learns about the story *and* the world.
- **Telling:** who or what delivers the plot (a narrator, a point-of-view character, a camera), with what knowledge and what bias.
- **Narrative:** the whole thing, meaning the audience rebuilding a story in a world from a plot delivered by a teller.

**The five gaps**

| Gap | Between | What it produces | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Departure | Reality and the world | Wonder, strangeness | *Dune*'s economy of water |
| Breach | The world's normal and what happens | Stakes; a reason to tell the story | A rich single man moves into Netherfield |
| Withholding | Story order and plot order | Suspense, curiosity, surprise | "Rosebud" in *Citizen Kane* |
| Perspective | What happened and what the teller says | Irony, unreliability, voice | Stevens in *The Remains of the Day* |
| Knowledge | What the audience knows and what characters know | Dramatic irony, dread | Hitchcock's bomb under the table |

**Seven principles**
1. **Nested inference.** The audience rebuilds the world from what's shown, and the story from the plot. Neither is ever handed over directly.
2. **Breach.** A story is a departure from the world's *own* normal. The world decides what counts as an event, what characters want and what things cost.
3. **Pressure.** A world shows itself best through what it does to people under strain.
4. **Scheduling.** Plot decides when the audience learns each fact about the story and the world. Its tools are suspense, curiosity and surprise.
5. **Promise.** Every open question is either a *mystery*, which the narrative promises to answer, or a *wonder*, which it promises to leave open. Breaking either promise feels like betrayal.
6. **Perspective.** The teller sets the limits on what can be known, and so on what the plot can fairly hold back.
7. **Settlement.** The ending turns a sequence of events into a shape. What the narrative means is how its gaps are settled.

## Part 2: What the theory says

**1. Two inferences, one pattern.**
The Implied World theory said the audience never sees the world, only the surface, and infers the rest. Story works the same way. The Russian Formalists split narrative into *fabula*, the events in the order they happened, and *syuzhet*, the events in the order they're told. Audiences only ever get the syuzhet (plot) and rebuild the fabula (story) in their heads. *Memento* runs its scenes backwards, but nobody leaves the cinema thinking Leonard's life ran backwards. So every narrative runs two reconstructions at once: what the world is like, and what happened in it.

E. M. Forster drew the line differently. "The king died and then the queen died" is a story, he said, while "the queen died of grief" is a plot, because it adds cause. Here, cause belongs to story, because the grief is part of what happened. But Forster's next example points this theory's way: "The queen died, no one knew why, until it was discovered that it was through grief at the death of the king." He called that "a plot with a mystery in it." What changed isn't the events. It's the order in which you learn them.

**2. The world makes story possible, and story tests the world.**
The psychologist Jerome Bruner argued that we tell stories about what breaks from the expected. Put that next to worldbuilding and you get two levels of departure: the world departs from reality, and the story departs from the world. The first makes the world interesting. The second makes something happen.

That's why the world comes first logically, even if not in the writing. It decides what counts as an event. In our world a dragon attack is news; in a world full of dragons it might be weather.

It also decides what people want and what it costs them. *Pride and Prejudice* is rarely called worldbuilding, but its plot runs on one rule of its world. The Bennet estate can only pass to a male heir, so five daughters must marry well or face poverty, and every desire in the book is shaped by that. Realist fiction builds worlds too; it just leans heavily on the audience's defaults.

It works the other way as well. You learn what Gilead is from what it does to Offred, not from a history lesson. Characters are where world and story meet: a character is a desire the world has shaped, chasing something the world resists. A world with no breach is a travel guide. A breach with no world could happen anywhere, and its stakes feel arbitrary because nothing sets the price.

**3. Plot is a schedule of knowledge.**
If story is what happened, plot decides what the audience learns, when, and how strongly it's stressed. The literary theorist Meir Sternberg traced three basic interests to this:
- **Suspense** is a gap about the future: you know what's at stake but not how it ends.
- **Curiosity** is a gap about the past: something happened that you haven't been told.
- **Surprise** is a gap you didn't know was there: you thought you understood and you were wrong.

Hitchcock's illustration shows the difference. A bomb under a table that simply goes off gives the audience fifteen seconds of surprise. Show them the bomb first and you get fifteen minutes of suspense. The story is the same; only the plot changed.

Plot schedules facts about the world as well as the story. Sometimes the biggest reveal is about the world itself, like the Statue of Liberty at the end of *Planet of the Apes*. Sometimes a writer refuses to make it a twist. Ishiguro lets the truth about the students in *Never Let Me Go* come out early and quietly, because the horror he's after is their acceptance, not the reveal.

**4. Mystery and wonder.**
This is where worldbuilding's gaps and plot's gaps meet, and it's the theory's sharpest claim. Every open question carries a promise, and emphasis is what sets it. A gap that the camera lingers on, that characters chase and that keeps coming back is a mystery. A gap mentioned in passing and never pressed is a wonder. Tom Bombadil stays a wonder because the book never makes solving him anyone's goal.

Break the promise one way and you get betrayal: the common complaint about *Lost* is that it raised questions insistently and then didn't answer them. Break it the other way and you get deflation: the midi-chlorians explained a Force that worked because it was unexplained. J. J. Abrams once praised the "mystery box," the unopened box as pure possibility. The theory's amendment: an unopened box is fine, as long as you never promised to open it.

**5. The teller decides what can be known.**
A plot always arrives through someone or something, and that limits what can fairly be held back. A narrator who hides what they know is cheating, unless the hiding is part of who they are. That's why the narrator reveal in *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd* is still argued over a century later, and why Stevens in *The Remains of the Day* works: his evasions *are* the story.

The two media lean different ways. Film's natural gap is between audience and character: the camera can show a bomb the characters can't see, and editing can cut between people who don't know about each other. Prose's natural gap is between the telling and the truth: voice, inner life, and the slippage between what a narrator says and what we suspect. So film tends to excel at suspense and dramatic irony, and prose at the irony of voice.

**6. Endings turn sequence into shape.**
Kierkegaard observed that life is lived forwards but understood backwards. Narrative lets you do both at once. Peter Brooks called reading an "anticipation of retrospection": we read forwards so that we can look back from the ending and see the shape. In this theory, meaning is how the gaps get settled: which mysteries close, which wonders stay open, how the world's normal differs at the end from the start, and what that cost. That's also where theme lives. A theme is the story's answer to the question its world poses.

**Five questions a writer can ask**
1. What is my world's normal, and what breaks it?
2. Which of my open questions are mysteries and which are wonders, and does my emphasis match?
3. In each major scene, what does the audience know that the characters don't, and the other way round?
4. Is anything held back only because I want a twist, rather than because the teller couldn't or wouldn't say?
5. By the end, what has changed from the opening normal, and what did it cost?

**In one line:** the world sets the normal, the story breaks it, the plot decides when you find out, the teller decides what you're allowed to see, and the ending decides what it all meant.

**Where the parts come from:**
- The fabula/syuzhet split is from the Russian Formalists.
- Breaks from the expected come from Bruner.
- Suspense, curiosity and surprise are Sternberg's.
- The work of endings is from Brooks, and from Paul Ricoeur's idea of "emplotment."

What's new here is treating every layer as the same kind of gap, the two levels of departure, the mystery/wonder promise, and fitting all of it together with the Implied World theory.
