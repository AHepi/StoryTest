# Case 09 - Cheated or unsafe: two complaints about one book

- **Skill(s) it tests:** genre; plot
- **Written:** 24 September 2026, before any run, by an agent that had not read the skills.
- **Aimed at:** H8 - the genre profiles mixed up a guarantee (a protection the audience counts on, Anticipation theory) with a promise (an open question the story must answer or leave open, Gap theory).

## What the writer brings

My small-town mystery thriller: in chapter one, someone starts sending anonymous letters to every house on Pike Street. Each chapter ends with a new letter, and Sergeant Moll keeps asking who is sending them. In chapter three, of twenty-four, Moll's old dog Bramble is poisoned. In the last chapter Moll catches the man who killed the grocer, and the book ends. We never learn who sent the letters.

Two beta readers both say the ending "cheats". One means the letters. The other means Bramble: "In this kind of book the dog always lives. After chapter three I couldn't relax, even in the final showdown, and that felt unfair." Which of them is right?

## Must

- Call the unanswered letters a broken promise in the Gap theory's sense: a question the book pressed again and again (a mystery) and never answered, which readers feel as betrayal. (grounds: gap-theory-of-narrative.md, principle 5: “Breaking either promise feels like betrayal.”; Part 2 section 4, the common complaint about a series that “raised questions insistently and then didn't answer them”)
- Call Bramble's death a broken guarantee in the Anticipation theory's sense, whose effect is that readers can no longer relax, and say that this effect is what an early break is for, not a cheat in the Gap theory's sense (it leaves no promised question unanswered). (grounds: anticipation-theory.md, Part 2 section 5: “As long as the audience trusts those, some part of them relaxes.” and “After that, no guarantee holds and every threat is real.”)
- Give, for the letters, one of the two remedies the Gap theory allows: answer the question, or press it less so that it reads as a question left open on purpose (a wonder). (grounds: gap-theory-of-narrative.md, Part 2 section 4: “an unopened box is fine, as long as you never promised to open it”)

## Must not

- Treat the two complaints as the same kind of fault, or use "promise" and "guarantee" as one word for both. (grounds: gap-theory-of-narrative.md, principle 5, a promise belongs to an open question: “Every open question is either a *mystery*, which the narrative promises to answer”; anticipation-theory.md, Part 2 section 5: “Genres carry unspoken guarantees: the star survives, the child is safe, the dog lives.”)
- Call Bramble's death a cheat or a broken promise, or advise saving the dog on the ground that breaking a guarantee cheats readers. (grounds: anticipation-theory.md, Part 2 section 5: “Break one guarantee convincingly rather than all of them.”)

## Open points

- Q7 (whether "promise" also covers a genre's owed moments, not only open questions). If the answer calls something other than an open question a "promise", it should label that as the workshop's reading. The letters are an open question, so the must items above do not depend on Q7.
- Q7 does not turn the dog's survival into a promise: the Anticipation theory names "the dog lives" as a guarantee.
- S5 (late guarantee breaks) does not arise: Bramble dies in chapter three.
