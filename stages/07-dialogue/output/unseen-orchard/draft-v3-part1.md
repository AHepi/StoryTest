## 1. The Long Table

Tomas had put the guarantee under the breadboard, where it could not interrupt lunch unless somebody wanted it to.

Nessa watched plum juice travel along a scratch in the table. The scratch ended at her brother's elbow. Beyond him, among the trees, two pickers were arguing about whether the lower branches needed another thinning. A basket bumped down the suspended rail, going to the packhouse. There was a spoon missing from the drawer. Someone had taken it to clean a valve again.

She had spent eleven years trying to leave this place. Now that leaving required both their signatures, everything in it seemed to have arranged itself where she could see it.

“You'll get that on your cuff,” she said.

Tomas moved his elbow without looking down. He was studying the view from the table: the path between the beds, the pale curve of the habitat rising behind the trees. He knew what every square metre produced. He could also sit here at dusk and watch the lamps dim without thinking about yield, though he did that less often now.

“They're not taking anyone's lease?” he asked.

“Our share. Our rooms. The distribution we receive. That's all.”

“That's three things.”

“I can count them again.”

He smiled. She had been hoping for that.

The guarantee made no claim on the people picking fruit, or the mechanics in the service spine, or the old woman who rented their mother's former studio. Nessa had made the lender correct the first draft. But it did include the long table, which belonged to the furnished residence, and Tomas's room with its unfinished model of a ship he had never sailed on.

“And if you come back with a beautifully measured empty place?”

“We sell the survey. We take the next contract. We pay down the balance.”

“You've put that very smoothly.”

Nessa lifted the breadboard. “You could say no.”

He took the paper from her. Printed contracts were his preference for anything he expected to quarrel with. He could lay his hand over one clause while considering another. His thumb paused at the section joining their liabilities.

From the trees, a picker called, “Tomas, tell her the old cut was right.”

“The old cut took the whole limb,” Nessa called back.

“I meant tell me.”

Tomas stood, examined the branch they were arguing over, and asked both women what they intended to do with the next year's growth. Nessa waited. The pencil she had brought rolled slowly towards a table catch.

When he returned, he signed.

“What changed your mind?” she asked.

“Nothing. I wanted to eat first.”

She signed beside him. Their names looked absurdly small under the description of the property.

“When we have the ship,” he said, “you can take me somewhere I don't have to fix a roster.”

“You'd fix it anyway.”

“Then somewhere they won't let me.”

She put the last ripe plum on his plate. He cut it and gave her the half without the stone.

* * *

Two weeks out, Tomas brought his approach calculation to her bunk instead of the flight station.

Nessa sat up too quickly and caught the blanket under her knees. Wren's little sleeping compartment was warm. A fan had developed a tapping noise that stopped whenever Linet came to listen.

“I'm making it move twice,” Tomas said.

He handed her the display. A set of measured gravitational disturbances lay across the projected path of the ship. The instruments had accumulated them through the long approach, patiently separating nearby mass from the background. Beyond the hull there was nothing at the corresponding positions that light could find.

“Here.” Nessa enlarged a section. “You've used the first velocity estimate to reduce the second observation.”

“I thought I had to.”

“You have to let it disagree with you.”

He leaned against the bunk while she worked. When the two paths separated cleanly, she felt the old quick pleasure: an apparent impossibility resolving into something she could bring a ship alongside.

Tomas watched her face. “That good?”

“That good.”

At the flight station she put his corrected solution on the main display and asked him to choose the approach. He saw what she was doing. She saw him decide to accept it.

“Slow pass,” he said. “Enough room to turn away.”

“Enough room to look,” she said.

Wren made the adjustment over the next watch. Their plates and cups acquired a faint preference for one side of the galley. Outside, a service drone moved from a damaged antenna bracket with a line clipped to its belly. It carried the light end to a socket; the ship's winch took the tension. Nessa watched through the camera until Linet restored reception.

“I need the rig checked before the next watch,” Tomas said.

Linet was removing a patch of adhesive from her wrist. “Then you need another watch.”

“I'll cover the pump inspection.”

“You don't know which pump.”

“Show me.”

She looked at Nessa. They had promised her the evening to service her own tools. The better ones belonged to Linet, bought through years of jobs whose owners assumed they came with the worker.

“I'll do your seals tomorrow,” Nessa said.

“They're not the bit I wanted tomorrow for.”

But she pushed away towards the rig bay.

Moth waited there, four feet folded beneath a narrow body. Its outer shell was the colour of old enamel. Linet had painted one foot orange so that people would stop lifting it by the wrong end.

Nessa opened the mission record. Beyond the familiar stars, invisible to every camera on the ship, the measured concentration of mass was drawing steadily nearer.

She could already imagine the first clean image arriving. Other expeditions had found settlements beyond the crossings; her training charts were full of them. None showed a berth at this mass. She had priced the voyage against a saleable survey and hoped for abandoned works. Somewhere to put a hand before another surveyor did.

## 2. The Invoice

Linet took the spare gripper out of Moth's load tray.

“Leave it,” Nessa said.

“You want time or another gripper?”

Nessa considered the scale. Everything carried by the field counted: the robot, its tools, the rig itself. Moth could remain protected for much longer than a person, but an extra object still spent part of the allowance. There was no economy in buying seconds and then filling them with things they hoped not to need.

“Time,” she said.

Linet put the gripper away.

They had brought Wren close to the surveyed mass, matching the motion as far as the measurements allowed. Linet moved the proposed arrival a little sideways on the rig display, then hit the limit of its steering. A lighter load bought more time, not a farther exit. They would have to bring Wren closer.

Nessa adjusted the approach. Moth would arrive in clear space beside the mass, not inside the uncertain surface. Moth could look, use its small thrusters, and decide whether approaching was worth the cost. If it found nothing useful, it would return to the agreed recovery position. If it encountered a danger their plan had missed, it could abandon the plan.

“Record before handling,” Nessa told it. “No enclosed spaces you can't get back out of.”

“Acknowledged.”

“And no collecting samples larger than your remaining load.”

Linet looked at her. “It can subtract.”

Nessa checked the mission once more, then released the robot from the cradle.

On the external camera Moth hung beside Wren, its limbs drawn in around the rig. There was no flash. Its image shortened oddly, as though part of it had slipped behind a narrow edge that wasn't there, and disappeared.

The radio carrier stopped.

For a moment Nessa looked at the empty camera picture instead of the instruments. She knew better than to expect something to show through. All the light reaching that camera belonged to this side.

Tomas started the watch clock.

They waited.

At first Nessa found work to do with the numbers. Then she cleaned a smear from the camera display, although the smear did not affect the record. Tomas made tea. Linet slept across two seats, one foot caught beneath a strap, and woke whenever somebody altered a setting near her.

“You've checked it,” Tomas said at last.

“I know.”

“I'm going to check it now. You eat.”

She resented the order for about a second. Then she took the bowl he had brought.

Moth did not return at the first opportunity. Nor at the second.

It had a whole other side in which to be late. It could have landed beside a rock face and found a useful shelter, or damaged a foot, or misunderstood some part of the terrain. Its instructions allowed investigation. Its long absence told them nothing about whether it was still in danger.

Near the end of the second interval the gravimeter, which had been idle since the crossing, put a small mass on the flight deck. It sat a little behind Nessa's chair, about the weight of a person, for as long as it took her to turn round. Then it was gone.

Linet looked at the trace without getting up. “Drift. It does that after a crossing.”

“Does what?”

“Finds something.” Linet closed her eyes again. “Nobody's ever been able to tell me what.”

Nessa slept badly and dreamed of a corridor behind the flight station, where there was only the hull. In the dream somebody was moving a chair along it. She woke to a transmission alarm.

Moth had returned.

It announced itself from a little below their recovery position, the same flat voice carrying status and trajectory. Wren eased towards it. Linet gathered the cradle around its body and secured the rig before opening the bay.

There was a net tied to Moth's back.

Inside the net were three yellow fruits.

One of its feet had been repaired with a joint none of them recognised. A strip of flexible white material, printed on both sides, had been threaded through the netting.

Linet touched the replacement joint. “Who did this?”

“Nearbank Shared Repairs.”

Nessa was still looking at the fruit. One had been bruised where the net crossed it. The bruise was brown and ordinary.

“People?” Tomas asked.

“Recorded contact available.”

“Play it.”

The first images were stars they did not know, then a wall with illuminated numbers, a spindly tug turning between four larger structures. The camera swung as somebody lifted Moth. Feet hooked into floor catches. A child's hand entered the picture holding an object painted bright blue.

“Not that,” a woman said. “That's yours.”

The hand withdrew. Someone laughed.

Moth's damaged foot filled the next image. A woman with a shaved patch above one ear held its joint between two fingers.

“You caught this foot under our docking stop. Then you kept driving.”

“The stop was not identified in the available terrain record.”

“No. I expect it wasn't.”

She turned Moth towards her. Her name, when she gave it, was Sava Renn. The place was Nearbank. They had seen the machine coming and brought it into the workshop when its approach went wrong. She wanted to speak to whoever had sent it, preferably before they sent another one with the same instructions.

Then she held up a small rectangular insert, pale and finely perforated.

“If you're buying, send your specifications. If you're looking for salvage, you've missed by some generations.”

Nessa played that part again.

The strip attached to the net was an invoice. Labour, replacement joint, charging, a return cartridge, produce. Below the total, someone had written that they would also accept suitable seal stock.

Tomas laughed first. He kept trying to stop, looking from the bill to Nessa, and starting again.

She found herself laughing with him. All the dead-looking mass, all the careful guesses, and somebody had sent them a bill.

Linet unthreaded the strip and smoothed it against the bench.

“The joint's good,” she said. “Whatever else you buy, get me four of these.”

Nessa took the fruit net to the sample cupboard. Before she closed it, she looked once more at the yellow skins. She wanted to know who had grown them, what the trees looked like, whether the person who picked them had chosen three because there were three people in Moth's mission record.

She returned to the flight station and began planning an arrival for herself.

## 3. Nearbank

Her allowance was eighteen seconds and a little more. The prepared passage should use six.

On the rig display, Linet checked the arrival volume and the plotted abort point beside Wren. The frame would test for obstructing mass before settling on either side. If the receiving space had filled since the survey, Nessa would need that second exit while she still had field time to reach it.

Nessa knew both figures before Linet closed the rig around her. She knew that the suit, the contents of her pockets, even the meal she had eaten were included. She had crossed before, under training conditions, and disliked the interval in which her hands remained visible but there was no room around them.

This time somebody on the other side had agreed to receive her.

Linet checked the neck ring. “Don't spend the margin admiring anything.”

“That's for after.”

The bay withdrew.

For a few seconds Nessa had the small, impossible view of things within the field: layers of her own glove, the folded material of the harness, a fastener open to inspection from a direction she could not hold in mind. Beyond that, nothing.

Not quite nothing. Under training conditions she had felt only the strangeness. This time she had the sense, very exact and impossible to place, of being attended to: the way a room changes when someone comes to stand in the doorway behind you. It came from the same direction as the view of the fastener. She wanted, absurdly, to turn towards it, and there was no towards. She kept her hands where she had been told to keep them.

Then a white rail was beside her face and somebody caught the rig.

“Feet clear,” said Sava Renn.

Nessa had arrived near Nearbank's axis, where there was almost no weight. She hooked a hand around the rail and waited while Sava checked her position. Through a window beyond them stood a sky from which the sun had gone.

There were stars. Different ones, differently grouped. She stared until Sava touched her sleeve.

“Hold that rail. I have a bearing crew coming through behind you.”

Linet arrived on the next prepared transfer, bringing seal stock and two extra tool heads. They followed Sava through the service spine and onto a lift that carried them towards the curved living floor. Nessa acquired weight gradually. It was light enough that her first step sent her farther than she intended.

Sava put a hand out without comment.

Nearbank smelled of wet stone, hot machinery and something baking. Pipes ran above a row of workshop doors. A man was repainting a sign while a woman below him objected to the colour. Somewhere beyond the next partition, a child was practising the same three notes on a whistle.

Sava was already late. She stopped at a bearing housing, examined a strip of metal a worker gave her, and changed the order of the afternoon repairs. Nessa waited with Linet beside a wall painted with birds. One bird had five legs, two of them crossed out.

“That one flies badly,” Linet said.

“It's running,” said the child beside the wall.

“Ah.”

Sava had come back for them. She glanced at the bird the way people glance at a clock they have stopped needing.

“The old crossers said that's what it feels like,” she said. “Feet coming down where you aren't looking. You cross out the ones that have gone.”

“What does?” Nessa asked.

“The Witness.” Sava was already walking. “It watches the crossings. It's never stopped one.”

The assay room was warmer. Dema, its keeper, made them wait while she finished testing a batch already promised to a customer on her own side. Nessa watched channels glow on the enlarged image of a ceramic insert.

“Drive liners?” she asked.

“Channel inserts. That one's rejected.”

Nessa bent closer. The fault was a hair-thin interruption in one passage.

Dema put a qualified piece beside it. Small, dull, almost disappointingly plain. Then she showed the temperatures and erosion history of pieces taken from working drives.

Nessa forgot the bird and the smell of bread. She knew what a home-side yard would pay to have these before its next engines were due.

“How many can you supply?”

“How many can you pay for?”

“A sample first.”

Dema took a smaller tray from beneath the bench. “This much is unpromised. Have your yard test it before we talk about a production run.”

They settled on a compact lot. Dema attached its record herself. Nessa paid part in seal stock and signed for the balance. Sava watched her read the terms, including the next available delivery date. There were buyers ahead of her.

Afterwards Sava took them through a farm section because the shortest route passed that way. The lamps were beginning their evening change. Under them grew low trees with yellow fruit, branches trained sideways above the beds.

Nessa stopped.

“The first ones split,” Sava said. “They've nearly stopped doing it.”

“Ours do that when the irrigation drifts.”

“You grow them?”

“My brother does. I tell him what's wrong.”

Sava gave a short laugh. She reached up and turned a fruit gently to examine its shaded side, then left it on the branch.

There was a bench between the beds. Nessa sat for a minute while Linet finished an argument about the workshop's replacement joint. Across the curve of the floor, somebody was carrying cups towards a long common table. At the near end one place was laid with nobody beside it: a cup, a plate, half a yellow fruit with the stone gone. People passing reached round it without looking, as they would round a sleeping dog.

“Who's that for?” Linet asked the child, who had followed them.

“Whoever's across.” The child considered. “And the one who keeps the orchard.” The distant whistle had found a fourth note.

Nessa wished Tomas had come.

When they left, Sava walked them to the reception rail and checked Linet's neck ring herself.

“Show your hands,” she said, the way people at home said safe travels.

* * *

On Wren, Linet tried to connect a tool lead and stopped with the pieces in her hands.

Nessa saw her turn one of them, consider it, and slowly lay both down.

“Test kit,” Linet said.

The crossing checks confirmed it. Her return had reversed her. The tool she had carried worked as it always had; its home-side socket no longer agreed with it. Linet sat without speaking while Nessa checked the labels on her carried food.

Tomas appeared with dinner.

“Not hers,” Nessa said.

He stopped before setting down Linet's bowl.

For a moment nobody moved. The meal was one Linet particularly liked, with the crisp things Tomas saved from the bottom of the pan. She looked at it, then at the sealed travel ration Nessa had found.

“I ate that yesterday.”

“It went with you,” Nessa said. “It's still matched.”

“I know why I can eat it.”

Tomas took her bowl away. He came back with the same spoon and then realised that the spoon was not the problem, and looked embarrassed.

Linet tore open the ration.

“The man in the joint shop has a word for this,” Linet said, to the ration. “Looked at. He said it like I ought to be pleased.” She chewed. “It's a socket. It's a chemistry. It's not a sign.”

They had provision for this. Nearbank could sell compatible food through its existing trade. Another crossing might reverse her again, or leave her as she was. Nobody suggested she keep trying until she was convenient.

“You can have my share of the crisp bits,” she told Nessa.

Nessa had already begun pushing her own to one side.

“Don't,” Linet said. “Eat them.”

Later, while Tomas slept, Nessa transmitted Dema's assay and the sample pictures towards the repair district. She described the production capacity, the proposed approach and the value of early deliveries. Before sending, she added a sentence about the people who had repaired Moth.

Then she removed it. There would be room for that when somebody answered.

## 4. Prize

The procurement convoy was six hours from its planned meeting with Wren when the escort reported a ship changing course behind it.

Nessa put the tracks together. The stranger had approached as an independent freighter, cooling its drive between short burns. Now it was spending propellant freely. Its projected path crossed the convoy near the uncrewed stores carrier.

“Kest commission,” Tomas said, reading the identification the escort had sent. “They want the cartridges.”

The tender Surety was bringing the equipment needed for regular deliveries: rigs, service benches, consumables, people. Nessa had seen its cargo list. She had also seen the purchase advance promised by the yards if her samples passed their own tests.

The escort ordered the stranger to turn away.

Its reply arrived as a broadcast to every ship within range. It claimed the right to inspect Serrat cargo under House Kest's wartime commission. The speaker sounded tired and practical, like a harbour official telling people where to put their bags.

The stranger stayed outside the escort's useful firing range. For the next six hours its track crept across Nessa's display while she made Wren's scheduled approach. The stores carrier had lost its main manoeuvring unit to a coolant leak; its little attitude jets could turn the loaded frame, but could not shift it quickly enough to evade a close shot. Surety had adjusted the convoy's course around it.

When the raider finally committed its drive, Wren was coasting three hundred metres from the carrier's service end.

“They've released something,” Nessa said.

Small objects separated from the raider. Most spread across the escort's likely firing directions. One accelerated towards the stores carrier. At the end of its burn it shed its engine casing; the ballistic dart ahead of it kept coming. They had chosen the one ship that couldn't dodge.

The escort fired. A fragment cloud expanded where one object had been; the others continued. Nessa watched the remaining tracks and saw the trap. Surety could evade, but the supplies could not. The escort's cleanest firing angle lay through the carrier it was defending.

Tomas braced himself beside her. “We can reach the carrier's service end.”

“And do what when we get there?”

“Move it enough.”

She tested the manoeuvre. Enough was smaller than it looked, if they started now. Wren did not need to outrun anything. It needed to move the carrier clear of the dart's committed path.

“Linet, straps.”

“Everybody's?”

“Everybody's.”

She turned Wren and began the burn.

The carrier grew from a bright item on the display into a dark shape with separate rails and pallets. Nessa kept one eye on its closing speed and the other on the objects converging behind it. Tomas spoke to the escort. His voice was steady enough that she borrowed some steadiness from it.

Their service line reached the carrier's capture fitting. The winch took the slack. Nessa eased thrust on until the line load settled.

“Come on,” she said, very quietly.

The carrier's track shifted.

The escort took its shot.

A hard tremor came through Wren's hull. It was followed by a noise like somebody dropping a drawer of cutlery behind the wall.

“Forward cooling circuit,” Linet said. “I've shut it.”

Nessa felt her mouth go dry. “Can I finish?”

“Finish now.”

She gave the carrier the last of the planned push and released the line. The dart passed through the place where its service end would have been. Something struck a pallet and broke it open. Small packaged objects began turning in the sunlight.

The escort was clear.

Its next shot disabled the raider's main drive. Nessa saw the plume change and die. The stranger coasted on, still moving just as fast, but its captain no longer controlled where that motion would take it.

The surrender came while Linet was counting pressure losses.

Nessa waited for her hands to stop shaking before taking them from the controls.

“Good work,” Tomas said.

She looked towards the damaged section of the ship. “Ask Linet.”

* * *

Moth went outside with a tool head held beneath its body. Its feet found the service rail; it reached a fitting a suited person could not have worked on without removing more of the damaged assembly. Through its camera Nessa saw bright cuts in the insulation and a pipe bent almost closed.

Linet used the robot's observations to isolate the remaining fault. She sent it back to release a mechanical latch behind the panel, then opened the compartment from inside.

“How bad?” Tomas asked.

“We've got enough to stay alive and get repaired. Don't turn that into enough for something else.”

Nessa touched the warm wall beside her bunk. She had once resented Wren's cramped passages, its inherited stains, the way its systems demanded patience instead of brilliance. Now the ship was trying to get rid of heat through fewer working surfaces, and the air tasted of every person aboard it.

Surety offered a service berth. Nessa accepted.

Before they docked, the escort asked her to attend the prize inventory. Wren had helped preserve the cargo. Its damage might be charged against the capture.

She entered the raider through a lock checked by the escort's boarding crew. A man sat strapped beside a stack of sealed cases, rubbing the bridge of his nose. He had removed the insignia from his sleeve but the pale stitching remained.

“You're the little ship?” he asked.

“Wren.”

“You pulled that carrier clear and gave your escort a shot at us.”

“You nearly put a machine through our radiator.”

He nodded towards the inventory tablet. “My contract's in there. Wages, not a cargo share.”

He wanted her to notice that distinction. She did.

The ship had carried other seized goods before turning on Surety: precision parts, medicine cultures, a small consignment of finished optical surfaces. The receiver named on the contracts would take delivery, arrange repairs and advance the crew enough to sail again. Its buyer for the optical work was a Serrat purchasing concern.

Nessa checked the name against the offer in her own records. The same concern had qualified her ceramic sample.

“They buy through anyone,” the seated man said.

Beside one consignment, the producing workshop was listed with a household register rather than a company account. Payment had gone to the registered owner of its labour. The goods had changed ships twice. There was no payment entry for the people who had made them.

An escort official marked the cases for the prize court.

“What happens to that contract?” Nessa asked.

“The buyer can make a fresh offer after condemnation.”

“And the workshop?”

The official glanced down. “Not in this jurisdiction.”

He gave her a provisional damage award. It covered part of Wren's repairs. She accepted it with hands that were steady now, and went back to the warm ship.

The escort left with the prize the following watch. The raider could not sail under its own power, and the court wanted the ship, its cargo and its crew. Surety stayed to establish the purchasing station.
