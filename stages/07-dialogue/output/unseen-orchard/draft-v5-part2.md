## 5. Surety

Orra Serrat met them in a room with a folded bed behind the worktable. Its restraint straps had been tucked neatly beneath the mattress. A coat lay across the pillow, and there was a child's drawing fixed to the wall beside an old family seal.

The woman herself was shorter than Nessa expected. She wore no jewellery except a narrow ring she kept turning while she read.

“Sit down. I'm nearly finished.”

She was watching a message. On the screen, another woman stood in a workshop with its machinery covered against dust. Her face resembled Orra's, particularly around the mouth.

“We've got thirty-one of them sleeping in the house now,” the woman said. “The children think it's wonderful. Send the bearing stock before you send another apology.”

Orra stopped the recording. Nessa saw the description of a sold property before the screen changed.

“Your sister?” Tomas asked.

“Yes. Her yard was hit. The people got out. Now we pay them until there's somewhere to work.”

Behind her, a live connection opened to a man sitting under a carved lintel. The signal had enough delay that he began speaking over her greeting, and went on for a few words after she interrupted him. Nessa watched him stop.

He offered to pay for the repairs, the forward operation and the worst of the war debts, if his branch could name the expedition's commander. Orra stopped turning the ring.

There was money on offer. Nessa could feel it almost as a physical opening: Wren made sound again, their home no longer the answer to every bad calculation.

Orra declined, and put the refusal in writing while he watched.

“He could have helped,” Nessa said, when the screen was dark.

“He could have taken the expedition.”

Orra placed another document before Tomas. He read its first page, then returned to the heading.

“An appointment?”

“The compact permits an allied guarantor. You know the approach. You've run a working habitat.”

“I haven't commanded an armed party.”

“You will have officers who have.”

“Then appoint one of them.”

“They haven't pledged Vale Orchard.”

For several seconds the only sound was the room's ventilation. Then Tomas asked who would answer for discipline, and for civilian supplies, and what authority Nearbank's own council would keep. His questions became practical. Nessa recognised the change before he signed.

* * *

Surety's service crew opened Wren's damaged panels. Two contract hands came aboard to cover the work the three of them could no longer keep up with. The rig attendant gave his full name twice and the name of his home berth. The night-watch pilot, asked for his, said, “Pilot will do,” and went to look at the flight station as if he had been away from one for some time. The attendant moved Linet's tool case without asking, and she made him move it back.

Nessa spent the afternoon with Orra, buying the repairs. She worked down the offer as she would have worked down a flight plan.

“Direct wages,” she said. “No intermediary who keeps the whole amount.”

Orra amended a clause.

“Inspection rights. For me, not somebody appointed in my place.”

Another amendment.

The exclusive-access provision remained. Nessa had written beside it that local operators must approve the schedule. Orra returned the page with the note struck out.

“The Berth Council has offered reception access. They can make the arrangements.”

“Sava doesn't answer to them.”

“Then we won't purchase Sava's services.”

It was true in the narrow way that mattered to this page. They could hire other carriers.

Tomas came in carrying a request from the orchard manager. A packing motor had failed; the household's next distribution would be smaller unless they approved a replacement at once. Nessa could hear the basket rail in her mind, the little bump where the track joined above the table.

She authorised the replacement. Then she signed the access agreement.

Orra released the repair payment before Nessa had finished checking the final copy. In Surety's navigation room, Nessa's survey opened on a second display, and then on a third. Beyond them, the route she had found was already being divided into arrival windows.

“You got good terms,” Tomas said.

“Some of them.”

He put an arm around her shoulders, an awkward gesture in the cramped doorway. She leaned into him for a moment.

## 6. The Receiving List

Dema placed two papers on the assay bench. One certified the ceramic lot. The other objected to the transfer of her daughter.

The adjudicator moved the second paper aside. “We'll finish the delivery first.”

“They're the same delivery.”

The hearing occupied half the assay room; the other half stayed at work behind a glass partition. Nessa sat beside the shipment purchaser. Near the door stood two berth guards with Nearbank's blue shoulder bands. One had helped carry Nessa's sample crate on her first visit. The guards now carried two sets of instructions.

The adjudicator consulted the register. Dema's mother's name was linked to a transport household; so was her grandmother's. Beside the names lay an old balance, carried forward through offices that no longer existed.

Her daughter waited outside the partition with a bag at her feet. Rin was perhaps twenty, with the same stillness around her mouth. She had a place on an independent workshop crew. The transfer order sent her instead to an establishment supplying Serrat's war yards.

“Rin has a contract,” Dema said. “She accepted it before your recognition. Sava witnessed it.”

“A person under a continuing service claim cannot make that commitment without consent.”

Dema turned the contract towards him. Beside Sava's signature, and beside Rin's, there was a short slanted stroke, the kind Nessa had seen on the delivery records and taken for a clerk's habit.

The adjudicator wrote *transport descent* beside Sava's name and crossed out the witness signature.

“There were two witnesses,” Dema said.

He looked at the page again. “I see one.”

“Yes,” said Dema.

Nobody in the room laughed, and nobody explained. One of the guards by the door had put his hand flat on his own chest, and took it away when he noticed.

“She's free,” Dema said. “Her parents were free.”

“That does not qualify her as a witness.”

“You accepted her berth guarantee,” Nessa said.

“This is testimony.” He pushed the contract back to Dema.

“Whose consent did she need to be born?”

The adjudicator waited until the room was quiet again. He held his pen above the lot acceptance. Nessa could withhold her signature. Without it, the purchase advance would wait, the next shipment would wait, and the repair credit secured against her guarantee would stay unsettled.

“The wages in our agreement apply to everybody working this lot,” she said.

“They do,” said the purchaser.

“And the transfer accommodation will be inspected.”

“By your office.”

Dema was looking directly at her now.

Nessa signed. As the pen lifted she had the feeling of the crossing field without the field: the room very slightly too close, as if it had been opened from a side and someone were looking in at all of it together, the bench and the trays and her hand and the girl beyond the partition, with nothing in front of anything else. Then the official called the next name, and the room was a room.

Dema picked up her rejected objection and left her certification on the bench. “You won't receive another from me.”

Outside, Rin had shouldered her bag. She made for the reception passage with a departure token in her hand. The familiar guard stepped across her path. He did not raise his weapon. He put two fingers on the token, pushed it down, and told her the independent departure had been cancelled.

* * *

The soldiers came in groups small enough to fit the rigs and large enough to alter the rooms they arrived in. First there were uniforms beside the reception rail; then field beds in an unused classroom, and a duty roster on the passage Nessa used to reach the farm section.

Tomas learned people's names. When a soldier knocked a worker's meal from her hands during a search, he made the man clean the floor and gave her his own unopened ration. Nessa found him later beneath a water-service panel, holding a light for a Nearbank mechanic. By evening the hammering in the pipes had stopped. He had split the elbow of his shirt.

“You'd think I'd know how wide a door was,” he said.

She brought him a clean one and sat across from him, mending the old one while he finished his list.

“Sava has carriers free,” she said. “She can take the dependent households and their stores. The receiving agreements are on record. Linet checked the capacities.”

“That's not the question.”

Nessa pulled the needle through.

“They're not going to disappear,” she said.

“They disappear from our responsibility. Then someone withholds work, somebody else seizes their stores, and I'm sent to recover them.” He rubbed his eyes. “We have eighty people here who need regular supply and owners threatening to withdraw accommodation. I can't run this on whoever happens to like us today.”

She saw the names on his list, arranged by household, with work assignments beside them. Permission to depart depended on those assignments being completed.

“You said temporary,” she said.

“Until the situation is stable.”

He reached for the shirt. She had not finished it.

At the dispatch station the two alternatives waited on adjacent displays. Sava's carrier could take the first household that afternoon. Tomas's order kept them in their rooms and supplied them there. Nessa checked the independent route again. There was nothing wrong with it.

She selected Tomas's list.

An attendant asked her to confirm the cancelled departure. She did. The carrier left with empty places in its manifest. That evening the household's food arrived exactly when she had promised, and Nessa received the delivery confirmation while she was returning Tomas's repaired shirt.

## 7. The Room That Wasn't There

They were packing the meal away before anybody had finished eating.

Nessa entered the common room carrying Dema's rejected production schedule. Linet followed with a box of repaired instruments. On the long table stood dishes with serving spoons still in them, half-cut fruit, a cup painted with a lopsided blue animal set at the empty place. People moved around these things with an urgency they were trying to keep quiet.

Sava had a tug manifest open against the wall. “The assay bench first. Then the spare bearing cages. People in the remaining places.”

Dema put a covered pan down hard enough to make its lid jump. “The household from the end rooms. Their doors are on the next inspection list.”

A tug operator took a cargo token off Sava's board and held it in her fist. “I'm not leaving them for it.”

Nobody glanced towards Nessa. Nobody asked her to decide. Sava studied the fuel figures, moved the instruments to a later run and sent a message to another crew. Someone else would have to cover the approach her tug had been meant to defend. The operator opened her hand and returned the token.

Nessa set down the schedule. It no longer seemed a useful thing to be carrying.

“How long have you been doing this?” she asked.

Sava was already lifting a crate. “Longer than you've been inspecting us.”

A low alarm sounded from the passage. The room stilled. Then a woman at the far table began getting the children into suits.

Sava was looking at the service wall. “Away from there.”

The instrument at her wrist showed a mass growing where the wall appeared empty. Nothing moved on its painted surface. Nessa could still see the five-legged bird. She recognised the place before the machine appeared: her own interior survey had marked the recess behind that wall as clear service space.

There was a brief confusion of shape. A narrow black body caught the edge of the recess and unfolded a manipulator into the room. Sava swung a repair clamp across its first joint and two workers hauled it closed. The machine drove against it, feet scrabbling. A second body came through nearer the ceiling.

“Back passage,” somebody shouted.

Over the local channel came Tomas's voice, relayed by a soldier somewhere else in the drum. “Hold the exits. No firing into occupied rooms. The instruments and named personnel are to be recovered intact.”

A bowl rolled free and broke. Sava and the workers held the first machine while the last children were passed into the service passage. Nessa's suit caught on a bracket. By the time she freed it Linet was beyond the next bend with Dema and the instrument box, and the overhead machine dropped between them.

She backed into the recess beside the farm door.

Her rig had one ready cartridge, kept for the return to Wren. The emergency bypass had been checked: out of Nearbank, a few metres past the wall, back into the service passage. Its abort point lay in clear vacuum on Wren's side. Eighteen seconds; less with the tool pack she was carrying.

She let the pack go. It fell slowly in Nearbank's light weight.

The machine turned towards her. Nessa activated the rig.

Blackness took the room. She drew her hands in and waited for the passage to acquire surfaces around her.

The return mark changed to red.

OBSTRUCTED.

The frame did what it had been built to do. It held her while it turned for the abort, and the holding took longer than any crossing she had made.

She saw herself.

Not as a mirror shows you. All of her, at once, from the side: the seams of the suit and what was under the seams; the folded harness; the pocket with the pencil in it; the meal she had eaten; the knot under her breastbone that had been there since the assay room. Nothing was in front of anything else. And the looking did not come from her. It came from beside her, from the direction with no name, as close as breath and entirely without hurry. It was not unkind. It was not anything. It saw the pencil and the knot with the same attention, and it did not reach in.

She wanted it to. That was the worst of it. She wanted, with a want that had no floor, for it to put out a hand and move one thing, any one thing, and it did not, and it went on looking.

She found she was listening, too, and did not know for what until she caught herself at it: waiting, as if in a moment it would tell her where to put her hands. It said nothing.

The abort mark opened. Something bright struck her visor.

The sun.

She was in space. Her boots found nothing. There was no drum, no passage, no painted wall. The familiar stars stood around the sun, horribly clear. She had repinned on Wren's side.

Her suit held pressure. She checked that twice, because the other thing she wanted to check, the room she had left, was not there to check.

She was moving. The floor had been carrying her around Nearbank's axis, and she had kept that motion when the floor vanished. Wren slid across her visor, a few hundred metres away and changing position much faster than she liked.

“Wren. Nessa. Wrong side. Suit intact.”

The night-watch pilot answered. He sounded startled, then precise. “I have your beacon. Keep clear of your rig thrusters unless I ask.”

She wanted to turn herself. Instead she kept her hands where he could see them, though he could not, at that distance, see them at all.

A service drone detached from the hull with a line trailing behind it. It reached her at arm's length and presented the clip. She missed it once.

“Again,” the pilot said.

She caught it and secured it to her harness. The line tightened and the winch took her momentum across her hips and shoulders. For a while she was pulled sideways through sunlight towards a ship that looked too small to receive her.

Inside the lock she could not make her hands release the rail. The pilot waited without touching her.

“Linet,” she said eventually.

“No word.”

Nessa tried the channel they had used in the common room. No carrier, no hiss of a damaged link, nothing to repair. Linet might have been close enough to touch if Nessa had possessed another direction in which to reach.

“The frame held me,” she said. “Before the abort. It held me a long time.”

The pilot looked at her hands on the rail.

“You'll want to go back in,” he said. “Not to Nearbank. In. To have it again.” He said it as he had said *again* to her over the clip. “Don't take a rig to an unlit mass to find out. People do.”

“You did.”

He did not answer that. He said, after a while, that there was a transport hull on an unlit mass three crossings out, called *Patience*, from the years before frames checked for mass, where the receiving bay had filled before its people arrived. They had come through into rock and into the hull's own ribs. “Their hands were where they'd been told to keep them,” he said. “Every one.” He was quiet a moment. “The old ones say it talked, in the Transport. I went to hear it. That's what I found instead.” Then he unclipped her line for her and went back to the flight station.

They waited through a service cycle and then longer. When the returning rig finally announced itself, Nessa almost fell getting to the receiver.

The recording showed Linet seated on a workshop floor, breathing hard. Dema was beside her. Behind them, someone carried the child with the blue cup towards a waiting tug.

“We're out,” Linet said. “Where are you?”

Nessa watched it three times, listening to the question as though Linet might yet hear her answer. Then she opened the rig record. The service passage had failed its final mass check: behind Linet in the later footage lay the second machine, with a table driven beneath it. Her frame had taken the plotted abort. It had kept her out of the wreckage and left her with all the motion of the floor.

## 8. An Exception

Orra had left the chair opposite her empty.

Nessa remained standing. Her harness had marked her hips; sitting brought the pressure back. She had put on ordinary clothes because she did not want to begin looking as though she needed to be looked after.

“Your inspector's access remains valid,” Orra said. “The retrieval order was not directed at you.”

“I noticed who it was directed at.”

“Those instruments were included in an adjudicated workshop claim.”

“There were children in that room.”

“Which is why Tomas forbade indiscriminate fire.”

Nessa pulled the chair back. For a moment she meant to sit, then merely held it.

Orra turned two documents towards her. One exempted Linet from requisition and guaranteed her matched provisions. The other extended protection to Vale's household interest if deliveries resumed on schedule.

“Dema?” Nessa asked.

“A separate claim.”

“Her daughter?”

“The receiving establishment has agreed to inspection.”

“By me.”

“If you wish.”

Nessa remembered Rin holding the cancelled token. She had not looked afraid until the guard pushed it down.

“Tomas said the families could leave once you had the next lot.”

“Tomas cannot release the guarantee behind a delivery he hasn't completed.”

“He gave his word.”

Orra's tiredness showed then, a brief closing of the eyes. “So did I.”

From the corridor came the squeak of a trolley wheel. Nessa waited until it passed. She could feel the offers pulling separate parts of her life into order: Linet supplied, Tomas vindicated, the orchard's rooms still theirs. There would be a way to work from inside it. There was always another inspection.

She let go of the chair.

“I won't sign.”

“Take them with you. Think about it.”

“No.”

Orra drew the papers back. She did not threaten her. She asked whether Wren still required the scheduled service berth, and Nessa said it did.

* * *

Sava received her in a tug's maintenance compartment on Nearbank's side, so small that they stood around the engine housing. Dema rested one hand on a boxed assay instrument. Linet had laid out the plans Nessa brought, then moved them aside when she saw Sava's.

The plan was simple to say. Nearbank's tugs would move all four drums at once, so that every arrival the occupiers had plotted became a bad guess. The machines could find the masses again and come through outside, where the defenders could see them. It would buy minutes. People had spent savings and risked their berths to buy those minutes.

“What do you need from me?” Nessa asked.

“Surety stopped before it restores the route.”

She had expected to offer that. Hearing Sava ask made it different.

“I can get a maintenance docking.”

“Can you hold it?”

“If we prepare for it.”

Dema leaned forward. “Who decides when you leave?”

“The flight decision has to be—”

“Who decides what you've stayed for?”

Nessa stopped. Something in the question turned her, the way the field had, and showed her the side of herself she had kept towards the wall. Sava was watching her and offering no help.

They worked until the lights changed for the next watch. Nessa kept command of Wren; the coalition set the objective. Nobody would become a passenger in a war they had not agreed to join.

“Tomas has twenty at the outstation,” Sava said. “There are places for all twenty on the neutral carrier. The households can use our carriers, or stay. Their choice.”

“He'll want to keep them together.”

“Then he can ask them. Send him a recording. Put the position in it.”

She would have preferred to go herself. But Tomas's post was twenty kilometres from the rendezvous, and he had chosen it to hold a store and the households kept with it. It was not somewhere she could drop into on her way home.

On Wren, they put the decision to the contract hands. The rig attendant declined; he had a parent expecting his earnings and no share in the orchard or the ceramics. Nessa arranged his transfer to a neutral convoy berth and paid him what she owed without asking for an explanation in writing.

The pilot stayed.

“Not for the prize,” he told her.

“There may not be one.”

“That's why I said it.”

Linet waited until the others had gone before coming to the flight station. “You still owe me a day for my tools.”

Nessa looked up, uncertain whether she was joking.

“If we get through this, I mean to collect it.”

“All right.”

Linet sat beside her and began making the list of parts they would need.
