## 5. Surety

Orra Serrat met them in a room with a folded bed behind the worktable. Its restraint straps had been tucked neatly beneath the mattress. A coat lay across the pillow, and there was a child's drawing fixed to the wall beside an old family seal.

The woman herself was shorter than Nessa expected. She wore no jewellery except a narrow ring she kept turning while she read.

“Sit down. I'm nearly finished.”

She was watching a message. On the screen, another woman stood in a workshop with its machinery covered against dust. Her face resembled Orra's, particularly around the mouth.

“We've got thirty-one of them sleeping in the house now,” the woman said. “The children think it's wonderful. Send the bearing stock before you send another apology.”

Orra stopped the recording and examined a payment confirmation. Nessa saw the description of a sold property before the screen changed.

“Your sister?” Tomas asked.

“Yes. Her yard was hit.”

“I'm sorry.”

“The outer sheds. The people got out. Now we pay them until there's somewhere to work.”

She pulled their chairs into place. Behind her, a live connection opened to a man sitting under a carved lintel. The signal had enough delay that he began speaking over her greeting.

“I've considered the revised exposure,” he said. “Tomas, you will appreciate that the command question cannot be separated from—”

“You have my proposal,” Orra said.

The man continued for a few words before her interruption reached him. Nessa watched him stop.

He offered enough branch money to repair Wren, expand the forward service operation and ease the most urgent war obligations. His branch would nominate the expedition commander. Orra would retain her title and the domestic responsibilities attached to it.

“Those responsibilities include the guarantees,” Orra said.

“They belong to the head of the House.”

She stopped turning the ring.

“My father accepted them with command.”

“Your father left us this position.”

Nessa looked at Tomas. He was studying the tabletop. There was money on offer. She could feel it almost as a physical opening: Wren made sound again, their home no longer the immediate answer to every bad calculation.

Orra declined.

The man waited through the delay, then asked her to put the refusal in writing. She did it while he watched. The connection closed.

“He could have helped,” Nessa said.

“He could have taken the expedition.”

“And the workers at your sister's yard?”

“Their wages have been paid.”

Orra placed another document before Tomas. He read its first page, then returned to the heading.

“An appointment?”

“The compact permits an allied guarantor. You know the approach, you've managed a working habitat, and your family's interest in a successful supply route is substantial.”

“I haven't commanded an armed party.”

“You will have officers who have.”

“Then appoint one of them.”

“They haven't pledged Vale Orchard.”

For several seconds the only sound was the room's ventilation.

Tomas asked who would answer for discipline. Orra answered that he would, within the charter and her instructions. He asked about civilian supplies, accommodation, the authority of Nearbank's own council. His questions became practical. Nessa recognised the change before he signed.

* * *

Surety's service crew opened Wren's damaged panels. Two contract hands came aboard to cover the work the three of them could no longer keep up with: a night-watch pilot and a rig attendant whose hands were permanently grey around the nails. Linet showed them where everything went. The attendant moved her tool case without asking, and she made him move it back.

Nessa spent the afternoon with Orra, buying the repairs.

At first she worked down the offer as she would have worked down a flight plan. Delivery quantities. Payment dates. Limits on replacement parts. What happened if a qualified batch failed in service. There were real concessions to obtain, and she obtained some.

“Direct wages,” she said. “No payment through an intermediary who can keep the entire amount.”

Orra amended a clause.

“Inspection rights. For me, not somebody appointed in my place.”

Another amendment.

“And the first extension on our guarantee isn't conditional on a military result.”

“It is conditional on deliveries.”

“Accepted deliveries.”

“Yes.”

Through the partition came the intermittent vibration of work in the cartridge shop. A machine stopped, somebody called for a measurement, and it started again. People were already making the agreement possible while they disputed its words.

The exclusive-access provision remained.

Nessa had written beside it that local operators must approve the schedule. Orra returned the page with the note struck out.

“The Berth Council has offered reception access,” she said. “They can make the necessary arrangements.”

“Sava doesn't answer to them.”

“Then we won't purchase Sava's services.”

Nessa considered the sentence. It was true in the narrow way that mattered to this page. They could hire other carriers, use other repair berths. Sava's objections did not prevent all commerce.

“I'll need to remain there often,” she said.

“That's why you've asked for inspection.”

Tomas came in carrying a request from the orchard manager. A packing motor had failed. The household's next distribution would be smaller than projected unless they could approve a replacement at once.

Nessa read the figures. She could hear the basket rail in her mind, the little bump where the track joined above the table.

She authorised the replacement.

Then she signed the access agreement.

Orra released the repair payment before Nessa had finished checking the final copy. On Wren, Linet's contractors began removing the broken radiator section. In Surety's navigation room, Nessa's survey opened on a second display, and then on a third.

Tomas found her watching.

“You got good terms,” he said.

“Some of them.”

He put an arm around her shoulders, an awkward gesture in the cramped doorway. She leaned into him for a moment.

Beyond the displays, the route she had found was already being divided into arrival windows.

## 6. The Receiving List

Dema placed two papers on the assay bench. One certified the ceramic lot. The other objected to the transfer of her daughter.

The adjudicator moved the second paper aside.

“We'll finish the delivery first.”

“They're the same delivery.”

The hearing occupied half the assay room. The other half remained at work. Through a transparent partition, a young man rotated an insert beneath an inspection light. Near the door stood two berth guards wearing Nearbank's blue shoulder bands. One had helped carry Nessa's sample crate on her first visit.

The Berth Council had admitted the Serrat agents. Its fabrication owners had secured advances against new production and recognition of property claims that had been disputed for years. The guards now carried both sets of instructions.

Nessa sat beside the shipment purchaser. Dema stood.

“Your certification is accepted,” the adjudicator said.

“Then you can accept my correction.”

He consulted the register. Dema's mother's name was linked to a transport household; her grandmother's to the same one. Beside the names lay an old balance and a succession of recognitions by offices that no longer existed.

“The objection requires the recognised holder's signature.”

“He ordered it.”

The fabrication owner seated at the other end of the bench folded his hands. He did not look at Dema.

Her daughter waited outside the partition with a bag at her feet. She was grown, perhaps twenty, with the same stillness around her mouth. She had a place on an independent workshop crew elsewhere on Nearbank's side. The transfer order sent her instead to an establishment supplying Serrat's war yards.

“Rin has a contract,” Dema said. “She accepted it before your recognition.”

“A person under a continuing service claim cannot make that commitment without consent.”

“Sava witnessed it. She checked the claim before Rin signed.” Dema turned the contract towards him.

The adjudicator wrote transport descent beside Sava's name and crossed out the witness signature.

“She's free,” Dema said. “Her parents were free.”

“That does not qualify her as a witness.”

“You accepted her berth guarantee,” Nessa said.

“This is testimony.” He pushed the contract back to Dema.

“Whose consent did she need to be born?”

The adjudicator waited until the room was quiet again. He held his pen above the lot acceptance.

Nessa could withhold her signature. Without it, the purchase advance would wait, the next shipment would wait, and the repair credit secured against her guarantee would remain unsettled. She looked through the partition at the qualified pieces in their trays.

“The wages in our agreement apply to everybody working this lot,” she said.

“They do,” said the purchaser.

“And the transfer accommodation will be inspected.”

“By your office.”

Dema was looking directly at her now.

Nessa signed.

The official called the next name. A shipping intermediary, marked with the same descent on his form, received an exemption for his household, granted in recognition of services to the new route. He thanked the fabrication owner. Dema picked up her rejected objection and left her certification on the bench.

“You won't receive another from me,” she said.

Outside, Rin had shouldered her bag. She made for the reception passage with a departure token in her hand. The familiar berth guard stepped across her path.

He did not raise his weapon. He put two fingers on the token, pushed it down, and told her the independent departure had been cancelled.

Nessa stayed by the assay-room door. Dema did not appeal to her again.

* * *

The soldiers came in groups small enough to fit the rigs and large enough to alter the rooms into which they arrived. It took days.

First there were uniforms beside the reception rail. Then weapons secured in lockers, field beds in an unused classroom, a duty roster covering the passage Nessa used to reach the farm section.

Tomas learned people's names. He remembered whose children slept badly through the pressure alarms. When a soldier knocked a worker's meal from her hands during a search, he made the man clean the floor and gave her his own unopened ration.

Nessa saw him later beneath a shared water-service panel, his sleeves rolled back, holding a light for a Nearbank mechanic. By evening the irregular knock in the pipes had stopped.

She brought him a clean shirt. He had split the elbow in his old one.

“You'd think I'd know how wide a door was,” he said.

She sat across from him and began repairing the tear while he finished his list.

“Sava has carriers free,” she said. “She can take the dependent households and their stores.”

“To whose berths?”

“Receiving agreements are on the record. Linet checked the capacities.”

“That's not the question.”

Nessa pulled the needle through.

Sava had shown her the offer that morning: fuel, open berths, cargo allocation, names of crews ready to make the runs. The households could leave together. Food would go with them. It would cost less than supplying the controlled accommodation Tomas was establishing.

“They're not going to disappear,” she said.

“They disappear from our responsibility. Then someone withholds work, somebody else seizes their stores, and I'm sent to recover them.”

“Or they're simply allowed to go.”

He rubbed his eyes. “We have a delivery commitment. We have eighty people here who need regular supply. We have owners threatening to withdraw accommodation because nobody will honour a signed order. I can't run this on whoever happens to like us today.”

She saw the names on his list. They were arranged by household, with work assignments beside them. Permission to depart depended on those assignments being completed.

“You said temporary,” she said.

“Until the situation is stable.”

He reached for the shirt. She had not finished it.

At the dispatch station the two alternatives waited on adjacent displays. Sava's carrier could take the first household that afternoon. Tomas's revised order kept them in their present rooms and supplied them there.

Nessa checked the independent route again. There was nothing wrong with it.

She selected Tomas's list.

An attendant asked her to confirm the cancelled departure. She did. The supply crates were reassigned; the carrier left with empty places in its manifest.

That evening the household's food arrived exactly when she had promised. Nessa received the delivery confirmation while she was returning Tomas's repaired shirt.

## 7. The Room That Wasn't There

They were packing the meal away before anybody had finished eating.

Nessa entered the common room carrying Dema's rejected production schedule. Linet followed with a box of repaired instruments. On the long table stood dishes with serving spoons still in them, half-cut fruit, a cup painted with a lopsided blue animal. People moved around these things with an urgency they were trying to keep quiet.

Sava had a tug manifest open against the wall.

“The assay bench first,” she said. “Then the spare bearing cages. People in the remaining places.”

Dema put a covered pan down hard enough to make its lid jump.

“The household from the end rooms.”

“There's another run.”

“Their doors are on the next inspection list.”

A tug operator took a cargo token off Sava's board and held it in her fist.

Sava looked at her. “We agreed the workshop load.”

“I'm not leaving them for it.”

There was no glance towards Nessa. Nobody asked her to decide.

Sava studied the fuel figures. She changed the order, moved the instruments to a later run and sent a message to another crew. The revised journey would keep the tug out of its planned defensive position. Someone else would have to cover that approach.

The operator opened her hand and returned the token.

Nessa set down the schedule she had brought. It no longer seemed a useful thing to be carrying.

“How long have you been doing this?” she asked.

Sava was already lifting a crate. “Longer than you've been inspecting us.”

A low alarm sounded from the passage.

The room stilled.

Then a woman at the far table began getting the children into suits.

Nessa's first impulse was to look at the door. Sava was looking at the service wall behind her.

“Away from there,” she said.

An instrument at her wrist showed a mass effect growing where the wall appeared empty. Nothing moved on its painted surface. Nessa could still see the five-legged bird.

She recognised the location before the machine appeared. Her own interior survey had marked the recess behind that wall as clear service space.

There was a brief confusion of shape. A narrow black body caught the edge of the recess and unfolded a manipulator into the room.

Sava swung a repair clamp across its first joint. Two workers hauled the clamp closed. The machine drove against it, feet scrabbling without purchase. A second body came through nearer the ceiling.

“Back passage,” somebody shouted.

Nessa followed Linet and Dema towards the packed tables. Over the local channel came Tomas's voice, relayed by a soldier somewhere else in the drum.

“Hold the exits. No firing into occupied rooms. The instruments and named personnel are to be recovered intact.”

The first machine dragged its clamp against a table leg. A bowl rolled free and broke. Sava and the workers held it while the last children were passed into the service passage.

Nessa's suit caught on a projecting bracket. She freed it. Linet was beyond the next bend, helping Dema with the instrument box. The overhead machine dropped between them.

She backed into the recess beside the farm door.

Her rig had a ready cartridge. She had kept it for the return to Wren, which waited at the survey station on their side. The emergency route around this section had been checked: out of Nearbank, a few metres past the wall, then back into the service passage. The longer routes on the display were beyond the frame's steering reach.

Its abort point lay in clear vacuum on Wren's side. She checked the suit pressure and selected the bypass.

Eighteen seconds. Less with the tool pack she was still carrying.

She released the pack. It fell slowly in Nearbank's light weight.

The machine turned towards her.

Nessa activated the rig.

Blackness took the room. She moved through the short prepared interval, hands drawn in, waiting for the receiver passage to acquire surfaces around her.

The return mark changed to red.

OBSTRUCTED.

She had time to see the abort mark open before something bright struck her visor.

The sun.

She was in space.

Her boots found nothing. There was no drum, no passage, no painted wall. The familiar stars stood around the sun, horribly clear. She had repinned on Wren's side.

The field was off. She was no longer spending its allowance. Her suit held pressure. She checked that twice because the other thing she wanted to check—the room she had left—was not there to check.

She was moving.

The floor had been carrying her around Nearbank's axis. She had kept that motion when the floor vanished. Wren slid across her visor, a few hundred metres away and changing position much faster than she liked.

“Wren. Nessa. Wrong side. Suit intact.”

The night-watch pilot answered. He sounded startled, then precise. “I have your beacon. Keep clear of your rig thrusters unless I ask.”

She wanted to turn herself. Instead she kept her hands where he could see them.

He brought Wren towards her path. A service drone detached from the hull with a line trailing behind it. Nessa watched its tiny corrections, trying not to watch how far the ship had moved meanwhile.

The drone reached her at arm's length and presented the clip.

She missed it once.

“Again,” the pilot said.

It held position. She caught the clip and secured it to the suit harness. The line paid out, then tightened. Pressure came across her hips and shoulders as the winch took her momentum. On her wrist display the line load rose past the drone's entire thrust rating. It had already unclipped and moved aside.

For a while she was pulled sideways through sunlight towards a ship that looked too small to receive her.

Inside the lock she could not make her hands release the rail.

The pilot waited without touching her.

“Linet,” she said eventually.

“No word.”

Nessa tried the channel they had used in the common room. No carrier, no hiss of a damaged link, nothing to repair. Linet might have been close enough to touch if Nessa had possessed another direction in which to reach. The radio could not find her.

They waited through a service cycle and then longer.

When the returning rig finally announced itself on their side, Nessa almost fell getting to the receiver.

The recording showed Linet seated on a workshop floor, breathing hard. Dema was beside her. Behind them, someone carried the child with the blue cup towards a waiting tug.

“We're out,” Linet said. “Where are you?”

Nessa sat down before the message ended. She watched it three times, listening to the question each time as though Linet might yet hear her answer.

Then she opened the rig record. The service passage had failed its final mass check. Behind Linet in the later evacuation footage, Nessa could see why: the second machine lay across that narrow space with a table driven beneath it. Her frame had taken the plotted abort. It had kept her out of the wreckage and left her with all the motion of the floor.

## 8. An Exception

Orra had left the chair opposite her empty.

Nessa remained standing. Her suit harness had marked her hips; sitting down brought the pressure back. She had put on ordinary clothes for the meeting because she did not want to begin it looking as though she needed to be looked after.

“Your inspector's access remains valid,” Orra said. “The retrieval order was not directed at you.”

“I noticed who it was directed at.”

“Those instruments were included in an adjudicated workshop claim.”

“There were children in that room.”

“Which is why Tomas forbade indiscriminate fire.”

Nessa pulled the empty chair back. For a moment she meant to sit, then merely held it.

Orra turned a document towards her. It exempted Linet from requisition of technical services during the emergency and guaranteed her access to compatible provisions. A second sheet extended protection to Vale's household interest if deliveries resumed on the proposed schedule.

“I have authority to make these exceptions,” Orra said.

“Dema?”

“A separate claim.”

“Her daughter?”

“The receiving establishment has agreed to inspection.”

“By me.”

“If you wish.”

Nessa remembered Rin holding the cancelled departure token. She had not looked afraid until the guard pushed it down.

“Tomas said the families could leave once you had the next lot.”

“Tomas cannot release the guarantee behind a delivery he hasn't completed.”

“He gave his word.”

Orra's tiredness showed then, a brief closing of the eyes. “So did I.”

From the corridor came the squeak of a trolley wheel. Nessa waited until it passed. She could feel the offers before her pulling separate parts of her life into order: Linet supplied, Tomas vindicated, the orchard's rooms still theirs. There would be a way to work from inside it. There was always another inspection, another condition she could ask for.

She let go of the chair.

“I won't sign.”

“Take them with you. Think about it.”

“No.”

Orra drew the papers back. She did not threaten her. She asked whether Wren still required the scheduled service berth, and Nessa said it did.

* * *

Sava received her in a tug's maintenance compartment, on Nearbank's side. There was barely space for all of them around the engine housing. Dema stood with one hand resting on a boxed assay instrument. Linet had laid out the plans Nessa brought, then moved them aside when she saw Sava's.

The local operation was already divided among crews. Fuel had been transferred. Households had moved. Workshop staff had removed portable tools from buildings their owners intended to surrender to Serrat.

Sava pointed to the four drum tracks.

“We move these. We take back the reception axes. Their old room arrivals become bad guesses.”

“They can find the masses again,” Nessa said.

“Yes.”

“And come through outside.”

“Where we can see them.”

It would buy minutes. People had spent savings and risked their berths to buy those minutes. Nessa followed the fuel commitments down the page and found the tug that had taken the extra household. It would still be away when the operation began. Another crew had agreed to defend its approach with less cover.

“What do you need from me?” she asked.

“Surety stopped before it restores the route.”

She had expected to offer that. Hearing Sava ask made it different. She looked at Linet, then at Wren's layout on her own display.

“I can get a maintenance docking.”

“Can you hold it?”

“If we prepare for it.”

Dema leaned forward. “Who decides when you leave?”

“The flight decision has to be—”

“Who decides what you've stayed for?”

Nessa stopped. Sava was watching her without help.

They worked until the compartment's lights changed for the next watch. Nessa kept operational control of Wren; the crew would not take blind orders from people who did not know the ship. The coalition set the objective and the conditions for committing its boarders. Nobody would become a passenger in a war they had not agreed to join.

“Tomas has twenty at the outstation,” Sava said. “There are places for all twenty on the neutral carrier. No one is being left without transport. The households can use our carriers, or stay. Their choice.”

“He'll want to keep them together.”

“Then he can ask them.”

“I'll speak to him.”

“Send a recording. Put the position in it.”

She would have preferred to go herself. But Tomas was now at a post twenty kilometres from the local rendezvous, and every journey occupied a vessel and a crossing cycle. He had chosen that position to control a store and the households held with it. It was not somewhere she could drop into on her way home.

On Wren, they put the decision to the contract hands. The rig attendant declined. He had a parent expecting his earnings and no share in the orchard or the ceramics. Nessa arranged his transfer to a neutral convoy berth before the operation. She paid what she owed him without requiring an explanation in writing.

The night-watch pilot stayed.

“Not for the prize,” he told her.

“There may not be one.”

“That's why I said it.”

Linet waited until the others had gone before approaching the flight station.

“You still owe me a day for my tools,” she said.

Nessa looked up, uncertain whether she was joking.

“If we get through this, I mean to collect it.”

“All right.”

Linet sat beside her and began making the list of parts they would need.
