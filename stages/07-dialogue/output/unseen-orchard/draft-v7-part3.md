## 9. Maintenance

The twelve boarders arrived over two days, with their equipment divided into loads small enough for Wren's two rigs. Nessa learned their faces through the little reception camera before she learned all their names.

They were tug hands, workshop guards and people who had done military service elsewhere. One woman had a weapon case tied with the same yellow cord as the packing crates in Sava's workshop. A man carefully counted out his matched food before permitting the empty rig to return.

The man unclipped the frame from his suit and passed it to Linet, and it went back empty for the next traveller. Nessa watched the repeated handovers produce an unfamiliar company in her small ship.

By the second evening people slept against every available wall.

Linet made them rehearse the maintenance entrance. Wren's pressure hood would fit around Surety's service vestibule, giving the first party shelter and a way back. The powered spreader would hold a damaged or resisted opening while they worked through it. Linet put a boarder inside the practice hood and closed its access until his breathing became audible over the channel.

“If the inner side's still held, you haven't arrived,” she said. “Don't send everyone after the first pair.”

Moth went through the exterior service sleeve with its narrow tool head. It had performed the same latch work on Wren's damaged panel. Linet made it withdraw and repeat the movement from the opposite orientation.

They marked the two portable frames A and B in large letters, for carrying news once the boarders were across. No radio would reach from the wrong side.

Linet timed the changeovers. Each arrival left the frame needing four minutes to cool and be checked before its next departure. After the final practice load they sent B back to Nearbank and left it ready at the relay. A would bring the burn report. B would wait there for the next message.

Surety had eight more rigs, in the shop they were trying to capture. Beyond it lay the bridge service junction. During Wren's repair Nessa had watched a tender engineer isolate the drive from there while people worked outside. The bridge could demand thrust; it could not override a physical safety cut.

She marked the narrow approach on the rehearsal plan.

“They'll defend this once they know where we're going.”

The woman with the yellow cord moved two names from the shop party to the junction party. “Then we don't stop at the racks.”

The escort was still towing the disabled prize towards the court, hours away even if it turned at once. Nessa sent its position to Sava with the plan.

Nessa recorded her message to Tomas after the rehearsal. She gave him the withdrawal offer first, including the households he would have to release. Then she told him she would be at the ordinary maintenance position and asked him to send a reply.

She nearly added that she could still take him home. The word still would have made a promise she no longer knew how to keep.

His reply came the next morning.

He was seated against a bare storage wall at the outstation. Someone offscreen handed him a cup. He thanked them before looking back at the recorder.

“I won't abandon the post with these people here,” he said. “I saw the carrier lists. You've put the troops on one and left the households to choose among the others. Who answers for them after that?”

Nessa could see the collar of the shirt she had repaired.

“The transfer rigs went back with the last supply cycle. We have suits, stores and the people we were sent to guard. Don't ask me to walk away from them.”

She paused the recording.

Twenty kilometres. On a habitat map it looked like a little distance. Wren could cover it; they had prepared a course when the outstation was established. Their small drones could not make the rendezvous in anything like the same time, even before carrying a person became the question.

She finished watching. Tomas asked about the orchard motor. It was the last thing he said.

* * *

That night Nessa could not sleep, and found the pilot at the flight station with the gravimeter log open. It was not the survey. It was Wren's own interior: a list of times, each with a small mass and a place on the ship where nothing stood.

“Linet calls it drift,” Nessa said.

“Linet's right. It's drift.” He did not close the list. “I keep the times.”

She read down it. Most meant nothing to her. One was the night of Moth's crossing, a little behind her chair. Another was a little behind her chair again, on the day of the attack on Nearbank, at the minute her frame had held her. She had not been on the ship.

She had the feeling he had stopped asking what it meant, and kept the list as some people keep a light on in a room nobody uses.

* * *

The maintenance appointment was genuine. Surety's forward service collar had begun loading unevenly under repeated docking, and Nessa retained a right to inspect the machinery governing her deliveries. She brought Linet, the correct spare fittings and a ship crowded with people who kept away from the inspection camera.

The collar officer asked for her personnel return. Three crew, she had written. No passengers.

“Your rig attendant?”

“Transferred to the neutral berth. The receipt is attached.”

He checked it. Then he made the three named crew appear on the cabin camera and required her to confirm the declaration aloud. Behind the closed galley door, twelve people waited without speaking.

“No other people aboard,” Nessa said.

She heard herself say it, and waited for the galley door to open from its side. It stayed a door.

The officer recorded her answer. Linet was to present the fittings at the vestibule camera before he opened the inner hatch. Surety cleared their approach.

Nessa settled Wren against the collar. The docking contact came through the deck as a soft, complicated knock. Linet extended the hood and brought its pressure up. Beyond it the service vestibule waited empty.

On their own side of space there was no sign of the tug burns.

Nessa looked at the gravimeter solution. The masses were moving, though the old fit tried to keep them where they had been. Someone on Surety would see that too. The invasion would not remain blind while they argued about it.

The courier rig appeared near the agreed position and transmitted its record.

Sava's face filled the display. The message was already old.

“First and third axes held. Second contesting. Fourth moving. The outside approach is covered, but the replacement crew has taken damage. Go.”

Nessa acknowledged receipt on their local circuit. Rig A was brought in for its checks and return. B was still waiting on Nearbank's side. She started the service clock.

Tomas's post remained on the far edge of her display.

“Linet.”

“Hood good.”

“Grapples to hold.”

The ordinary docking indicators changed. Wren had ceased offering Surety an easy separation.

Linet pushed the fittings trolley through the hood and turned its labelled face to the vestibule camera. The officer checked it against the job sheet. The inner hatch opened for the trolley and the service crew he expected behind it.

The boarders moved past Linet. Nessa watched the woman with the yellow cord check the next person's seal before moving into the vestibule.

Surety called to ask why the attachment had been locked.

Nobody on Wren answered quickly enough for it to remain a misunderstanding.

## 10. Twenty-four Minutes

The collar officer saw the first armed stranger on the vestibule camera. The inner hatch began closing. Before the fourth boarder reached it, the gap was almost gone.

He caught the narrowing gap with his tool, braced both feet and was forced backwards. Linet brought the spreader up. Its jaws entered where his tool had been, and the hatch shuddered against them.

“Pressure on it,” she said. “Keep out of the hinge line.”

Two people retreated into the hood. Others stayed crouched inside the vestibule, which was not yet a room they controlled. Beyond the obstructed hatch, Surety's defenders moved behind the next partition.

Orra called on the ship channel.

“Release your attachment. Withdraw your people. I will receive their surrender.”

Nessa kept her hand beside the control that would disengage the grapples.

“You have people in Nearbank who need the same offer.”

“Their orders include it.”

Surety's drive began to build thrust.

The joined ships moved together, but the attachment had not been designed for the loads Orra was now putting across it. A gauge on Wren's display passed its normal range. Linet adjusted the spreader's support before the angle changed enough to twist its jaws free.

Nessa countered with Wren's drive, easing the worst of the load. She could hold the opening for now. She could not make it harmless.

On the vestibule camera, a shot struck the partition beside the forward boarder. A fragment caught his upper arm; a patch on his suit sealed dark around it. The sound came over his microphone, painfully loud. He folded himself behind the hatch edge. The woman with the yellow cord returned fire through the gap while another pulled the injured man and his equipment back into the hood. One of the waiting boarders moved up to take his place.

“We can cross it,” the night pilot said.

He was looking at the portable rig that had just returned.

“A is hot,” Linet said. “B is still on Nearbank.”

Nessa enlarged the view beyond the hatch. Even a single person appearing there would arrive between defenders with no protected space behind them. Her inspection survey showed the shop three compartments farther in, beyond the portable frame's steering reach even empty. The nearer service recesses were occupied by racks or too small for a suited arrival. The clear strip at the hatch was the one place they could reach, and Surety's defenders had their weapons on it.

One transfer might put somebody across. It would not get the whole party into the ship before the mechanical breach could be finished.

“Stay on the latch,” she said.

Moth moved through the outside service sleeve. Its camera showed metal close on both sides, with a bar of sunlight behind it. The tool head reached towards the release that the powered hatch could not withdraw from its own side.

“Six minutes,” Linet said. “If that housing hasn't shifted.”

B arrived a little over a minute after A. Its record reached the flight station while the frame was being brought in for service.

It was Tomas.

He had used a Nearbank radio to send the message to the relay operator; the frame had brought it across. Its timestamp was less than a minute old.

“Nessa, I need Wren at the outstation.”

Behind him, a ceiling light swung on a loosened fitting. Somebody was talking too loudly beyond the camera.

“They've given us twenty-five minutes. We put down arms and release everyone, or they come in. I can't hold the store and the accommodation together.”

He rubbed his palm against his knee.

“The officers have agreed to surrender the post. They'll release the households. The local terms let the others withdraw, but they've named me to be held for a hearing.”

He looked towards the door.

“Their guard is one of the men I kept in those rooms. I don't know what happens once he has me. I'm asking you to take me. Just me. I'll get a transfer point cleared.”

He glanced away, then back.

“Please.”

Nessa stopped the playback. The word remained in the room after his face had gone.

He had refused to leave without his people. Now he would leave the surrender to the officers he had brought here. She wanted him aboard.

The rescue course was still stored. She opened it. Fifteen minutes to make the rendezvous, accelerating and braking rather than simply flying past him. There were roughly twenty-four left before the post surrendered and the withdrawal window closed.

“How long to pull everyone back and release?” she asked.

“Two minutes if they can cover the retreat,” Linet said. “I can't promise who gets back.”

A's remaining service would finish during those two minutes. They could take it ready to depart. At the outstation the empty frame would cross to Tomas carrying its return cartridge and check kit. Then it would need four minutes there, cooling while he loaded and checked it. Those four could not be spent on the flight. The two crossings themselves would take seconds.

Two, fifteen, four. A little over twenty-one minutes. She could reach him and bring him back before his post surrendered, if nothing else went wrong.

For a moment the rest of the displays lost their meaning. She saw a course she knew how to fly, a place she could reach and her brother waiting at its end.

Then the spreader load alarm sounded.

“Still holding,” Linet said.

The vestibule camera showed the two boarders in the hood. One was pressing a gloved hand against the side of his helmet as though trying to hear better. The woman at the hatch had stopped firing. She was waiting for Moth to release the latch.

“Six?” Nessa asked.

“No less.”

Six before they could get through, fifteen in flight, four with the empty frame cooling beside Tomas: more than twenty-five, even if Wren could separate the moment the hatch opened. Waiting would cost his chance to leave before the surrender. He could still put down his weapon. She could not promise what would happen to him then.

If she withdrew now, they would have to pull the boarders back through the one space keeping them alive, release the spreader and abandon the attack. Some might not make the withdrawal before Orra's defenders reached them. The tugs on the other side would still be burning their fuel, committed to the interval they had asked Wren to hold.

The night pilot brought up the alternative drone course and then closed it. He had seen the same result she had.

“I can fly the rescue,” he said. “If that's the order.”

Nessa's fingers rested on the edge of the display. She thought of Tomas asking where the orchard workers' leases went in the guarantee. Of him carrying tea to the flight station when she could no longer read the numbers. Of the sleeve she had mended around a list of people he would not let leave.

She wanted his hand on her shoulder. She wanted, stupidly, to ask him what to do.

And then, quite clearly, as if he were standing behind her, she heard him say it.

Go.

It did not say where.

For a moment she had all of them in view at once: Tomas at the outstation, the woman at the hatch, the man with the patch gone dark on his arm, the tug crews burning fuel on the far side of everything.

“Keep us here,” she said.

The pilot returned to the attachment loads.

Nessa opened a recording for Rig A. It took her two attempts to begin.

“Tom. I can't bring Wren. We're holding the entrance and the people in it. There isn't time for both.”

She swallowed.

“Take the surrender. Let the families out first. The delegates have put the terms on record. I'm sending this to them too.”

She almost said he would be safe. Instead she said, “Please take it.”

A's service clock still had two minutes to run. She put the recording in its queue.

On Moth's camera, the latch housing shifted into view. The robot set its repaired foot against a rib, changed the angle of its tool and began to turn.

A check caught a damaged contact cover. The pilot replaced it while Nessa held the attachment loads steady. In the vestibule the woman with the yellow cord changed places with another boarder. Linet eased the spreader a fraction, then restored its pressure. The housing was moving.

The two minutes passed. A's indicator cleared and the frame carried the message away. B's checks were still running.

Nobody asked Nessa to play Tomas's message again.

## 11. The Shop

Four more minutes passed at the latch.

Moth's tool slipped once.

Linet told it to withdraw, clear the head and try from the other side. Nessa watched a fleck of broken coating turn slowly between the camera and the fitting. Then the tool engaged.

“Released,” Moth reported.

The hatch moved far enough for the forward boarder to get his shoulder through. The woman with the yellow cord followed. Their cameras jolted, separated, found cover on opposite sides of an interior cabinet.

There was shouting inside Surety. A short burst of shots struck something Nessa could not see. Then the first boarder turned his camera towards the passage and signalled the others forward.

Linet eased the spreader.

The hatch stayed open.

“Foothold,” said the woman. “Bring them through.”

The people in the hood moved. The injured man came last, one arm over Linet's shoulder. Moth was still returning along the outside sleeve.

Surety changed its thrust.

The movement pulled Wren across the loaded collar. Nessa answered, but the first grapple was already at its limit. She could release it now. On the hood camera, Linet and the man were halfway through the exposed span.

“Keep moving,” Nessa said.

The failure struck through the ship as a deep metal crack.

Her display lost two sensors at once. The night pilot caught himself against the console. Beyond the outer camera, one of Wren's support struts had changed shape.

“Back off the damaged side,” he said.

She eased the drive. The remaining attachment held long enough for Linet to drag the man past the inner hatch. Moth's return light cleared.

Nessa released the grapples, retracted the empty hood and drew Wren away from the collar. The boarders closed the hatch behind them. They had a room they could hold without her pressure supply.

Warm air flowed past her ankle from a duct that had not previously opened into the cabin. She shut the section and watched the pressure stabilise. Wren's outline on the display was acquiring too many missing parts.

Linet's tool case lay on the vestibule floor beside her. She picked it up after the medic took the man from her.

“Don't lose that,” Nessa said over the channel.

“I know what it cost.”

The cartridge shop was three compartments beyond the vestibule. Nessa knew it from inspection: service racks, cooling fixtures, clean tables, the bins of consumables that turned individual passages into a working route. She had watched occupiers arrive there, one small load after another.

Now its workers were against the far wall with their hands visible. The boarders secured the entrances while two Nearbank operators and Linet checked the equipment. One service rack had been damaged. The others remained usable.

“Don't stop the cooling,” Linet told someone. “Those are still hot.”

A tender mechanic pointed out the damaged rack's feed. “You need to close that before it drains the others.”

One of the Nearbank operators took the panel. The mechanic watched her set the valves, then stepped back.

“I'm done. I want off this watch.”

Linet looked at the operator, who nodded. The guard let the mechanic collect her bag and go with the other civilians to the cleared mess.

The bridge still belonged to Orra.

At the service junction, two defenders fired along the narrow approach Nessa had marked. The boarders drew back behind a cabinet. The woman with the yellow cord called to the shop; an operator came out with a wheeled shielding panel, and they pushed it ahead of them until they could work at the junction without standing in the firing line.

One defender retreated towards the bridge. The other put his weapon down when the panel reached his cover.

The operator opened the junction. Nessa recognised the red handles from the repair watch. He called each label to Linet before pulling it. Drive authority first, then the remote door and power links. He left the local cooling circuits live.

The bridge could retain its people or fight to recover the junction. It could no longer command the whole tender from a closed room.

Surety's thrust fell away.

For several seconds Nessa heard only breathing on the channels.

Then Orra asked who was authorised to receive her terms.

* * *

When the boarders had checked the lock, the pilot brought Wren alongside it. Nessa crossed into Surety and entered the procurement room with a boarder beside her. The bed was still folded behind the table. Orra's coat had fallen to the floor during the manoeuvre. She picked it up before sitting down.

“My crew's treatment first,” she said.

The Nearbank delegates were on their own side. There would be no live conference. Nessa recorded Orra's demands, sent them with the next available frame and waited for the people who could actually authorise the withdrawal.

Orra wanted her personnel returned, civilian suppliers protected and the seizure recognised as temporary. She did not withdraw her House's property claims.

Nessa could promise none of those things by herself.

While they waited, a record arrived from the reception axes. One remained damaged; all four were now held by local crews. Occupation groups had begun surrendering when expected support failed to arrive. Others were still bargaining from store rooms and service stations. Their fallback orders had sent machines through clear vacuum approaches, but the defenders could see those approaches and meet them.

Tomas was not in the report.

Nessa checked its time. His interval had passed.

She put the display down very carefully.

Orra watched her. “Is your brother accounted for?”

“Not yet.”

“I ordered the officers to accept a recorded surrender agreement if support was cut.”

Nessa looked at her.

“Before the operation,” Orra said. “I haven't been able to give them new orders since you took the shop.”

The next return carried the delegates' terms, signed by four people the drums' emergency meetings had chosen. Households held under the charter would be released. Captives accused of particular acts would remain for local hearings.

The reception crews had taken the Berth Council's departure controls. Its guards were handing over the tokens that had kept the household doors locked. Sava's attached report named two workshops whose guards still refused. Crews had gone to their doors.

Nearbank operators and tender workers who agreed to stay would run the captured ship. The mechanic who had left her watch put her name down to leave.

Orra argued over the wording. She obtained provisions for medical care and access to records. She did not obtain her ship.

The record after that came from the outstation.

Tomas stood without his weapon beside a local guard. The storage doors behind him were open. People were walking out carrying bags, bedding, a cooking pot wrapped in a coat. He looked exhausted and angry and alive.

Nessa covered her mouth with her hand.

He had released the households before the deadline. His party had surrendered. He was being taken to Nearbank pending a hearing.

Later, Dema sent a message of her own. Rin had recovered her bag from the locked transfer store. The independent workshop contract she had accepted was still waiting for her; its carrier would take her on the next run. Dema needed the assay instruments brought back into service, and she had attached a list of missing parts.

Nessa forwarded the list to Linet.

Then she went back to Wren. The damaged strut lay along the hull like something awkwardly folded. Inside, the pilot had made the surviving cabin safe and set two cups where they would stay put.

After a while he said, without looking at her, that he had found an orchard once, out past Patience. People who had come back reversed had planted it, a long time ago. The fruit came right for nobody, either side. They had sat down under the trees.

“Nobody was keeping it,” he said. “It wasn't the one.”

She did not ask which one.

She sat beside him until the tea had cooled enough to drink.

## 12. A Place at the Table

The room where Tomas waited had been a small office. Somebody had removed its work terminal and left the pale rectangle on the desk where it had kept the surface clean.

He stood when Nessa entered. For a moment she thought he would embrace her. Then he noticed the guard remaining beside the door and sat down again.

“You're walking badly,” he said.

“Harness.”

“Have they looked at it?”

“Yes.”

She put the things she had brought on the desk: clean clothes, his spectacles from Wren, a sealed packet of food cleared for him. He picked up the spectacles first. One arm had come loose. She had forgotten to fix it.

“Sorry,” she said.

“It's a screw.”

He set them down.

Outside the room, people were arguing about moving a filing cabinet. Nessa listened to one voice insist it would fit if they turned it, and another say it was already turned. She wished the interruption would last longer.

“I watched the message,” Tomas said.

She nodded.

“You had time to come.”

“To come there. Not to do both.”

“I wasn't asking you to do both.”

He had always made room for the part of her life that happened elsewhere. He had learned enough navigation to ask useful questions about her work, though he would rather have been building the model in his room. She could remember dozens of occasions on which he had said go, I'll finish this.

“I wanted you to surrender,” she said.

“I know what you wanted.”

She looked at the loose spectacles. “I didn't know you'd be all right.”

His expression changed, but not towards kindness.

“Neither did I.”

They sat without speaking. The people outside got the cabinet through the doorway and cheered once, embarrassed by their own noise.

“The families got out,” Nessa said.

“Yes.”

“Did anyone from your party—”

“They're alive. Two need treatment. I've given the names.”

He asked, without looking up, about the pilot who had flown for her.

“The guard says a pilot with no name has usually been out to the unlit masses.” He turned the spectacles over. “Yours doesn't give one.”

“No.”

“Where did he go? Before.”

She looked at the loose arm of the spectacles.

“He doesn't say.”

“Ask him for me.”

She said she would.

He pushed the food packet closer to his folded clothes. The small acceptance hurt more than refusing it would have.

“I'm not ready to make you feel better about this,” he said.

“All right.”

When her time was nearly over, she asked if she could bring a screwdriver on the next visit. He looked at the spectacles and said yes.

* * *

The guarantee notice reached them through the ordinary home-side network, days after the surrender.

Nessa read it aboard Surety because Wren's communications were still being repaired. The lender had called the joint pledge. Vale's controlling orchard share and the family's residence rights were to be sold. The workers' separate leases continued under their existing terms.

The orchard manager had added a message asking where to send the contents of their rooms.

Nessa began a list. Tomas's model, packed without removing its rigging. The notebooks in their mother's studio cupboard, if the tenant was willing to let somebody retrieve them. The drawer of photographs beneath the table linen. She could not remember everything in her own room. That troubled her until she reached the request about the long table and had to stop.

Linet found her there later.

“Can it be dismantled?” she asked.

“The table?”

“You're looking at its measurements.”

“It belongs with the rooms.”

Linet stayed beside her while she sent the list.

* * *

Rin's first message from her new workshop was mostly about an ill-positioned washbasin. Dema played it while Nessa waited for a decision on the next survey schedule. At the end, Rin turned the camera towards a bunk she had arranged with a yellow cover and asked for a plant cutting if anyone was travelling that way.

Dema saved the message, then returned to Nessa's proposed work.

“No.”

Nessa drew the display towards her. “The approach is good through that interval.”

“And the receiving crew is working our existing shipment through it.”

“We'd only need—”

“You'd need the operators, a serviced frame and people waiting on this side. Put those in the schedule.”

Nessa checked the timings. Her preferred departure would require someone else's work to move. She had left that part blank because she hoped they could arrange it later.

She moved the survey to the following interval.

Dema examined the revision. Sava, seated at the other end of the table, asked the rig operator whether the return allocation was now workable. He changed one more time and passed it back.

Nessa accepted it.

Her navigation contract was for a fixed term, renewable by the operators. A separate notice required her at the hearings. She had supplied the lot acceptance she signed, the cancelled household departure and the access agreement, with the original messages still attached. One household had already asked to question her.

She had asked whether the hearing date could move around the survey. It could not. She entered it beside the revised flight interval.

Wren would need extensive repair before it could earn anything independently. She had skills to sell and liabilities whose totals she was still assembling. It was less than the future she had signed for at the orchard.

Beyond the reception window, Nearbank's stars shone over the dark curve of a drum. She had not yet learned all their patterns. On the next survey they would pass another unlit concentration in the measurements, far beyond this encounter. It might be rock. She wanted to find out.

Someone brought food to the table. A serving spoon was passed around the displays; work was shifted just far enough to make room. Linet set her separately packed meal beside Nessa's plate and took a seat.

Moth came through the open doorway carrying a small replacement fitting. One repaired foot made a faint, uneven click on the deck. It stopped beside Linet and waited while she cleared a place on the bench.

Nessa picked up a yellow fruit, tested its ripeness with her thumb and cut it in two.

She turned the half without the stone towards the place across from her. Then she set it on a small plate beside her own and began to eat the other.
