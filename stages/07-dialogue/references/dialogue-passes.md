# Dialogue passes

*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Story (his three tracks of dialogue, written as separate passes in his scene-writing exercise), and McKee, Dialogue (the questions he puts to every character in a scene, in the last chapter of the book), pointed to rather than restated; the dialogue skill's frame, which rests on what the four owner theories say about talk.*

**What is already in the skills, and not repeated here.** The dialogue skill's Building procedure (steps 1 to 7) is the method. Its modules already hold talk as action, whom a line is aimed at, the said, unsaid and unsayable, subtext, and exposition (`.claude/skills/dialogue/references/what-dialogue-does.md`); voice, what a character would never say, shared background, and key words (`.claude/skills/dialogue/references/voice.md`); beats, turns, the layers of an exchange, the pivot word, economy and silence (`.claude/skills/dialogue/references/line-and-scene-design.md`); the flaws and their fixes (`.claude/skills/dialogue/references/flaws-and-fixes.md`). This file adds the order of the passes (section 1), when to put the scene's questions and to whom (section 2), and a pointer for what insiders leave unsaid (section 3).

Contents: 1 The passes. 2 The scene's questions. 3 What insiders leave unsaid. 4 What the output holds.

## 1. The passes

The three layers of an exchange are in `line-and-scene-design.md`, section 6, under Truby's names for them (story, moral, key words). His scene-writing exercise has them written as separate passes, in that order, rather than all at once *(built: each pass has one job, and the second and third can only be judged against the first)*. On stage 06's draft, where each beat already has its move:
1. **Carry out each move.** Give each beat the words that do what its move says, and nothing more.
2. **Let values collide where the moral line falls.** Where stage 02's moral line, or a clash of values, falls in this scene, let the characters dispute what is right, and only where someone has something riding on the outcome (otherwise it is a lecture: `.claude/skills/plot/references/premise-and-theme.md`, section 6).
3. **Place the returning words.** Put in the words and lines that stage 03's symbols and the cast's key words call for, each coming back in a changed situation (`voice.md`, section 7).
4. **Test each voice alone.** Read each character's lines by themselves: could any of them be handed to someone else? (`voice.md`.)

## 2. The scene's questions

McKee ends his book with a list of questions to put to every character in a scene, and asks for them to be answered before the talk is written, while it is being written, and after *(built: each pass changes what the others found)*. Use his list from the book. Most of what it asks is already in the dialogue skill's Building steps 1 to 6 and on stage 06's scene card, which is where the workshop records the answers.

What the workshop adds is proportion. For a scene the story turns on, ask the questions for each character in it, not only the one who drives it, and ask again once the talk is written. For a small scene, one pass with the driver is enough. How far to go is the dialogue skill's call (its Building step 7, on using `hard-to-vary` in proportion). Note, per scene, any answer that changed the scene.

## 3. What insiders leave unsaid

People who share a great deal say less to each other, and that is already in the dialogue skill: `voice.md`, section 4, "Shared background", with its link to the owner theories. How much each group in this story shares was decided with the world, in stage 03 (`stages/03-world-and-symbols/references/world-and-symbols-worksheet.md`, section 2). Keep to it. Where the audience must understand something the insiders would never spell out to each other, look first for someone in the scene with a reason to ask.

## 4. What the output holds

`draft-vN.md` (the next version after stage 06's; or one file per act, episode or chapter), with the talk written, each scene keeping its card and markers, and a note per scene of the questions whose answers changed the scene, if any.
