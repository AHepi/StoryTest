# Premise worksheet

*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Story (his chapter on the premise, and the exercise that follows it for finding the seven key steps), pointed to rather than restated; the Gap theory (principles 2 Breach and 7 Settlement; writer's questions 1 and 5) as the frame; the Fourth Direction season project's practice (a brief, then rival pitches), as recorded on branch `claude/story-questioning-theme-ehokf0`. What is not credited to one of these is the workshop's own reading, and says so.*

**What is already in the skills, and not repeated here.** The premise line, the claim, the question the world poses and the designing principle: `.claude/skills/plot/references/premise-and-theme.md`, sections 1 to 5. Naming an idea's own problems early, and keeping one spine: the same file, section 2. A real final choice, between two goods: the same file, section 6, and who learns what, when, in section 7. Whose story to tell, the most interesting character or the one we are aligned with: the last rival in `.claude/skills/character/references/building-a-cast.md`, section 12, with its test in section 11. The seven jobs: `.claude/skills/plot/references/structure-and-conflict.md`, section 2. A genre's promise and question: the `genre` skill. This file adds the order of work for this stage, the brief, the card each rival fills, and a few pointers into Truby's premise chapter where the skills have nothing.

Contents: 1 The writer's own ground. 2 The brief. 3 Rival premises, and the card. 4 The change at the centre. 5 A first guess at the final choice. 6 Who it is for. 7 From a premise to the seven jobs. 8 Choosing.

## 1. The writer's own ground

Truby begins his premise work with two personal inventories, which he calls the *wish list* and the *premise list* (his premise chapter, step 1), on the grounds that a premise drawn from what the writer already cares about is less likely to come out generic *(asserted)*. Use them as he describes them when a writer is starting from nothing.

In this workshop the step is usually short: `_config/writer.md` already holds the writer's standing tastes, and a request tends to arrive with its own material (a document, a world, an earlier draft). Read those, note what keeps coming back in them (a kind of person, a kind of story, a subject, a voice), and write it at the top of the brief. Ask the writer only for what is missing.

*The workshop's link to the owner theories, not the theories' own claim:* the Implied World theory says a *world* that rides on its genre's defaults feels generic, because it spends almost nothing on departures (Part 2, section 1). The workshop reads a premise the same way: one assembled only from defaults gives the audience little it did not bring.

## 2. The brief

The brief is what every later stage, and every helper, starts from. It holds, in this order:
1. **The request, in the writer's words,** with everything attached, and whether each attachment may be committed.
2. **What is asked, and what is not,** and the nearest other reading, as said back to the writer (the line on taking a request in `CLAUDE.md`).
3. **Rules for this project:** every "must" and "never" the writer has given, numbered, so later files can cite them (`brief-v1 rule 3`).
4. **What to avoid:** earlier work it must not echo, and why. (The season's second brief listed what *The Catch* and the first attempt had done, so that neither was repeated.)
5. **Standing preferences that apply,** from `_config/writer.md`, and any that do not apply here, with the reason. An answer still marked *to confirm* is used only as a stopgap, and said to be one (stage 01, step 1).
6. **The writer's own ground** (section 1).
7. **Open questions,** each with the stopgap reading the run will use until the writer answers.

A brief is a version like any output: a new brief after the writer's feedback is `brief-v2.md`, and the first is kept.

## 3. Rival premises, and the card

**Why rivals.** An idea can be taken in many directions, and the first that comes to mind is seldom the one worth the most work (Truby makes the same point in his premise chapter, step 2). The season project gives a reason to write the rivals apart and from different starting points: its first attempt started two of its three pitches from one seed idea, which its own log says steered the result towards *The Catch*; its second attempt therefore started each of three pitches from a different kind of story (crime, coming-of-age, detective) and had them reviewed independently. As of 26 September 2026 the result is mixed: the owner chose the first pitch, *Seconds*, but the originality review found all three reaching for the same source material and still echoing the dropped first attempt, two of them in its ending (the season folder's files 10 to 12, on branch `claude/story-questioning-theme-ehokf0`). So starting rivals from different kinds of story is a design the workshop follows, not one shown to keep them apart. Two ways to find directions: ask what an audience would expect this idea to give them, and whether that is worth giving; and ask "what if" down several different paths.

**The card.** Each rival fills the same card, so that they can be compared line by line. The order is the workshop's, following the Gap theory: the world's normal first, then what breaks it, then whose story it is and what the telling does with it.

| Line | What to write | Where the craft is |
|---|---|---|
| Normal and breach | what is normal in this world, and what breaks it | Gap theory, writer's question 1 |
| The world's question | the question the story's world poses | `premise-and-theme.md`, section 4 |
| Premise line | the story in one sentence | `premise-and-theme.md`, section 2 |
| Whose story | the hero, and why this one | `building-a-cast.md`, section 12 (its last rival) and section 11 |
| Designing principle | the deeper process, and the fresh form it takes | `premise-and-theme.md`, section 5 |
| Genre | the genre, blend or subgenre, and what it promises | `genre` skill, Building steps 1 to 4; the genre file's section 10 for subgenres and blends |

| The idea's own problems | what this story must pull off, and the problems it brings | `premise-and-theme.md`, section 2 |

When the brief allows one genre only, "a different kind of story" for each rival means a different subgenre, or a different blend with that genre in the lead (the genre file's section 10; `.claude/skills/genre/references/how-genres-work.md`, section 8).

## 4. The change at the centre

Truby's premise chapter, step 8, gives a method for deriving the hero's starting weakness and their changed self from the one action at the middle of the story (his letters W, A and C). Use it from the book when it is present (`stages/how-stages-work.md`, section 1). Without the book, the invented case below is the model: one action at the story's middle, and the hero before and after it. What the workshop adds is that case and a check.

*An invented case.* The action: a lighthouse keeper must shelter a wrecked ship's crew through a winter. One reading: at the start a man who has arranged his life so that he needs no one; at the end one who asks for help. Another: at the start a man who gives freely but must be thanked for it; at the end one who can give unseen. The same action, two different stories.

**The check.** If the hero at the start would already do the action gladly (a generous man who must shelter a crew), the middle has nothing to push against. Change the action or the start.

**How it stands to the owner theories.** It fills in; it does not decide. The Gap theory measures an ending by how the normal has changed, and what it cost (principle 7, writer's question 5). The hero's change is one such measure, not the only one: a hero can stay the same while the world around them changes (`structure-and-conflict.md`, section 2, and `.claude/skills/character/references/change-and-growth.md`, section 9). So this card line is "change or settlement": where the hero does not change, write what the normal has become instead.

## 5. A first guess at the final choice

Truby asks for the hero's hardest choice at the premise stage, well before the plot exists *(built: a story's meaning tends to gather at that choice, so knowing it early aims the work; he expects it to change)*. Write one or two candidates, each as "A over B", where B is something the hero, and the audience, truly value. The rule for a real choice is already in `premise-and-theme.md`, section 6. What this step adds is its timing.

If no hard choice can be imagined, say which of two things that means. Either the moral line, if there is one, will carry no moral suspense, and then the threat must carry all of it (`premise-and-theme.md`, sections 6 and 7); or the story has no moral line at all, because its world's question is not a moral one (the second rival in `premise-and-theme.md`, section 9). Write down which, since each asks different things of the later stages.

## 6. Who it is for

Truby ends his premise work by asking whether the story would draw anyone beyond the writer's own circle, and advises dropping a premise that would not *(asserted)*. Whether that matters is the writer's aim, not a rule of craft, so the verdict is *held if*: it holds if the writer wants a wide audience (`_config/writer.md`, question 5). When the writer has said a wide audience matters, write for each rival who would come to it and why. When they have not said, give the verdict both ways and ask.

## 7. From a premise to the seven jobs

For the chosen premise only. Truby's exercise after his premise chapter finds the seven key steps from a rough list of the story's events; follow it from the book, with the jobs as `structure-and-conflict.md`, section 2, defines them and the plot skill's Building steps 2 and 3. The workshop adds three things:
- **The events listed are the story in the Gap theory's sense:** what happens, in the order it happened, not yet the order in which the audience learns it. The order of telling is stage 04's.
- **Where the hero does not change,** start from the settlement (what the normal has become, and what it cost), not from a self-revelation.
- **The detail of the flaws and the opponents is left to stage 02.** Here, a line for each job is enough.

## 8. Choosing

The choice is the writer's. Before it:
- **Have the rivals read by someone who did not write them** when `_config/writer.md` says so: stage 08, pointed at the pitches (`critique-pitches-round-1-<kind>.md`).
- **Run the designing principle's test** on each: could the line describe dozens of other stories in the genre? Then it is a label, not a principle (`premise-and-theme.md`, section 5).

Then write `premise-vN.md`: the chosen card, anything borrowed from the others and why, the reasons for the choice (the writer's, quoted), and the seven jobs.
