*Stage 03, run for a draft brought in, so there is no stage 02 cast sheet and no premise card: the draft stands in for both (recorded in the project log, entry 2). This file does not rebuild the world the draft already has. It designs one layer: the spiritual one the writer asked for, its lore and history, and where it meets the plot. ## 0. Research: what lore and spirituality do in a plot

The writer asked for research on lore, spirituality and how they blend with plot. Six ideas, each in the workshop's words, each tied to an owner theory and to a choice below. None is quoted from a book; nothing here is registered as a source, because none of it goes into a skill (to put any of it into a craft skill would go through `add-source` first).

| Idea | Whose | What it says, in short | What it gives this story |
|---|---|---|---|
| The numinous | Rudolf Otto, *The Idea of the Holy* (1917) | Meeting the holy is two feelings at once: dread before something overwhelming (*tremendum*) and a pull towards it (*fascinans*). | The watcher must frighten and draw at once. Nessa should want to be near it in the same moment she wants away. That pull is what sends seekers out. |
| The threshold | Arnold van Gennep, *The Rites of Passage* (1909); Victor Turner, "Betwixt and Between" (1967) | In a rite of passage there is a middle stage where a person has lost their old place and not yet got a new one. In it, ordinary rank falls away and the sacred is near. | The crossing already is a threshold: eighteen seconds where you are neither side. Lore says that is where the watcher is closest. It matches the writer's "border where identity collapses". |
| Seen from a higher direction | Edwin Abbott, *Flatland* (1884); Charles Hinton's essays on the fourth dimension (1880s to 1900s) | A being in one more dimension sees inside everything closed to us, as we see inside a square drawn on paper. It appears among us only as slices that change shape. Turning something through that extra direction gives back its mirror image. | The draft's crossings already work this way: Nessa sees a fastener "from a direction she could not hold in mind", and Linet comes back mirror-reversed. So the watcher is not added to the world; it is what the world's physics already implies someone could be. |
| The pilgrim's road | Andrei Tarkovsky's *Stalker* (1979), after the Strugatskys' *Roadside Picnic* (1972) | People go to a forbidden zone to reach a room that grants your deepest wish; the road is full of horror, and at the threshold no one goes in. | The shape of the writer's "journey that never resolves": the seekers are real, the road is real, the arrival never comes. |
| The silent answer | The Book of Job | A man demands an account of his suffering; the presence that answers gives no account at all. | The watcher sees, and does nothing. That is the spiritual horror at the centre, and it is the plot's moral question turned outward (section 1). |
| Glimpses of a larger history | J. R. R. Tolkien, as the Implied World theory already uses him (Part 2, section 4) | A world feels deep when it glimpses history it never tells in full. | Lore is told in fragments, by people who take it for granted, and most of it stays below the surface. |

**What the research says about blending them with plot.** In each case that works, the sacred is not a second story beside the plot; it is the plot's pressure seen from another side. *Stalker*'s Zone is the characters' despair given a geography; Job's whirlwind is his question refused. So the rule for this revision: every spiritual moment sits on a moment the plot already has, and gives it a second meaning. None adds an event. [from: 03 step 2; implied-world-theory principle 8; brief-v1 rule 1]

## 1. The layer in one line, and its arena

**In one line:** the crossings run through a direction no one can point to, and the people who have crossed for generations say something lives there that sees the inside of everything (rooms, ledgers, pockets, people) and has never once stepped in. [from: 03 step 2; world-from-story §2]

**Why this one.** The story's question, as the draft asks it, is what you owe the people your quiet signatures move but you never see: the pickers whose leases sit under the guarantee, the unnamed workshop on the prize manifest ("no payment entry for the people who had made them"), Rin's cancelled departure, the households on Tomas's list. Nessa's role is *inspector*. She asks for "inspection rights" as her great concession. She sees inside and signs. The watcher is that role made absolute: a witness that sees everything and acts on nothing. The horror of meeting it is recognition. That is the Implied World theory's thematic fit (principle 8): the main new departure carries the story's question. [from: 03 step 2; implied-world-theory principle 8]

**Arena:** unchanged: Vale Orchard, Wren, Nearbank, Surety. The watcher has no place of its own, which is the point: it is beside every place.

## 2. Departures added, each priced

The Implied World theory warns that each departure costs attention (principle 2), and the writer wants the middle lighter, not heavier. So only one new departure, and everything else is ripple from what the draft already shows.

| Departure | Price | Ripple into ordinary life (all from the draft's own material) | Stakes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Witness** (what Nearbank calls the watcher): a presence in the crossing direction that the transport households say sees every crossing. Never seen. Never explained. | One new idea, introduced by people who take it for granted. | See the rituals below; each is a habit the draft already has, given a second reason. | None that changes events. It changes what Nessa's choices cost her inside. |

**The ripple: rituals and habits** [from: 03 step 2; world-materials, rituals]

1. **"Show your hands."** Crossers keep their hands visible in the field. In the draft this is safety training. Nearbank says it at every departure, as people elsewhere say "safe travels": the Witness should see you carry nothing you would hide. (Draft hooks: chapter 3, "She kept her hands where she had been told to keep them"; chapter 7, "she kept her hands where he could see them".)
2. **The side place.** Transport households set one place at a long table for whoever is on the other side: the absent, the dead, and (the old people say) the one who watches. The half of the fruit without the stone goes there. (Draft hooks: the plum halves in chapter 1; the long common table in chapters 3 and 7; the last image of chapter 12.)
3. **The side mark.** Transport households sign with a short slanted stroke beside the name: room left for a second witness. (Draft hook: chapter 6, the adjudicator strikes out Sava's witness signature.)
4. **Turned.** A crosser who comes back mirror-reversed is "turned"; old people say "looked at". Linet is turned in chapter 3. Nearbank treats her with a care she finds embarrassing.
5. **Unaccounted mass.** Gravimeters pick up mass where light finds nothing; that is how Wren found Nearbank. Sometimes a ship's own gravimeter shows a small mass *inside* the ship, for a moment, where there is nothing. Engineers call it drift. Some crossers log the times. (Draft hooks: chapter 1, "measured gravitational disturbances... nothing at the corresponding positions that light could find"; chapter 2, Nessa's dream of "somebody moving a chair" in a corridor that is only hull.) This is the writer's "outwardly almost supernatural": an instrument reading with an ordinary explanation that never quite covers it.

**Rules the story keeps (show the rules, hide the thing).** The Anticipation theory's principle 3: give the threat's rules, hide the thing. The rules: it is felt only at the threshold and at decisions; it is never seen; it never acts; the gravimeter is its only outward trace. Nothing else is ever said about what it is. [from: 03 step 2; anticipation-theory principle 3]

## 3. History (the reserve, mostly unshown)

The reserve: what the workshop knows and the story shows only in glimpses (Implied World principle 6). [from: 03 step 9; implied-world-theory principle 6]

- **The Transport.** About three generations ago, a Serrat-chartered company moved bonded households across, one rig-load at a time, to build the drum that became Nearbank. Their "continuing service claims" are the ones the adjudicator reads in chapter 6. Many arrivals failed: receiving bays filled, or the frames' reach fell short.
- ***Patience*.** One transport hull whose receiving space was obstructed by a mass no survey had found. Its crossers arrived into rock and into the hull's own frame. The hull lies on an unlit mass. Seekers have found it.
- **The lore began with the Transport.** The bonded crossers said that something saw every crossing, every one that failed, and every ledger; and that it had not stopped one. The lore holds both: *it sees; it does not save.* The children's rhyme and the mural come from this time.
- **The orchard on no side.** The oldest story: the Witness keeps an orchard on a side nobody has reached, where the fruit is matched to everyone, turned or not, so that anyone can eat with anyone. It is the only promise in the lore. It is what seekers go to find. The title's second meaning.

## 4. The seekers

The writer's "people lost in a collapsed identity who go looking, find horror, and never resolve". [from: 03 step 2; request; gap-theory principle 5]

- **Who they are.** People whose sense of themselves broke (a debt, a death, an order they carried out) and who cross on purpose towards unlit masses, without a receiving crew, to find the orchard or to be seen properly once. Those who go give up their names for the road; they are called by their trade.
- **The one on the page: the night-watch pilot.** The draft never names him. Now that is why. He went, years ago, after something he does not tell. He found *Patience*, and a settlement of turned crossers who had planted trees that bore fruit nobody on either side could eat, and had starved among them. He once stayed in the field past his allowance and felt himself seen from the side, all of him at once, "like a box with the lid off". He came back. He did not find the orchard. He still logs the gravimeter's drift. He tells Nessa a little of this, twice; never all of it.
- **What it does in the plot.** Nothing new happens; he does what he already does in the draft (pulls Nessa in from the wrong side in chapter 7; stays "not for the prize" in chapter 8; offers to fly the rescue in chapter 10). His past gives those acts their weight: he is the one person aboard who has looked for an answer in the dark and come back without one, so his "That's why I said it" is a seeker's ethic, not a joke.
- **Who might go next.** Tomas, at the end, stripped of the orchard, the command and the self he thought he was, asks Nessa where the pilot went. And Nessa's last wish, to find out what the next unlit mass is, now reads two ways (a survey, or the road). Neither is answered.

**Why it stays a wonder.** The Gap theory: a gap the characters chase becomes a mystery, which the story must then answer. Here the protagonist never chases it; one minor character did, and his telling settles the promise in advance: no one has found it. The lore itself says so. So the story promises to leave the watcher open, and keeps the promise. [from: 03 step 9; gap-theory Part 2 section 4]

## 9. Hard-to-vary tests on the load-bearing choices

[from: 03 step 4; hard-to-vary, the quick version; by-domain §5]

- **The watcher as four-dimensional.** Swap it for an ordinary god or ghost: the mirror reversal, the view "from a direction she could not hold", the gravimeter that sees mass light cannot, all lose their link to it, and it becomes decoration. Remove it: the crossings stay physics, and the story keeps no spiritual layer. *Holds.*
- **It sees and does not act.** Swap for a watcher that helps or punishes: it becomes a mystery to be solved (what does it want?) and stops being Nessa's mirror as inspector. *Holds; this is the hardest-to-vary part.*
- **The pilot as the seeker.** Swap for a new character: adds a person to a middle the writer wants lighter, and loses the explanation of why the pilot is unnamed. Swap for Sava: she has a cause and a people; seekers are people who have lost theirs. *Holds.*
- **The side place at the end.** Remove it: the last image is still a gift to Tomas, and loses the third reader. Swap for any other rite: loses the plum of chapter 1. *Holds.*
- **The rhyme and the mural's origin.** Could be swapped for many others with no loss. *Surplus, on purpose* (Implied World principle 5): kept short.

## 10. Added in version 2: the voice that stopped (from Jaynes)

*Added for the writer's feedback 2: "Pull from Julian Jaynes book The origin of consciousness in the breakdown of the bicameral mind." The book is registered in `sources/README.md`; no copy was supplied, so this works from the book's widely known argument, in the workshop's words. Nothing is quoted. [from: 03 step 2; feedback-2; Jaynes, as registered]*

**The idea, briefly.** Jaynes argued that people of the ancient world did not decide things the way we do. Under the stress of a choice, one half of the brain produced a voice, heard as a god's, a king's or a parent's, and the other half obeyed. When the world grew too strange and too fast for the voices to cope with (migration, catastrophe, trade, writing), they fell silent over a few centuries. What took their place was the inner "I" that tells itself a story of what it is doing and decides. Oracles, idols, prophets, and the long lament for gods who no longer speak were, on his reading, people trying to get the voices back.

**Why it fits this story so closely (hard to vary).**
- *Two halves, and a mirror.* Jaynes's two chambers are the brain's two halves. A crossing that mirror-reverses a person swaps their left and right. So the old crossers' lore can say what Jaynes says, in the world's own terms, with no new physics.
- *The voice came at decisions under stress.* That is exactly where the writer asked for the spiritual element: despair, uncertainty, quiet decisions.
- *The gods went silent.* The Witness already never acts. Now it has a history: it once spoke, and stopped. The Transport (a forced migration into a strange world) is the kind of upheaval Jaynes blames for the silence.
- *The seekers are oracle-seekers.* They are not looking for a place, finally, but for the voice to come back and tell them who to be. That is the writer's "people lost in a collapsed identity", given its reason.
- *Nessa's arc becomes Jaynes's breakdown in one person.* All story long she acts on other people's instructions (Tomas's list, Orra's terms, the lender's draft) and longs, in chapter 10, "stupidly, to ask him what to do". At the climax the old pattern fires one last time, and she has to decide without it.

**The ripple: lore, history, moments.** Each sits on an existing moment; no event is added.

| Ch. | Moment | What is added |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | Sava on the bird | One more line of history: in the Transport, the old crossers said the Witness *spoke*: told them where to put their hands, which way to turn. "It stopped. Nobody knows when." |
| 3 | Linet reversed | The joint-shop man asked whether she *heard* anything on the way back: Nearbank's lore that the reversed, their halves swapped, sometimes hear it. Linet: "Like I'd been to a concert." |
| 3 | The side place | The child's answer stays; nothing added (a Jaynes reader will see the idol's meal; others need not). |
| 7 | The frame holds her | In the silence she listens for an instruction, as if there should be one. None comes. |
| 7 | The pilot in the lock | What he went for was not *Patience*: "The old ones said it talked. I went to hear it." |
| 10 | The decision | The old pattern fires once: she hears Tomas's voice, quite clearly, say "Go", the word he always said (ch. 12, "go, I'll finish this"). She knows he did not say it. It could mean go to him, or go on. She has to choose what it means, and so chooses. The Witness stays silent. |
| 12 | Tomas in custody | His question to Nessa gains one line: what the guard said seekers are listening for. |

**Rules kept.** The Witness is still never seen and now also never heard: the voice in chapter 10 is Tomas's, from inside Nessa, and the story never says the Witness spoke. Its once speaking is lore, told by people, never shown. The voice in chapter 10 must stay ambiguous: a reader can take it as grief and stress, which keeps it "almost supernatural".

**Rival (the writer's call).** A fuller Jaynes reading would let the Witness's voice be heard once, at the bottom of despair (chapter 7), and leave the reader unsure. That buys a sharper supernatural moment, and costs the rule that it is never directly experienced as anything but a presence, and the brief's "never resolve" a little. Not taken; offered.
