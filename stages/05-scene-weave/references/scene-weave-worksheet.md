*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Story (the scene list, and many-stranded television drama, both already held in the plot skill) and The Anatomy of Genres (serial television and moral decisions), in the workshop's own words; the Gap theory (principle 4 Scheduling, principle 5 Promise, Part 2 section 4 on mystery and wonder, writer's question 2) and the Anticipation theory (principle 7 The ratchet) as the frame; the Fourth Direction season project's practice (a plan holding every episode's events).*

## 1. Order of work

1. **From jobs to scenes.** Take stage 04's jobs table and reveal list. Each event becomes one or more scenes, each summed up in a single line by the one thing that happens in it.
2. **Fill the columns** of the scene list (`scenes.md`, section 6): strand, job served, gap changed, knowledge position, tension.
3. **Work the list** as `scenes.md`, section 6, says.
4. **Weave the strands** (section 7 there): what each crosscut is for; read each strand alone.
5. **Chart the tension column** and look for its faults (`suspense-and-fear.md`, section 7).
6. **Opening and closing scenes** (`scenes.md`, section 5).
7. **For a series,** the second level (section 2 below).

## 2. A series: the season and the episode

A season is a story in two sizes at once, and the plan has to show both *(the workshop's reading, from the season project's practice and the sources below)*:
- **Each strand in an episode does its own core jobs:** the rule for many strands in `scenes.md`, section 7.
- **The season does the seven jobs across its episodes.** The season's hero, desire, opponent, plan, final clash and settlement are placed episode by episode, in a season jobs table like stage 04's, with a column for the episode.
- **Each open question belongs to one size, and its emphasis matches.** In the Gap theory's terms, a mystery is a promise to answer and a wonder a promise to leave open, and emphasis is what sets which it is: a question lingered on, chased and brought back is a mystery; one mentioned in passing and never pressed is a wonder (principle 5; Part 2, section 4; writer's question 2). So for every question the plan opens, say whether this episode closes it, this season closes it, a later season will (name where), or it is a wonder. A question the plan presses cannot be filed as a wonder: the theory's own case of that is *Lost*, which pressed questions and left them unanswered, and read as betrayal. A mystery the plan never assigns is a promise that may be broken.
- **Moral decisions across the season.** Truby observes that many long-running serial dramas rest on a world that keeps putting its people to moral decisions, and that this is what lets them run for years *(fitted from a list of shows; asserted as the reason)*. Where the story's question is a moral one, place the steps of stage 02's moral line across episodes, one or two per episode, so the season argues its question through what is done. Where it is not, say so.
- **The ratchet across episodes.** Tension should rise in steps with partial releases (Anticipation principle 7); across a season, the release at an episode's end must not also release the season's larger threat, or the next episode starts from zero.

## 3. What the output holds

`scene-weave-vN.md`, each part with its marker:
1. The scene list, as a table with the columns in `scenes.md`, section 6.
2. The strands, and what each crosscut is for.
3. The tension chart, with its faults found and what was done.
4. For a series: an episode table (episode, strands, the season job each episode does, the questions it opens and where each closes), then one scene list per episode, each in its own file if the plan would pass 100,000 bytes (`stages/how-stages-work.md`, section 4).
