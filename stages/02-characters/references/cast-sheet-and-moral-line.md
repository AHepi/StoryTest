# Cast sheet and moral line

*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Story (his character exercise, which measures the whole cast by the same questions, and his outline of a moral argument carried by the plot), and McKee, Dialogue (his parts of a character's desire), pointed to rather than restated; the Bond theory (the ladder of care; the ten levers; principles 2 Alignment beats virtue and 4 Balance the levers; the limits of care) as the frame; the Gap theory (theme as the settlement's answer to the world's question). What is not credited to one of these is the workshop's own reading, and says so.*

**What is already in the skills, and not repeated here.** Building one character (flaw and need, desire, ghost, values, change): `.claude/skills/character/references/building-a-character.md`. The cast as a whole (jobs in the story, contrast, one question with many answers, how many to keep): `.claude/skills/character/references/building-a-cast.md`. Opponents, including the four-corner opposition and a threat that is not a person: `.claude/skills/character/references/opponents.md`. Change and the self-revelation: `.claude/skills/character/references/change-and-growth.md`. Theme argued through action, and when the meaning shows: `.claude/skills/plot/references/premise-and-theme.md`, sections 6 and 7. This file adds two tools: one sheet on which the whole cast is compared beside the Bond levers (section 1), and a care plan laid against the moral line (section 2).

Contents: 1 The cast sheet. 2 The moral line and its care plan. 3 Order of work.

## 1. The cast sheet

Truby's character exercise compares every character by the same few measures, so that differences show *(built)*; use his list from the book. The workshop's sheet takes that idea, puts the Bond theory's levers beside it, and orders the columns by what the owner theories ask first: what the character says to the story's question, and what builds the audience's relationship with them. One row per major character.

| Column | What to write | Where the craft is |
|---|---|---|
| Their answer | how this character would answer the story's question (from the premise), in one line | `building-a-cast.md`, section 5 |
| Levers | the three or four that carry them, and the ones kept low on purpose | Bond theory, the ten levers; `.claude/skills/character/SKILL.md`, Building step 3 |
| Want | the concrete goal in this story | `building-a-character.md`, section 5 |
| Flaw | the one that harms only them, and the one that harms others | `building-a-character.md`, section 4 |
| Need | the inner change that would free them | `building-a-character.md`, section 4 |
| Values | what they hold good | `building-a-character.md`, section 7 |
| Power | what they can make happen, and to whom | `opponents.md`, section 4 |
| Job | the part they play in the plot | `building-a-cast.md`, section 2 |
| Never says | what they would never say aloud, and why | `.claude/skills/dialogue/references/voice.md`, section 4 (stage 07 uses this column) |

Read the sheet down each column. Two characters with the same answer are doing one job; a blank answer is a character with nothing to say to the story (`building-a-cast.md`, section 5). A column where everyone is alike is contrast the cast has not yet found (`building-a-cast.md`, section 4).

**A threat that is not a person** (a storm, a plague, a voice from nowhere) does not get a row it cannot fill. Follow `opponents.md`, section 9, instead, and do not invent a mind for it to fill the columns.

**The want, and what it is for.** McKee separates a character's *object of desire* from their *super-intention* (his terms; "The Complex of Desire", in his chapter "Story/Scene/Dialogue"). The workshop's check, drawn from that: if the want column could be moved into another story unchanged, it is still too general to drive this one. Make it something only this story could supply. Whether getting the want would really give the character what they need is a question the need column should answer.

## 2. The moral line and its care plan

Truby lays out, step by step, how a story argues its view of right action through what the hero does, from the first act that harms someone to the final choice (his chapter on moral argument, its "basic strategy") *(fitted from many stories)*. Use the steps from the book when it is present (`stages/how-stages-work.md`, section 1). Without the book, write the moral line as the hero's acts that harm someone, in story order, from the first to the final choice, and hold it to `premise-and-theme.md`, sections 6 and 7 (theme argued through deeds, a real final choice, when the meaning shows). That the audience believes deeds over speeches is already in section 6 there. Laid beside the plot's jobs (stage 04 does that), each moral step should happen as an event, not a speech.

**What the owner theories add: a care plan for each act that harms someone.** Every such act puts the audience's allegiance at risk: the Side with rung of the ladder of care, not care itself. An audience that turns against a character still cares what happens to them (Bond principle 6). The Bond theory holds allegiance through a harmful act in two ways:
- **Alignment:** time in the character's company and access to their mind; we side with whoever we spend time with and understand, often whatever they have done (principle 2);
- **Balance:** another lever turned up to make up for the Decency turned down (principle 4).

It also says some acts end allegiance for most audiences anyway (the limits of care). So beside each harmful act on the moral line, write in a small table:

| The act | Who objects, if anyone | The rung the audience should be on | What holds allegiance through it: alignment, a balancing lever, or "meant to lose it here" |
|---|---|---|---|

Planning this act by act, and asking for the balance in the same stretch of story, is the workshop's reading of the theory, not the theory's own wording; the same reading, for the opening, is in `building-a-character.md`, section 4, "The price". The character skill's order for building the Side with rung (time and access first, Decency last) is in `.claude/skills/character/SKILL.md`, "The procedure", Diagnosing, step 3.

**The story's question need not be moral.** The Gap theory reads theme as the settlement's answer to the question the world poses, and that question is not always about how to treat others (`building-a-cast.md`, section 5, and the rivals in `premise-and-theme.md`, section 9). When it is not, the moral line may be thin or absent. Say so on the sheet rather than forcing one.

**A short work** may not have room for every step of the moral line. Whether it may fold steps together is owner question S17. Until it is answered, the stopgap reading is the plot skill's trap "Letting a checklist decide" (`.claude/skills/plot/SKILL.md`, Traps): steps fitted from books are prompts, and a missing step is a question to ask, not a fault to fix. Say which steps are folded or left out, and why.

## 3. Order of work

1. The pair at the centre first, the hero beside the main opponent, then every other major character (*Truby, built*: an opponent is defined by how they attack the hero).
2. The hero's change, worked back from the end (`change-and-growth.md`, section 2), or, if the hero does not change, what the settlement measures instead (section 9 there).
3. How each opponent strikes at the hero's flaw, each in a different way (`opponents.md`, section 3), or, for a threat that is not a person, `opponents.md`, section 9.
4. The moral line with its care plan (section 2).
5. How care is built for each lead, rung by rung (`.claude/skills/character/SKILL.md`, Building step 7); for an opponent the audience is meant to oppose, the rungs point the other way (Diagnosing, step 3; the workshop's reading, owner question S6).
