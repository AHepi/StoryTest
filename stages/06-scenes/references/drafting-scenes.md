# Drafting scenes

*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Story (his advice, in his scene-writing exercise, to draft scenes without dialogue before adding it), and McKee, Dialogue (his five steps of behaviour, and his view that talk comes last), pointed to rather than restated; the Gap theory (principle 4 Scheduling) as the frame. ## 1. Action now, talk in stage 07

Two books arrive at the same order of work from different directions. Truby's scene-writing exercise has the writer draft each scene as behaviour alone before any line is written, then add the talk; McKee holds that talk is the last thing a writer can know about a scene, after what each person wants and does *(both built: a scene whose behaviour works gives the talk something to carry out, while talk drafted first tends to do the behaviour's job)*.

In this workshop the order becomes two stages. This stage drafts each scene as behaviour: who does what, in what place, beat by beat, with each line of talk marked only by what it does to its listener (one *-ing* word: pleading, cornering, stalling), not by its words. Stage 07 writes the words.

*The workshop's link to the owner theories, not the theories' own claim:* this fits the Bond theory's limit that care can't be ordered, since care is built by what the audience sees done, not by talk that announces it. Extending the same point to a scene's stakes and turn is the workshop's own step.

*An invented case.* Two sisters sort their late father's workshop. The draft: the elder boxes tools fast, without looking; the younger takes each one back out and turns it over; the elder stops, holds out the box (offering); the younger puts a chisel in her own coat pocket (claiming); the elder sees, and seals the box (conceding). The turn is on the pocket. No word is written yet, and the scene already has its want, its resistance, its turn and its landing.

## 2. McKee's five steps, divided between two stages

McKee breaks each piece of behaviour into five steps, each with his own name for it (his chapter "Story/Scene/Dialogue"; *asserted as a description of how people act*). Read his account there. The workshop uses them only as a division of labour:
- **This stage writes the first four for each beat,** and each fills a field of the scene card (`scenes.md`, section 2): the desire is the driver's want in this scene; the sense of antagonism is the resistance, as the character reads it; the choice of action is the tactic; the action is the beat as drafted.
- **Stage 07 writes the fifth,** which he calls *expression*: the words.

When a beat will not come right, look in the earlier steps before the words; McKee's own phrase is that "dialogue problems are story problems".

**The value in play.** Write on each scene card the value at stake and its charge at the start and at the end, McKee's terms for what `scenes.md`, section 1, calls the change a scene must make: a scene whose charge ends where it began has not turned.

## 3. What the output holds

`draft-vN.md` (for a long work, one file per act, episode or chapter), with, for each scene in the order of stage 05's list:
1. The scene card (`scenes.md`, section 2), with the value's charge at start and end.
2. The behaviour draft, beat by beat, each beat's talk marked by its move only.
3. The marker for each part.
