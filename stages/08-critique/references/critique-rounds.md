# Critique rounds

*Sources: the Fourth Direction season project's practice (separate critics, each hunting one kind of problem, then a revision log); the Interpretable Context Methodology paper (tracing a fault in an output back to what produced it), in the workshop's own words; Truby, The Anatomy of Story (his closing chapter, on stories that go on working after they end), pointed to rather than restated; the Gap theory (principle 5 Promise; Part 2, sections 4 and 6) as the frame.*

**What is already in the skills, and not repeated here.** How each craft skill diagnoses (its "Diagnosing" procedure: pin the symptom, find the cause, build the rival diagnosis, fix the smallest thing) and how it replies, with the reply check shared by all five; the three false endings, and the rule that an ending should change the opening (`.claude/skills/plot/references/premise-and-theme.md`, section 8); a reversal's two checks (`.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 4); dialogue's flaw families (`.claude/skills/dialogue/references/flaws-and-fixes.md`); the claim check before anything reaches the writer (`.claude/skills/error-correction/SKILL.md`, "Before any claim reaches the owner or a writer"). For a whole work, `story-critique` leads when the writer's account has it. This file adds how a round is set up (section 1), the shape of a finding with its trace (sections 2 and 3), a lens for what the ending does to everything before it (section 4), and a rival (section 5).

## 1. Setting up a round

- **One critic, one kind of problem.** Each critic hunts one kind, so that no kind is crowded out by another. The kinds for a round come from `_config/writer.md` (question 11); the season project's first round used logic and rules; care, suspense and structure; and theme and hard to vary. Others that fit: the brief's rules and originality; dialogue; the ending-backwards lens (section 4).
- **Critics who did not write the work.** A critic is an agent that wrote none of what it criticises, and did not see another critic's report before writing its own.
- **Each critic's brief is its stage contract.** It gets the target, the brief (and the premise, once there is one), the earlier outputs the target rests on, and the craft skill for its kind, and no more. Briefs are checked before they go out, in proportion (the error-correction skill's `references/reviews-and-briefs.md`, section 5).
- **Each file is named by its target and round:** `critique-<target>-round-N-<kind>.md`, where the target is `pitches`, `plan` or `draft` (or a part, such as `draft-ep03`). Rounds are counted per target, so a round on the pitches does not use up the plan's.
- **Rounds are limited.** As many rounds per target as `_config/writer.md` (question 11) allows, then the result goes to the writer, unless the writer asks for more. The season project planned two rounds of criticism; the workshop's own rule for reviewing its changes also stops at two (`.claude/skills/error-correction/references/reviews-and-briefs.md`, section 4), which is used here by analogy.

## 2. A finding

Each finding in a critique is a numbered entry with:
1. **Target:** the file, the passage, and the passage's marker.
2. **What is wrong,** in one line.
3. **Grounds:** the owner theory, reference or earlier output it rests on, cited.
4. **The suggested fix,** concrete to this draft, and what it gives up or puts at risk.
5. **Its standing:** *bears*; *held if* an aim only the writer can give (give it both ways, and ask); or *does not bear yet*, when it needs a fact nobody has given (such as which of two drafts came first).
6. **Where it came from** (section 3).

Before the critique is handed on, run the craft skills' reply check on it: each finding survives the flip test (would the critic be as sure of the opposite?) and the swap test (would it fit any story?).

## 3. Tracing a finding to its source

The paper this layout follows argues that a fault found late is best mended where it began, in whatever the stage was given to work from, rather than in the output alone. The markers make the question answerable. For each finding, read the marker on the target passage and say which of three it is:
- **This output only:** a slip in this run. The revision stage mends the output.
- **An earlier stage's output:** the fault was already in, say, the plot, and the scene only inherited it. The revision stage mends the earlier output, and every later output that read it becomes stale (`stages/how-stages-work.md`, section 7).
- **The workshop itself:** a stage contract or reference produced it, and would produce it again in the next run. The revision stage mends this run's output, and the fault in the workshop goes to the `error-correction` skill as a sign.

## 4. The ending-backwards lens

Truby's closing chapter argues that a story keeps working after it ends when a second encounter finds it changed, and names ways to build for that *(fitted from stories that last)*; read it there. The workshop's critic asks a nearby question from the Gap theory's side. The theory's point is that the ending turns a sequence of events into a shape, read backwards from the end within a single reading (Part 2, section 6); the second viewing is Truby's, and close to it, not the same. The critic's questions, in the workshop's words:
- Does the ending make earlier scenes read differently, and were the signs there to be found? (A reversal's two checks: `reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 4.)
- Which mysteries does the ending close, and does their closing recast what came before?
- Which wonders stay open, never pressed, so that something is left for the audience to go on wondering about?
- What in the opening scene reads differently once the ending is known? (`premise-and-theme.md`, section 8.)

The lens applies with one limit, the next section.

## 5. Rival: leaving the final choice unseen

- **The theory's side.** Gap principle 5: every open question is either a mystery, which the narrative promises to answer, or a wonder, which it promises to leave open, and breaking either promise feels like betrayal. The plot skill's trap: leaving a pressed mystery open and calling it depth is betrayal, not a wonder (`premise-and-theme.md`, section 10).
- **The book's side.** Truby, in the closing chapter of *The Anatomy of Story*, proposes two ways to keep a story alive past its end: leave its moral argument ambiguous, or cut away before the hero's final decision is shown. His reason is that the audience is then made to weigh the hero's actions, and the choice itself, against their own lives *(built: he gives the mechanism)*. He offers it for stories that go beyond a simple contest of good against evil, which the outcome below does.
- **One outcome.** A story presses one question through its last act ("Will she hand her brother over?"), then ends a moment before she answers. Afterwards, does the audience feel cheated?
- **Each side's prediction.** Truby's side: most of the audience does not feel cheated. The theory's side: most of it does, since a promise was broken. Only this outcome is scored: that the audience goes on arguing the choice is predicted by both sides (the theory's own case, *Lost*, was argued over for years and still read as betrayal), so it cannot tell them apart. The two predictions on feeling cheated cannot both hold for most of the same audience. Where the choice was never pressed as a question, both sides agree that leaving it open is fine (it is a wonder), so the test must use a pressed one.
