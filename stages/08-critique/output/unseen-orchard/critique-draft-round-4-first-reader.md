# Critique of the draft, round 4: a first-time reader

Target: *The Unseen Orchard*, draft 6, parts 1 to 3 (`stages/07-dialogue/output/unseen-orchard/draft-v6-part1.md`, `-part2.md`, `-part3.md`), chapters 1 to 12.

Critic: an agent that wrote none of the story and has seen no earlier draft, design note, critique, log or theory. Its one perspective is a first-time reader's.

**How it was read.** Each part was read once, every word, in order, and the reading log below was written from that first pass before the judgement and findings. Then two kinds of lookup were made, and only to check this critique's own claims: a word search of the three parts for where the war, the houses and some lore words first appear (it confirmed that the first mention of a war is in chapter 4), and a re-read of passages quoted below. The form of a finding comes from `stages/08-critique/references/critique-rounds.md`. Section 2 was the part asked for, but I read the whole file, which is short. Two cautions. First, I could not avoid seeing the italic heading at the top of each part. It says the draft was revised for "a spiritual layer, raised by lore and history" and for "Jaynes". That may have made me readier to look for patched-in passages than a reader with no heading would be. I have kept each such finding to what my reading log shows. Second, item 6 of the finding form ("where it came from") is not traced. My brief kept me from the markers and the earlier outputs, so the revision stage must trace each finding.

## 1. Reading log, chapter by chapter

## 2. Judgement of the whole

**Does the middle drag?** No. Chapters 4 to 6 hurry rather than drag. The war, the decision to send soldiers, the "recognition" and the old service claims all happen between scenes, and the reader has to put the politics together from names and contract phrases. My attention dipped twice in the middle, both times over paperwork: the prize inventory in chapter 4, and the list of terms in chapter 5. It came back hard whenever a person stood in front of the paperwork: Rin and the two witnesses in chapter 6, and the shirt. The middle's cost is murk, not slowness.

**Does the strange element feel felt and earned, or explained and bolted on?** Both, and you can tell which is which.
- **Felt.** It is felt in five places, each better than the last: the first crossing (chapter 3), the signing (chapter 6), the long hold (chapter 7), the drift list (chapter 9) and the decision (chapter 10). These never explain. They give a sense ("being attended to", "nothing in front of anything else") and refuse to make it act. "It did not choose. It did not help." That refusal is the point, and it is earned.
- **Explained.** It is explained mostly through people passing on what others said: Sava ("They used to say..."), Linet ("The man in the joint shop has a word for this..."), the pilot twice, and Tomas passing on the guard. The pilot's first telling (*Patience*) is strong. The later ones repeat the same point, that people go out to listen, and the chapter 10 telling is placed where it costs the most.

**Do the lore and history feel like a world with depth, or like a lecture?** Mostly depth, because the history keeps arriving as a present-day fact with a person attached: the slanted stroke on Rin's contract, "transport descent" written beside Sava's name, the laid place, "Show your hands", the five-legged bird. The lecture risk is confined to the passed-on stories noted above.

**Does the ending land?** Yes. The opening is changed by it: the half without the stone, which Tomas gave her, she now gives away. "Go, I'll finish this" became the voice that "did not say where". The long table she spent eleven years trying to leave is lost, "It belongs with the rooms", and she sits at another one. The cost is honest: hearings, a smaller future, Tomas unreconciled but asking for a screwdriver. Two small things blur it: the last image's blocking (finding 3), and the "He doesn't say" exchange (finding 4).

**Which characters do I care about, and when did it start?**
- **Tomas:** from the plum in chapter 1. The care holds through his worst ("named personnel") because chapter 6 shows his decency, with the meal, the panel and the split elbow.
- **Linet:** from her tools in chapter 1, and fully from "Don't. Eat them." in chapter 3. She is the steadiest presence in the book.
- **Nessa:** I respect her from her work in chapter 1. I care about her from chapter 6, when she gets it wrong in silence.
- **The pilot:** from "Again" in chapter 7.
- **Dema:** from chapter 6.
- **Orra:** more than I expected, from her sister's message.
- **Sava:** I trust her more than I know her.
- **Rin:** mostly seen through others, which suits her.

## 3. Findings, ranked by what each costs a first reader

Item 6 of the form (where it came from) is not traced for any finding. My brief kept me from the markers and the earlier outputs, so the revision stage should trace each one.

### Finding 1. The war arrives without warning, and the move from trade to occupation happens off the page
- **Target.** Chapter 4: "It claimed the right to inspect Serrat cargo under House Kest's wartime commission." Chapter 5: "I haven't commanded an armed party." / "You will have officers who have." Chapter 6: "She accepted it before your recognition."
- **What is wrong.** Nessa has surely known all along that her home side is at war, but the reader first hears of it in chapter 4. From there, the reasons for soldiers, and for Serrat's legal hold at Nearbank, arrive as names and contract phrases rather than as things the reader is shown.
- **Grounds.** In my reading log for chapters 4 to 6, my main confusion was who Serrat and Kest are, why a purchasing station needs an armed party, and what "recognition" is. The war word search confirms that nothing in chapters 1 to 3 mentions a war. The story is told closely from Nessa's side, so a fact she must know, held back from the reader, reads as a seam rather than a surprise. A surprise belongs to the character; this one belongs only to the author.
- **Fix.**
  - Give the war one or two quiet lines in chapters 1 to 3. For example, the lender's terms are priced at wartime rates, or a picker's son has gone to the yards. In chapter 3, "She knew what a home-side yard would pay to have these before its next engines were due" could say whose engines, and why they are due.
  - In chapter 5, give Orra one line on what the armed party is for: holding the route against Kest, or holding Nearbank's output.
  - In chapter 6, let one line say what Serrat recognised and what that let it do.
  - Cost: the gentle, small-scale wonder of chapters 1 to 3 gets a shadow earlier, and the jolt of chapter 4 is smaller. Three to five sentences in all.
- **Standing.** Bears. If the writer wants the reader blindsided, the fix should still give Nessa's knowledge to the reader, because the blindside is not hers.
- **Tests.** Flip: I would not be as sure the reader needs no footing. My own log shows two chapters of assembling. Swap: it turns on this story's houses, this convoy and this narrator's knowledge.

### Finding 2. The pilot's orchard story stops the climax, and its key words point back too faintly
- **Target.** Chapter 10: "Later, she never knew in which of the minutes, the pilot spoke without looking away from the loads. / 'I found an orchard once,' he said. 'Out past *Patience*... It wasn't the one.'"
- **What is wrong.** It sits inside the tightest stretch of the book, between the decision and the latch, and it halts the countdown. Its last line, "It wasn't the one", depends on a single line from a child seven chapters earlier ("And the one who keeps the orchard"), which I did not link up while reading.
- **Grounds.**
  - My reading log for chapter 10: I wanted the latch and got lore whose point I could not see. I only made the link to chapter 3 afterwards.
  - The title, *The Unseen Orchard*, raises this thread higher than the text supports. One line sets up "the one", and one line pays it off.
  - The story already has a quiet place made for the pilot. At the end of chapter 11 he has made the cabin safe and set two cups "where they would stay put", and Nessa sits "beside him until the tea had cooled enough to drink."
- **Fix.**
  - Move the orchard story to that tea scene at the end of chapter 11, where it can be heard, and where Nessa has just lived through the minutes it would have broken.
  - In chapter 3, or in the gravimeter scene of chapter 9, give "the one who keeps the orchard" a second touch so that "It wasn't the one" lands. A few words would do: the child's line could be repeated by Sava, or the pilot could glance at the laid place.
  - Cost: the strange stillness of a story told "in which of the minutes" is lost, and the climax gets no breath of lore.
- **Standing.** Bears for the placement. Held if for the second touch: if the writer wants the orchard keeper to stay almost entirely unseen, keep a single plant, but then "It wasn't the one" needs to name the orchard keeper outright so that the reader can find it.
- **Tests.** Flip: I would not be as sure that it works where it is. It cost me twice, in tension and in meaning. Swap: it is tied to this scene, this line and this title.

### Finding 3. The last image's blocking splits in two directions
- **Target.** Chapter 12, last paragraph: "She turned the half without the stone towards the place across from her, where nobody sat. Then she set it on a small plate beside her own, and began to eat the other."
- **What is wrong.** The half is offered across the table, to the empty place, but then put down beside her own plate. On a first reading I could not tell where the gift ends up, or so who it is for.
- **Grounds.** My reading log for chapter 12. It is the last image, and it carries the book's chief echo, of chapter 1's "the half without the stone" and chapter 3's laid place. A blur here costs more than the same blur anywhere else.
- **Fix.** Pick one direction.
  - She sets it at the empty place across from her, on its plate, among Nearbank's people. This is a gift to whoever is across, and to the orchard keeper.
  - Or she keeps it on a small plate beside her own, for someone who will sit there (Tomas, after the hearing). This is a gift kept waiting.
  - Cost: either choice narrows the ending's reach a little. The first is wider and stranger; the second is closer to Tomas.
- **Standing.** Held if: if the writer wants the gift aimed at the laid place, use the first; if at a waiting seat beside her, use the second. Both are better than both at once.
- **Tests.** Flip: I would not be as sure the blocking reads clearly. I re-read it twice. Swap: it is tied to this sentence.

### Finding 4. "He doesn't say" contradicts what the reader saw the pilot tell her, and the same exchange repeats the lore a fourth time
- **Target.** Chapter 12: "'The guard told me about people who cross to the unlit masses... He said they go to listen.'... 'Where did he go?' Tomas asked. 'Before.' / 'He doesn't say.'"
- **What is wrong.** The pilot told Nessa where he went twice: in chapter 7 ("I went to hear it talk. That's what I found instead") and in chapter 10 ("Out past *Patience*"). Her answer is either a protective lie, which would be rich, since Tomas may be thinking of going, or a slip. Nothing tells the reader which. The guard's lore, passed on through Tomas, is also the fourth time the story tells us people go out to listen.
- **Grounds.** My reading log for chapter 12, and the passed-on pattern logged for chapters 3, 7 and 10.
- **Fix.**
  - If it is a lie, give it a sign: a beat before she answers, or her eyes going to the spectacles. Then "She said she would" becomes a second, quieter lie, or a promise she means to keep late.
  - If it is a slip, change the answer to something true that still guards him, such as "Somewhere he won't go again."
  - Either way, cut the guard's lecture to Tomas's own question ("Your pilot doesn't give a name... Where did he go? Before."). The reader already knows what the guard said, and Tomas asking at all shows he has been changed.
  - Cost: we lose the irony of the man Tomas feared passing him the lore, and one link to the custody setting.
- **Standing.** Held if: the writer must say whether Nessa is lying. Give the fix both ways, as above.
- **Tests.** Flip: I would not be as sure the line reads as intended. A careful reader will see the contradiction. Swap: it is tied to these two characters and this pilot.

### Finding 5. Nobody asks whether the long hold reversed Nessa
- **Target.** Chapter 7, after the rescue ("The frame held me... It held me a long time"), through chapter 8.
- **What is wrong.** Chapter 3 spends a whole scene on Linet coming back reversed: the test kit, the bowl, the ration. Nessa then goes through a crossing far stranger than Linet's, and the text never checks her. Only the last scene (she eats Nearbank fruit; Linet has a separate meal) lets the reader work out that she was not reversed.
- **Grounds.**
  - My reading log for chapter 7: after chapter 3, I expected a food check.
  - It matters in chapter 10. The only setup for the voice is Linet's joint-shop speech ("when you come back turned your sides swap, and the quiet one gets a word in"). A reader who remembers it will wonder whether "Go" is that voice, and that turns on whether she was turned.
- **Fix.** One line in chapter 7 or 8: Nessa reads a ration label, or Linet runs the test kit on her over a channel. Either answer sharpens chapter 10.
  - If she is matched, "Go" is grief and habit, or the Witness, and not the reversal. The joint-shop man is then simply wrong, which suits Linet's scorn.
  - If she is reversed, "Go" can be read through his word, and the last scene's food must change.
  - Cost: one line, and a little of the open question around "Go".
- **Standing.** Held if: the writer must say whether the voice in chapter 10 is meant to be readable through the reversal lore, or kept free of it. Give the line both ways.
- **Tests.** Flip: moderately sure. A less careful reader may never ask, but a careful one will, because chapter 3 trained them to. Swap: it is tied to this story's mirror-turning rule.

### Finding 6. The surrender terms in chapter 11 read as minutes, just when the reader wants Tomas
- **Target.** Chapter 11: from "The next return carried the delegates' terms. Four people had signed, chosen by the drums' emergency meetings..." through "The mechanic who had left her watch put her name down for one."
- **What is wrong.** Three paragraphs of settlement come between "Tomas was not in the report" and his recording, and I skimmed them.
- **Grounds.** My reading log for chapter 11. The suspense around Tomas is real ("His interval had passed"), but it is held up by procedure rather than by anything that sharpens the wait.
- **Fix.** Keep what touches people we know: the household doors (the Berth Council's tokens handed over), and the mechanic putting her name down. Cut the rest to a sentence. Or keep Orra arguing the wording on the page, as a scene, with Nessa's eye on the clock. Cost: some of the sense that Nearbank's liberation is lawful and orderly, which suits the hearings in chapter 12.
- **Standing.** Bears.
- **Tests.** Flip: not as sure of the opposite. The paragraphs do close the households' and Rin's threads, but those also close on the page through the outstation doors and Dema's message. Swap: it is tied to these paragraphs.

### Finding 7. Small blocking slips that cost a reader a re-read
- **Targets and fixes.**
  - Chapter 4: "The escort took its shot. A hard tremor came through Wren's hull." It reads as the escort hitting Wren, and only later does Nessa blame the raider ("You nearly put a machine through our radiator").   - Chapter 6: "'Whose consent did she need to be born?'" No speaker is given, and I guessed Dema. Fix: attribute it. If it is Nessa's, it changes her.

  - Chapter 11: "'Don't lose that,' Nessa said." Linet is aboard Surety and Wren has drawn off. Fix: "over the channel".

- **Grounds.** My reading log for those chapters.
- **Cost.** A few words each.
- **Standing.** Bears.
- **Tests.** Flip: each was a real stop on first reading. Swap: each is tied to its line.

## 4. Considered and dropped (they failed the flip or swap test)

- **The sums in chapter 10 are hard to follow.** I slowed down, but the choice came through, and the sums are what make it a real dilemma rather than a convenience. I would be nearly as sure of the opposite, so I dropped it.

- **Sava's packed Witness speech in chapter 3.** It is brisk, in character, and "What does?" asks the reader's question. "The Transport" is a term held open that pays off in chapters 6 and 7. Dropped as a finding, but kept in the log.

## 5. What works and must be kept

- **The frame of the book.** The guarantee under the breadboard, the long table, and the half without the stone, given at the start and given away at the end. "Go... I'll finish this" returns as the voice that "did not say where". "It belongs with the rooms." / "Can it be dismantled?"
- **The rules of the crossing shown through choices, not stated.** "You want time or another gripper?" / "Time." "All the light reaching that camera belonged to this side." "It had a whole other side in which to be late."
- **First contact as a bill.** "Somebody had sent them a bill." The three fruits, and "Whatever else you buy, get me four of these."
- **The felt strangeness.** "The way a room changes when someone comes to stand in the doorway behind you." "Nothing in front of anything else." The hold in chapter 7, above all: "She wanted it to. That was the worst of it." This passage is the heart of the strange element. Keep it untouched.
- **The weight behind her chair.** The gravimeter's mass in chapter 2, its return on the pilot's list in chapter 9 at the minute she was held, and "the way some people keep a light on in a room nobody uses." This is the best kind of lore: a record and a habit, with no claim attached.
- **The two witnesses** in chapter 6. "There were two witnesses." / "I see one." / "Yes," said Dema. And the guard's hand on his chest. This is where the history becomes the present.
- **Nessa's silent fall.** "There was nothing wrong with it. / She selected Tomas's list." The book's moral hinge, done with no comment.
- **Tomas's voice on the channel** in chapter 7: "The instruments and named personnel are to be recovered intact."
- **Linet throughout**: the tools, the bowl and the spoon, "Don't. Eat them.", "You still owe me a day for my tools.", "I know what it cost.", "Can it be dismantled?"
- **The pilot**: "Pilot will do.", "Again.", "Not for the prize." / "That's why I said it.", *Patience*, "Every one."
- **Echoes that pay without being pointed out.** "The room was a room", then "It stayed a door". The drone and line in chapter 1, which become the rescue line in chapter 7. The "four larger structures" of chapter 2, which become the four drums of chapter 8. Moth's repaired foot clicking in the last scene.
- **The prison visit** in chapter 12. "You had time to come." / "To come there. Not to do both." / "I wasn't asking you to do both." "I'm not ready to make you feel better about this." The spectacles and the screwdriver. Unreconciled, but not closed.
- **Nessa's change shown in a schedule.** Dema's "No." and "Put those in the schedule."; "She had left that part blank because she hoped they could arrange it later." Then "It might be rock. She wanted to find out." The appetite that started the book survives its cost.
