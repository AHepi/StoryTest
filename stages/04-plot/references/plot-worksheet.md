# Plot worksheet

*Sources: Truby, The Anatomy of Story (his order of work for a plot: check it against the designing principle and theme, choose the teller, set out his long list of steps, then read the reveals as a sequence by themselves) and The Anatomy of Genres (strategy against tactics, from his chapter on Action), in the workshop's own words; the Gap theory (principles 2 Breach, 4 Scheduling, 6 Perspective and 7 Settlement) and the Anticipation theory (principles 1 Multiplication, 6 Knowledge position and 7 The ratchet) as the frame.*

**What is already in the skills, and not repeated here.** The plot skill's Building procedure (steps 1 to 8) is the method, and its modules hold the book detail: the seven jobs and Truby's longer list regrouped as six more jobs, where to start, crisis, climax and resolution, in `.claude/skills/plot/references/structure-and-conflict.md`; the reveals in sequence, checked for logic, weight and pace, in `.claude/skills/plot/references/reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 4; choosing a teller, in `.claude/skills/plot/references/telling-and-perspective.md`; each genre's beats, as jobs, in section 6 of its file under `.claude/skills/genre/references/genres/`. Truby's long step list is used there regrouped as jobs, and is not set out step by step here either. This file adds the order of work, the shape of the output, and one tool the skills lack: the plan read at two levels (section 3).

Contents: 1 Order of work. 2 The jobs table. 3 The plan at two levels. 4 What the output holds.

## 1. Order of work

1. **Hold the plot to the premise.** Keep the designing principle and the story's question from stage 01 in view; Truby checks every plot against them first *(built: a plot that drifts from its principle becomes a string of incidents)*. Where one symbol from stage 03 stands for the whole story, the plot should act it out.
2. **Place the idea, then the normal and its breach, then the story in the order it happened** (plot skill, Building steps 1 to 3).
3. **The jobs table** (section 2 below), built from stage 01's seven jobs, stage 02's moral line and stage 03's places.
4. **The plan at two levels** (section 3 below), for the hero and the main opponent.
5. **The schedule of knowledge** (Building step 4): set the reveals out by themselves, in order, and run the three checks in `reveals-and-withholding.md`, section 4.
6. **The teller, and the knowledge position scene by scene** (Building steps 5 and 6).
7. **The genre's beats:** for each beat in section 6 of the genre file, what job it does and where this plot gets that job done, or how it does without it (genre skill, Building step 5).
8. **The settlement** (Building step 7), with the false endings to avoid (`premise-and-theme.md`, section 8).
9. **Test each load-bearing choice** with `hard-to-vary` (Building step 8).

## 2. The jobs table

One row per job: the seven core jobs, then whichever of the six longer jobs this story needs (`structure-and-conflict.md`, sections 2 and 3). A short piece may need only the seven.

| Job | The event that does it | Where (stage 03's place) | Moral step (stage 02) | Genre beat | What the audience knows here |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

A job with no event is a gap in the plot; an event that does no job is a candidate to cut or to give a job (the remove test). Where two jobs share one event, say so: it is often the strongest kind of scene.

## 3. The plan at two levels

In his chapter on Action, Truby distinguishes *tactics* from *strategy* (his terms), and notes that heroes are often better at the one than the other *(fitted from Action stories)*; read his account there. The workshop uses the distinction as two levels at which to write any plan: the **campaign**, the whole course of action meant to win the goal, and the **moves**, what the character does when trouble arrives. The two can come apart, and when they do, the gap between them can drive the middle *(the workshop's reading)*.

**The move.** For the hero's plan (the Plan job in `structure-and-conflict.md`, section 2), write two lines: the campaign (the course that would win the goal) and the moves (what they do when trouble comes). Do the same for the main opponent, whose plan should be written as fully as the hero's (section 3 there, item 3). Then ask:
- **Which level is the hero weak at?** A hero strong in moves and weak in campaign comes out of scene after scene on top while the goal slips further off; that weakness can be the flaw itself, or grow from it (stage 02). A hero strong in campaign and weak in moves has a sound plan that keeps breaking on contact.
- **When does the weakness cost them?** That moment is often the first plan's failure, the turn by failure in `structure-and-conflict.md`, section 3, item 4.
- **Who knows the plan?** A plan the audience sees lets it predict and fear; a hidden one buys surprise. That is the Gap theory's scheduling choice, already the Plan row's third column in section 2 there.

*The workshop's extension, not Truby's claim:* the two levels apply beyond Action wherever a hero pursues a goal against a thinking opponent (a heist, a courtroom, a campaign of seduction); in a story where the resistance is the world rather than a mind, they add little.

## 4. What the output holds

`plot-vN.md`, in this order, each part with its marker:
1. The normal, the breach, and the story in the order it happened.
2. The jobs table.
3. The plan at two levels, for hero and opponent.
4. The reveals in order, with the three checks' results.
5. The teller, and the knowledge position at each major scene.
6. The genre beat map.
7. The settlement: what has changed from the opening normal, what it cost, which mysteries close and which wonders stay open.
