# Pitch review: theme and the owner's rules

One lens only. For each pitch: is the core message earned by what happens rather than said? Is the doom caused by the culture's own habits rather than luck or one villain? Were those habits once right? Does the story avoid teaching that questions are dangerous, or that the heroine is always right? Is it free of any trace of the second attachment and of the banned words? Is it truly not a dystopia? I also checked the other owner rules (not The Catch, not Caraway, religion not in a stock form) where they touch theme.

A word search of all three pitches found none of the banned words used as ideas, and no book of ideas, maxim, creed or Greek-letter name in any of them. The problems below are subtler than that.

---

## Pitch A: SECONDS (the Marlows, Essex thieves)

### Strengths
- **Every rule was right once, and the pitch shows why.** "Nothing on paper" kept them out of court. "Make weight" bought seconds. "Face home" kept them on their own world. "We feed our own" kept the turned off a police list. Each rule has a "but" that grows the doom.
- **The family's success changed its own world.** Thieves like them got so good that the City moved to storing gold by weight. That made jobs longer, and long jobs turn people. Then the world changes a second time, from outside: the neighbours move a mountain. The family's habits make that fatal: a binned envelope, a margin cut to a tenth of a second, and Peg's lucky fry-up. That is the brief's pattern, done without a single line of explanation.
- **The theme lives in objects.** The chalk board of weights, applause at the weigh-in, burned tissue, a burned ferry timetable, a binned envelope, a pencil Tess never uses. And Peg's own hand writing "Frank. 10 st 2. Turned." The question that cracks the season is an ordinary one: "Why's it a kilo out?"
- **There is no villain.** Ray hunts a "spinner" who doesn't exist, and episode 7 shows that nobody spun anybody except Danny, who spun himself. The blame is shared. Peg made the fry-up, binned the letter and misremembered Frank. Danny said yes to Liam. Tess cut the margin and gave Rosie nine seconds. Ray fed them without ever asking them to change.
- **The heroine is plainly wrong, and it costs.** "I gave you nine." Tess's own numbers turned Rosie, and her own scale killed Liam.
- **"Always dooms" is earned.** Frank shows the code was already killing its founder twenty-four years ago, hidden by a memory that had turned. The last image, an Ahern girl cheered on the bathroom scale, shows the unchanged mirror family still on the same road. Maggie changes but Declan never does, so the Aherns show that one right person is not enough.
- **The people learn to ask, not just the heroine.** The whole family writes on the rolls all night. Tess asks each of them whether to hand the rolls over. Kieran adds "checked against Maggie's."
- **Not a dystopia, and the ordinary world helps.** The Trading Standards law that forced them to keep twenty-four years of audit rolls is what saves them. The working world is the family's ally, and the family never knew it.

### Serious problems, with fixes
1. **The one time a Marlow breaks the code, she is punished and nothing comes of it.** In episode 9 Rosie, for the first time in her life, doesn't face home. She nearly drowns. Peg tips the sea down the drain, and nothing in episode 10 uses what Rosie found. The audience learns that "face home" was right and that leaving the family's path drowns you. That is close to teaching that asking is dangerous.
   *Fix:* make her discovery feed the answer. The weighbridge printer chattered when she landed in a gush of seawater. Next morning, Kieran weighs the mop bucket on Peg's kitchen scale and then on the bridge, and the two numbers disagree. File it. In episode 10 it backs up Danny's "Why's it a kilo out?" Her one rule-break then becomes one of the two clues that save the family.
2. **The ending proves "never go to them" right.** The Register "puts them on a list the police can read," and the season ends with Tess and Danny in prison. A viewer can fairly conclude that the family was right to fear the state all along. The theme needs this rule to have been right once and wrong now.
   *Fix:* the police stopped getting that list years ago under the Looking Act, and the letter saying so is among the envelopes Peg bins. The prison sentences then come only from choosing to hand over the rolls as a confession of the thefts. Asking for help doesn't jail anyone; choosing to give the evidence does.

### Smaller notes
- Religion is left out. The brief allows that, but the owner said "the religious angle is fine," which suggests they liked having one. If you want one, it should grow out of the crossers' own lore rather than be added. The Hill that "takes you", the minute beside a lost crosser's weight and Frank's photo above the chalk board could carry a strange, home-grown devotion with no priests or texts. This is optional.
- Tess, the family member who keeps every number, is a cousin of Caraway's Tallykeeper heroine. The pitch gets away with it because her counting is her flaw, and the saving record is paper she never kept. Keep it that way.
- Rosie staying turned, and Terry cooking food he can't share, sit near The Catch's ending (the heroine who can't share food with her family). That's fine as long as no episode ends on an image of a meal that can't be shared.

### Verdict
Passes, and by the widest margin of the three. The culture is admired, warm and self-dooming. The heroine is often wrong, nobody names the idea, and the world is ordinary. Fix episode 9 so that breaking a rule is rewarded, and make "never go to them" visibly out of date.

## Pitch B: HONEST WEIGHT (the Carricks, Saskatchewan, 1911)

### Strengths
- **The main departure is the theme, without a word said.** The wheat fills and doesn't feed. Its virtues are its poison: no rust, no weevil, no mould. The mill's own slogan, "No mouse will live in Carrick flour," is the diagnosis in plain sight, and on a second viewing it reads the other way.
- **The strongest "once right" mechanism of the three.** "Save the cleanest ears" is a rule a thousand years old. Here it picks turned grain every harvest, because no blight can touch a turned ear. Good husbandry breeds the poison.
- **More of the same, more lovingly.** The town answers with bigger loaves, Mercy porridge on the sick ward, a quarantine on the healthy Galician farms, and a bid to make Mercy the nation's famine reserve. Shown the pigs in episode 4, the town chooses more.
- **The people weigh for themselves.** Agnes Tait weighs her own children on the elevator scale in episode 6. At the fair, other mothers step onto the platform unasked. In 1935 the women try a new seed on the hens and the scale before anyone sows it.
- **Religion at its best is fresh.** Nan's faith grows from what a crossing really shows: everything lit from inside, her baby's heart "like a lamp." Pryce weighs the dying to find the soul. Her waltz is answered by listeners she will never see. There are no priests or texts.
- **Not a dystopia.** A friendly boomtown at peace, with no banned words.

### Serious problems, with fixes
1. **The central reversal repeats Caraway.** In episodes 4 to 6, the town's revered elder is revealed as secretly turned ("the sacred mouth is a turned mouth"). Caraway's high keeper, secretly turned, was on the list of things not to repeat, and the owner will see it at once.
   *Fix:* don't hide Nan's turn. Make her afternoon at the Stone the district's favourite story, told in episode 1 as "Nan's lucky day," and let everyone believe it did nothing, or blessed her. The mystery then moves to B's truly new idea: a turned palate picking turned grain, confirmed every year by "cleanest ears." The blood test becomes confirmation, not a reveal. Episode 6 lands on what is truly hidden: Andrew and the pigs, and Nan knowing that the world tastes of chalk and choosing not to say so.
2. **An unquestioning family is saved by luck.** The Hnatiuks keep their rules ("seed is not for strangers," cut before Illia's day) just as blindly, and are spared only because the rust came late. On screen that says a culture that never asks can do fine if the weather is kind, which works against "always dooms."
   *Fix:* make their rule cost lives too, in plain view. The grandmother's "seed is not for strangers" keeps the only healthy seed away from the starving town for weeks. Name a child who dies in that gap. Olena's choice to break the rule, once she knows why her family cuts early, is what ends it.
3. **An official settles it.** At the fair, "the Cerealist withdraws Mercy; the farmers vote to burn it." If the Dominion's official taster rules first, the town has only swapped one trusted mouth for another.
   *Fix:* reverse the order. The farmers vote to burn Mercy against the Cerealist's "superb" and against the contract, and he withdraws it afterwards, following them.
4. **Three echoes of The Catch.**
   - Two bowls where "baker's yeast won't raise Mercy and Nan's leaven won't raise Hnatiuk flour: 'like two kinds of life'" restage The Catch's two dishes of turned germs that nothing will eat. The hens, the pigs, the loaves that never mould and the dog already carry this. Cut the bowls, or show it only as the baker's dead dough.
   - Nan's blood on a cloth, tested for which way it turns light, is close to The Catch's flask of turned blood. Test something else of hers, such as the sugar from her private garden's beets.
   - In the ending the neighbours raise land and wait with lanterns for an old woman, and she goes to them. That is The Catch's kind figure from the next world who takes people in and tends them. The pitch's own check ("the neighbours never appear and take no one") claims too much.
   *Fix:* let the island be raised for the neighbours' own reasons, as the source's mountain is moved. Cut "lanterns, waiting," and leave whether anyone receives Nan as a wonder. She chooses to go to a floor that happens to be there. The answered dance proves only that someone is listening.

### Smaller notes
- In the 1935 tag, Olena the teacher tells a child to "find out" why the town weighs everything. That is the one moment where someone speaks the moral aloud. Cut the line; the hens and the scale already say it.
- "Charity fed the famine" can slide into "charity kills." Keep the Saturday loaf at the end, now made from Hnatiuk flour, and add the weighing, so the lesson is to keep giving and check what you give. "The first Saturday weighing" currently reads as if it replaces the loaf.
- "Nan's mouth is never wrong" makes one woman the thing nobody questions. Keep the shared habits on screen (cleanest ears, the Saturday loaf, strangers' seed, the Cerealist chewing grain) so it reads as a town's way, not a cult of one saint. "Saint" and "sent back to feed people" drift toward stock near-death-vision talk. The lamps and the baby's heart are the fresh part, so lean on those.
- Major Hollis, the War Office man who wants the Stone, adds nothing to this theme. Keep him small, so he doesn't become the third-act villain.
- Bel is wrong early: she hid the prints, handed out the loaves and sat on the blood result. She also shares the family's mistake about the dancer. That is enough; no change needed.

### Verdict
The theme is embodied more beautifully here than anywhere else, and nobody preaches it. But the central reversal repeats Caraway's secretly turned keeper, the ending leans on The Catch's kind neighbours, and one unasking family is let off by luck. All of that can be fixed without losing the core. Make Nan's turn known from episode 1 and let the mystery be the bread.

## Pitch C: SLACK WATER (Inverfarrach, the Cure, the Hand)

### Strengths
- **The cleanest "once right" mechanism.** Three things made the Cure work, and the town knew only one. Isa wore her diving weight belt out of habit. The ferrymen were big men fed a huge breakfast. The pilgrims arrived starving. So the pilgrim was always the lighter of the pair, and the turn always went to them. The photograph "The First Slack" is a fair plant.
- **The town's kindness quietly flips the odds.** The Strengthening makes pilgrims heavier than their ferrymen. The fix keeps the kindness: feed the pilgrims, and put the weight on the ferryman. This is the best answer of the three to the risk of "kindness kills."
- **The missing question has a moral cost.** Asking whether it would still work if they let go sounds like abandoning the sick, and letting go is how Alasdair was lost. That is why a kind town doesn't ask. This is well observed.
- **One right person is shown not to be enough.** Jess loses the argument at the meeting, and even Isa backing her changes nothing. So she hands the frame to the households and they count. Duncan, not Jess, stops the Great Slack.
- **The asker isn't made a saint.** Aditi asks everything and is wrong for most of the season.
- **Not a dystopia.** A working Scotland, and "nobody is forbidden to ask anything." There are no banned words.

### Serious problems, with fixes
1. **The mint jar.** Pilgrims taste mint from home, and if it tastes of caraway they are still turned. The Catch's key reveal is "nothing has happened to the mint." The rejected attempt was titled CARAWAY and turned on a mint taste test. The pitch even names caraway, and the finale's Night of Leaves is built on every household tasting its own mint.
   *Fix:* use this town's own tests. The Ferry House dogs sniff every returning pilgrim; the source gives this ("the dog knows before the lab does"), and Bran is already on screen. On the Night of Leaves, each household sends its own wind-up clock or watch across and chalks which way the hands run when it comes back. That suits a town that lives by Loch Time, and Callum's watch has already come back running anticlockwise.
2. **The skeleton is Caraway's, in secular dress.** C has:
   - a community built round a crossing machine, with rules from the founder's grief;
   - a heroine who keeps the community's count (Caraway's Tallykeeper, C's "Keeper of the Table");
   - a banned pariah;
   - weight near the crossing that sets the odds of turning (Caraway's dead fed to the machine; C's sea overhead);
   - a cure found by changing where or when you cross;
   - a community's love that changes the conditions (Caraway's funerals, C's Strengthening);
   - an episode 8 titled "Custody by Pantry," the same title as Caraway's episode 5.

   The owner has rejected this shape once.
   *Fix:* at the least, rename episode 8, drop "Keeper," and cut the list above down wherever a beat can go. The deeper fix is to drop the show's first new rule (mass overhead sets the odds of turning). Let slack water matter only for getting home: the pull toward home is steadiest then, so fewer people are lost. Turning then depends on the grip-and-weight rule alone. That cuts the tie to Caraway's mechanism and keeps C's best idea.
3. **The pitch says it has no religion, but it does.** It has pilgrims, a living saint whose sleeve they touch, a miracle cure at a special water, a rule the town calls sacred, a founder's key, and a memorial of the Lost that children stroke. It also has capitalised offices (the Hand, the Strengthening, the Lost, the Night of Leaves) and set sayings: "The Tide takes some. We don't speak of it," and "give the Tide time." That is Lourdes with a crossing frame, plus the liturgy of fossil phrases the brief bans.
   *Fix:* choose one way. Either strip it: make the Cure a licensed clinic trade with patients, fees and waiting lists, founded by a respected old diver rather than a saint, and let the town's pride be civic, like a famous hospital town. Or make faith fresh on purpose, in a form that is not a pilgrimage.
4. **The heroine is right from episode 4 on.** After the first reversal, every finding Jess makes is correct and the town is wrong every time. Her failings (the jars she opened for Callum, her silence) all lie in the past.
   *Fix:* give her an error now, with a cost. She sets every allowance. In episode 6, have her print the Double Hand's allowances at the old slack herself, because the Table is her life's work and she doesn't yet trust her own gauge. Shona's turn is then partly hers.
5. **The doom gathers in one man.** Fraser runs the Strengthening and the back-door kitchen, fakes the cure rate and padlocks the store. The pitch says there is no villain, but on screen one kindly, controlling man does most of the harm.
   *Fix:* spread it. Each ferryman shades his own log a little "so the pilgrims don't lose heart." The council votes for the padlock while the town cheers. Pilgrims' families demand the Strengthening.

### Smaller notes
- Episode 4's "The Tide is fine. It's us." is The Catch's reveal (the world didn't change, we did) and states the theme outright. Episode 9's "One right person is not enough" is the brief's own sentence. Keep both out of dialogue, and out of the show bible too.
- The epilogue ("children cross leaves after school and chalk the results") and the sign "Counted by Inverfarrach" tip toward a moral. The chalked numbers alone are enough.
- Weight read on the Pool screen "where nobody stands" at the moment of a vanishing is close to The Catch's needle that kicks when an object vanishes. A lost sibling answering a gesture across the barrier is close to The Catch's hands on glass. Both come from the source, so keep them, but stage them unlike The Catch. For example, show the reading later, cold, in a log.

### Verdict
C's theme mechanics are the most exact of the three, and its last two episodes show a whole town learning to count. Under the owner's rules it fails as written: mint that tastes of caraway, a shrine town with a saint, and a skeleton the owner has already rejected. Mending it takes real surgery. If this story's strengths are wanted, carry two of them into another pitch: the lighter body turning in a held pair, and the households counting for themselves.

---

## Across all three
- The scaffolding in each pitch ("The culture that never questions," "The doom, step by step," "The theme, earned," "The question the culture has no place for," "what a swap would break") is fine in a planning document. None of it may ever reach a script.
- All three make kindness the thing that kills, as the brief asks. The risk is an audience learning "don't be kind." Each ending should keep the kindness and add a check. C does this best (feed them, and weigh the ferryman). A needs Kieran's "checked against Maggie's" to stay on screen. B needs the Saturday loaf to survive.
