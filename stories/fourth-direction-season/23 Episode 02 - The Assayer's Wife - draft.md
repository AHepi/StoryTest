# Episode 2: The Assayer's Wife

*To sell the painting by Friday, the Marlows must make it look like itself, and Rosie secretly uses every attempt as a cure.*

---

## Cold open

### Scene 1. A vault under Cheapside, Tuesday, 04:16.

Deep under the City. A night guard in his sixties sits in the corridor outside a strongroom with a flask of tea and a crossword folded to a square. On his monitor: the vault. Steel drawers from floor to ceiling, a yellow line painted on the floor, the grey panel of the weigh-wall on the far side. Above the drawers, the dark mouths of the bead hoppers.

Nobody is in the vault. Nobody has been in it since six last night.

04:17. The guard fills in a clue.

The roar reaches him through the door before he finds it on the screen. Tungsten beads, grey as river gravel and heavier than lead shot, pour out of the hoppers and bury the drawers to their handles. Grey dust hangs in the camera light. His tea is in his lap.

GUARD: (into his radio) There's nobody in there. There is nobody in there.

The radio answers with a dozen voices at once. Other buildings, other basements.

The same second, again and again: an art store where crates go under a grey tide; a jeweller's strongroom, beads hissing into velvet trays; a cash room; a safe-deposit hall whose brass numbers disappear one row at a time. A hundred basements across the Square Mile, filling together, with nobody in any of them.

Then quiet. Beads settling, ticking like rain on a shed roof.

### Scene 2. The Marlow house, the bathroom, Grays, a few minutes later.

Dark. The frosted window is the grey of the estuary before dawn. A tap drips.

ROSIE MARLOW, 22, in an old jumper of Danny's that reaches her knees, pads in barefoot and hooks the bathroom scale out from under the basin with her toe. She steps on. The display lights blue under her chin.

43.1.

She smiles, alone in the dark, the way a girl smiles at a good exam result. Then she steps off and slides the scale back with her toe, slow, so it doesn't scrape, so nobody wakes.

### Scene 3. The weighbridge office, Grays, Tuesday, 07:10.

Peg's kitchen is also the office of a public weighbridge, and it smells of bacon, diesel and toast. A counter with a hatch onto the yard. The ticket printer. A long plated floor at one end, the little bridge, with Peg's green safe standing at its far edge. Frank's one photo on the wall, a big man laughing in the yard; the framed Ministry receipt ("One object, found wet, sand adhering"); the framed forensic cuttings; the chalk board of everyone's weight. Outside the window, fog on the river, the Tilbury cranes coming out of it one by one.

On the radio, the same calm BBC voice that reported the silent merger.

RADIO: ...a ripple lasting several seconds, recorded by gravity meters around the world at seventeen minutes past four. Scientists are calling it a next-door event. In the City of London, every weigh-wall tripped at the same moment, and several banks say their vaults will be closed for days while...

KIERAN MARLOW, 12, in school uniform, sits at his homework desk in the corner with the night's tickets in a pile, sorting them like a bank clerk. Lorries that weighed after hours, their numbers in the printer's purple ink. His own ticket from nine last night, the cast-iron test weight on the little bridge: 20.00. He writes it in his log.

Then one more. He holds it up to the window.

KIERAN: Nan. Who weighed something at seventeen minutes past four?

PEG MARLOW, 72, at the stove, a fish slice in one hand, doesn't turn round.

PEG: Nobody weighs anything at seventeen minutes past four.

KIERAN: The little bridge did. Nought point three one. Nothing on it.

He looks at the plate. Nothing on it now, either. Just the steel, and the safe at the end.

TERRY MARLOW, 66, Peg's brother, cracking eggs into a pan in yellow washing-up gloves, nods at the radio.

TERRY: Somebody next door's dropped the good china.

Kieran tapes the ticket into his log, under the poster above his desk: a map of the sky in purple smudges and threads, printed by his school. THE NEIGHBOURS' GALAXIES: FOUND BY WEIGHING, NEVER BY LOOKING. Along the bottom: SIXTY YEARS WE POINTED DISHES THE WRONG WAY.

PEG: That's my roll you're sticking in your book.

KIERAN: It's them, Nan. Sir says you can't look at them, you can only weigh them. If I weigh the same thing every night for a year, I'll see their tide.

PEG: And have you?

KIERAN: (honest) Not yet.

PEG: Next door can pay for my roll.

She slides a plate of eggs onto the counter for a lorry driver. Kieran smooths the ticket flat with his thumb: 04:17. 0.31 kg.

**MAIN TITLES.**

## Act one: Peg's plan

### Scene 4. The weighbridge office, Tuesday, 07:40.

Breakfast, which at the Marlows is also business. Two drivers eat bacon rolls at the counter while they wait for tickets; MICK, 50s, big, with a sports page, is one of them. The family is at the long table. DANNY MARLOW, 41, foreman, in his concrete-grey work jacket. His son LIAM, 19, big-boned, quiet. Cousin SHAUN, 26, wiry. TESS MARLOW, 38, pencil behind her ear, which she never uses. Terry at the stove. Sixpence, the old lurcher, under the table, waiting on Liam.

At the dresser Peg pricks her finger, touches the drop to her glucose meter, reads it, and drops the meter back in the cutlery drawer among the fish knives. The morning ritual.

Rosie comes down last, in the big jumper. She's grey under the eyes and bright everywhere else.

ROSIE: Forty-three one.

PEG: On what?

ROSIE: Bathroom.

PEG: Bathroom scale's a liar. On the bridge. Before you eat.

Rosie steps onto the little bridge in her socks. The printer chatters and pushes out a ticket. Peg tears it off and holds it up like a winning raffle number: 43.10. Danny bangs the table. Shaun whistles. Mick, who has seen this before, claps with a bacon roll in his hand.

PEG: Lightest in this family since your Auntie Tess.

Peg chalks 43.1 beside ROSIE on the board, under 43.6. Tess watches her do it. Something crosses her face and goes. She was fourteen when her name first went at the top of that board.

The gate buzzer. DILYS's launderette van is in the yard, the way it is every Tuesday. DILYS, 60s, carries in a crate of white plastic tubs with printed batch codes on their lids and sets it on the side for Terry. Peg counts notes into an envelope from the drawer. It's a thin envelope.

PEG: Tell him the rest next week.

DILYS: He's not fussed, Peg. He never is.

PEG: I'm fussed.

Dilys goes. Terry stacks his tubs in the fridge, one shelf of his own, gloves on.

PEG: (to the table) That was the last of the float. So.

She takes the catalogue cutting off the wall, where she pinned it last week, and lays it beside the painting on the table. *The Assayer's Wife*: a woman at a window in a fur-trimmed jacket, a little brass balance in her hand, light coming in over her shoulder. In the cutting the window is on the left. On the table it is on the right.

PEG: I've got a buyer. Private. Collector, over for the spring sales. He's seen the photo. Copy, real, I don't care which: he says if it matches the book, it's the real one, and he'll pay what the insurers would have.

DANNY: It don't match the book.

PEG: He flies home Friday.

TESS: So it doesn't match by Friday.

PEG: So we make it match.

DANNY: How do you turn a picture round?

PEG: Same way it went. Hop it.

A beat. Mick's roll stops halfway to his mouth; he suddenly takes a great interest in the racing.

TESS: It went wrong on one trip. Putting it right might take a hundred.

PEG: Then it takes a hundred. You face home when you cross, don't you? A picture doesn't. It's got nothing to face with. You hold it loose, you let it roll about, and one of these goes it comes back the right way.

DANNY: She's got a face, Mum. She's looking out the window.

PEG: Wrong window.

Laughter round the table. It's family lore, as old as the rigs, said the way the family says all its lore: as a joke everybody already knows. Only Terry, at the stove, doesn't laugh. He turns the bacon.

ROSIE: I'll do it.

She says it before Peg has finished looking round the table.

SHAUN: I'll do it. I've got eight seconds.

TESS: (not looking up) Eight point four.

ROSIE: It's my picture. I carried her out, I'll carry her round.

LIAM: I could do it.

PEG: Not with that breakfast in you, love.

The table laughs again, at the big lad. Liam laughs too, a beat late, and puts his fork down. Under the table Sixpence gets his bacon.

Tess is looking at Rosie. Properly looking: the grey under her eyes, her wrists in the big sleeves.

TESS: Three seconds.

PEG: Three?

DANNY: That's a surgeon's hop.

TESS: Three. In, count, face home, out. Not a tenth more. Look at her.

ROSIE: Three's lovely. I'll be a surgeon.

TESS: You're grey.

ROSIE: It's March. Everyone's grey.

PEG: Not on my bridge, mind. I'm not having forty tickets of a picture on my roll. Do it by the stove.

She pins the catalogue cutting back on the wall beside the stove, at eye height, and smooths it flat with her palm.

Terry, back to the room, hums "Show Me the Way to Go Home" into the pan.

PEG: Give it a rest, Tel.

### Scene 5. A bank on Cheapside, Tuesday, 11:00.

Underground. The strongroom from the cold open, its door open now. Grey beads lie knee-deep across the floor; men in masks work industrial hoovers that scream and cough. Two insurers in good coats stand in the corridor on their phones, both saying the same words: "by the summer".

A FACILITIES MANAGER in a lanyard and a suit that has had a bad morning shakes Danny's hand. Behind Danny, Liam holds a tube of drawings in both arms like something that might break.

FACILITIES MANAGER: Every wall in the City went off at once. With nobody in the vault. Insurers say if the walls can be tripped from — wherever that was —

DANNY: Next door.

FACILITIES MANAGER: — then a wall isn't enough. They want the floor done. Thicker. Before they'll renew.

DANNY: A wall's a tripwire. Tripwires go off. A floor doesn't go off. A floor's just there, being thick.

He crouches, takes a handful of beads and lets them run through his fingers.

DANNY: I know what slows a crosser down. Show him, Li.

Liam, surprised to be asked, unrolls a drawing on top of a filing cabinet: a section through a vault floor, concrete in layers with grey dots thickening towards one edge.

LIAM: Beads in the pour. Not even, though. Thickest at the back, where they'd come in from the street side. Every bit of that a crosser's got to be in the black for. And you can't see the beads till you're in it.

FACILITIES MANAGER: You sound like you've done it.

DANNY: We pour it. Same thing.

The manager laughs. Danny doesn't.

FACILITIES MANAGER: Who else have you done?

DANNY: Half this street. Did a vault further down in '24, they had us back last autumn for the floor underneath. Two floors to get through, not one. That's a long time to be nowhere.

The manager signs. Liam rolls the drawing up again. His ears are pink. He has been praised for his head, not his size, and he doesn't know where to put it.

### Scene 6. The weighbridge office, Tuesday, evening.

Danny's phone buzzes on the table between the ketchup and the teapot and doesn't stop. He answers it, writes on the back of his hand, rings off; it goes again. Peg has the order book open. Every page this week is full, and she's started on next month.

DANNY: Banks. Banks I've never heard of. One wants Marlow Mix in a basement that hasn't got a vault in it yet.

PEG: Three times what we had on Monday.

She shuts the order book with satisfaction.

PEG: Every job just got a second longer. Good for us both ways. We sell them the floor. And who's light enough to get through it?

She looks at the chalk board. So does everyone. Rosie's name at the top.

TESS: A second's a lot.

PEG: Then find it.

TESS: I'll find it.

She says it the way she says everything about numbers: as if it's already done.

Later. The family has gone up. Tess is last, turning off lights, and she stops at Frank's photo. The big man, laughing in the yard with his sleeves rolled up.

TESS: That copper said he met Dad.

Peg, at the sink.

PEG: Your dad never met a copper in his life. Too quick for them.

TESS: He said he met what was left of him.

PEG: There's nothing left when the black takes you. That's the point of it. Don't you let that man in your head.

Peg turns off the tap. The painting lies on the dresser under a tea towel. The kitchen is dark except for the small green light on the printer.

## Act two: seven hops

### Scene 7. The weighbridge office, Wednesday, 05:30. Hop one.

Before the gate opens. Blinds down. The yard lights make orange bars on the lino.

Rosie is in her rig: the padded belt, the collar, the wrist unit on her left arm. She stands on the lino by the stove, not on the plate, and holds the painting against her chest in both arms, loosely, the way Peg showed her: "like a tray of hot tea". Danny, in his work jacket, sits on the table's edge. Peg stands by the safe. Tess has her eyes on the wall clock. Terry is at the stove with the kettle, which is always on.

TESS: Three. Face home. Out.

ROSIE: Yes, miss.

She presses the wrist unit.

*The black.* Sound drops to breath and heartbeat. There is no floor. Her field glows round her, a soft circle the width of her arms, and in it, and only in it, everything is open. The painting is not a picture any more but a thing with an inside: the weave of the linen like a woven field; the paint lying on it in layers, like the faces of a gravel pit; and under the woman's hand on the balance, a second hand, an earlier one, painted out centuries ago by someone who had changed his mind. Rosie looks at it. Beyond her glow there is nothing at all.

The wrist unit counts tenths. 2.1. 2.2. She feels for the tug of home, turns to it, the way you turn in bed towards a warm back.

3.0. She steps.

*The office.* A gasp. The smell of bacon fat and the kettle. Rosie on the lino, the painting in her arms.

DANNY: Straight?

ROSIE: Straight.

Everyone leans in over the painting. The window is on the right.

Rosie's eyes go past them, to the catalogue cutting on the wall. She looks at it for exactly as long as it takes to read a line of print. Then she looks at the painting like everyone else.

PEG: Coin didn't come up.

DANNY: First go.

PEG: Again tonight.

Under the table, Sixpence growls at Rosie, a low steady note.

DANNY: Still jealous, is he? She's only been gone three seconds.

### Scene 8. The weighbridge office, Wednesday noon to Thursday dawn. Hops two to four.

**Hop two. Wednesday, 12:30.** The gate chained "for dinner". Rosie by the stove, the painting loose in her arms, the wrist unit. Tess counts aloud for the room, "...two... three", though nobody in the black can hear a word of it. The gasp. Peg this time:

PEG: Straight?

ROSIE: Straight.

The window on the right. Rosie's eyes to the cutting, and back.

**Hop three. Wednesday, 22:00.** Danny back from a pour, grey dust on his eyebrows, eating a pie standing up.

DANNY: Straight?

ROSIE: Straight.

Right. The cutting. Back.

DANNY: Bloody thing's facing home better than we do.

**Late Wednesday, the empty office.** Everyone has gone up except Liam, who has been sweeping. He leans the broom on the counter, looks at the stairs, and steps onto the little bridge. The display climbs. He watches it and steps off quickly, before the reading can settle, before the printer can chatter. He goes up with the ticket unprinted.

**Hop four. Thursday, 05:30.** Blinds down. Rosie is slower into her rig; Tess does the collar clip for her.

TESS: Three.

ROSIE: I know.

The black, briefly: the woman's face in the glow, the earlier hand under the paint, Rosie's own breath. Home.

PEG: Straight?

ROSIE: Straight.

The window on the right. The cutting. Back.

### Scene 9. The weighbridge office, continuous.

Nobody moves for a second. Four goes, and the woman is still looking out of the wrong window.

PEG: Coin's got to come up sometime.

Terry is drying a pan in his gloves. He isn't looking at anyone, least of all Rosie, who stands by the stove with the painting against her chest and her eyes on the cutting on the wall.

TERRY: (gently) The black never gave me back.

Peg's hand stops on the tea caddy.

DANNY: It's a picture, Tel.

TERRY: I know, love.

He hangs the pan up. Rosie looks at him. He doesn't look back. He opens his fridge, takes out a white tub, and eats standing, a spoon at a time, with a nip of gin beside it.

Peg starts the fry-up. It isn't yet six, and the lorries come at seven.

### Scene 10. The weighbridge office, Thursday, 08:15.

Open for business. A queue of tippers on the big bridge outside, engines ticking. Peg loaded a new paper roll into the printer first thing, and the printer is going like a till.

Rosie is behind the counter, doing the tickets. She's quick and she's funny with the drivers, and she's greyer than she was on Tuesday. Mick's lorry settles on the big bridge; the printer chatters; Rosie tears off his ticket and holds it out through the hatch.

ROSIE: New roll's printed backwards, Nan.

Tess, at the table with the job diary she keeps in her head, looks up sharp. It's a look she didn't choose. For a second her face is completely open.

Rosie sees the look and laughs.

ROSIE: Joke.

TESS: Not funny.

ROSIE: Bit funny.

MICK: (taking his ticket) You want your eyes testing, girl.

ROSIE: I want a lot of things, Mick.

The camera sees the ticket as Mick folds it into his cab: ordinary purple print, the right way round, MARLOW AGGREGATES along the top.

Nobody checks. Tess goes back to the numbers in her head. Peg tears off the next ticket herself.

## Act three: the other bank

### Scene 11. Kieran's bedroom, Thursday, 12:30.

Kieran is off school with a cold: red nose, a box of tissues, a hot-water bottle, his log open on the duvet.

Terry comes in with a bowl of soup on a tray, gloves on, the way he always handles food.

TERRY: Chicken. Your nan's. I only stirred it.

KIERAN: (thick) Will you catch it?

TERRY: Me? Not had a cold since '21. Only perk.

KIERAN: What are the other perks?

TERRY: That's the list, love. That's all of it.

He tucks the hot-water bottle in and goes. Rosie comes and sits on the end of the bed without being asked, the way she always has, and tucks her feet under the duvet by his.

ROSIE: Budge up. Look.

She holds out her phone. A video: an operating theatre, bright and green. A child asleep on the table, maybe six. A SURGEON in a gown with a rig on her belt stands beside him. A voice counts. The surgeon is there; then she isn't, for three seconds, just an empty space by the table with the nurses watching it; then she's back, and in her gloved hand is something the size of a walnut. The child hasn't moved. There isn't a mark on him.

KIERAN: Did it hurt him?

ROSIE: He never even woke up. She can see all of him in there, Kiz. Every bit. Heart going, blood going. And she knows which bit's wrong, and she takes it out, and comes home.

KIERAN: Does she ever turn?

ROSIE: Never. Not once. Four thousand of them.

She plays another. And another. Her face in the light of the phone is someone else's: hungry, and not for food.

KIERAN: You could do that.

ROSIE: In my sleep.

KIERAN: So why don't you?

ROSIE: They want exams. Certificates. Letters after your name. Paper.

KIERAN: You could do exams.

ROSIE: Marlows don't do paper.

She says it lightly, a joke everyone knows. Kieran pushes the soup bowl towards her.

KIERAN: Want some?

ROSIE: Making weight, me.

KIERAN: You're already the lightest.

ROSIE: Show-off, me.

She takes the phone back and puts it away.

### Scene 12. The weighbridge office, Thursday, 18:30.

Terry cooks the family's tea, sausages and mash, in his gloves, with the cordless phone clamped between ear and shoulder. He can't taste it properly; he goes by colour and smell and the clock. A pan of his own dripping gravy simmers at the back, for him.

TERRY: (into the phone) No, dripping first. Beef, if you can get it. Then the flour.

A voice on the line, faint and old, soft at the edges, from somewhere with a river in it. We can't make out the words.

TERRY: (laughing) Gin? In the gravy? You daft ha'porth. (listens) ...Go on, then. A drop.

Peg comes through with a basket of washing.

PEG: Who's that?

TERRY: Con.

PEG: Who's Con?

TERRY: Fella I talk to.

Peg rolls her eyes and goes. Terry tips a capful of gin into his gravy and stirs.

TERRY: Con says a drop of gin in it. (to the phone) I'm telling them you said so. (listens; softer) No, it doesn't taste of much. Nothing does. But it's warm.

Kieran is on the stairs in his dressing gown, blanket round his shoulders, listening.

### Scene 13. The weighbridge office, Thursday, 21:00. Hop five.

The gate shut. Kieran comes down to do his reading. Nobody is allowed to do it for him. He hauls the 20-kilo cast-iron test weight out from under his desk by its handle, which takes both hands and a lot of breath, and waddles it onto the middle of the little bridge. The printer chatters.

KIERAN: Twenty point nought nought.

TESS: Same as yesterday.

KIERAN: Same as every day. That's the point. It's got to be the same thing every night or it's not data.

He writes it in the log, tapes the ticket beside the others, and heaves the weight off the plate again. Tess, watching, doesn't help. She's proud of him and says nothing.

Rosie is rigged by the stove. The painting in her arms. Kieran stays on the stairs, blanket round him, to watch a real hop.

TESS: Three.

Rosie goes. The empty place by the stove. Tess counting. Kieran holding his breath without knowing it.

The gasp. Rosie's back, the painting in her arms, and before anyone else can speak:

KIERAN: Straight?

He says it exactly like Danny, man to man. Rosie grins at him.

ROSIE: Straight.

The window is on the right. Rosie's eyes go to the cutting. Five.

### Scene 14. The office step, Thursday, 21:30.

The yard floodlight, fog coming off the river in slow sheets, a ship's horn somewhere towards Tilbury. Tess and Rosie sit on the office step with mugs of tea. Rosie holds hers in both hands for the warmth.

TESS: Five.

ROSIE: Five.

TESS: Two more, and if she's still wrong we're another job short.

ROSIE: Then I'll do the job.

TESS: You'll do nothing till you've got some colour.

A long quiet. The horn again.

ROSIE: You ever come back wrong? When it was you.

TESS: Marlows come back straight.

ROSIE: But you. Ever.

TESS: Twelve years. Never once. I faced home.

ROSIE: Course you did.

TESS: Fourteen, I was. The week your grandad went, Nan stood me on that plate and everybody clapped.

ROSIE: I know. Nan tells it at Christmas.

TESS: Nan tells it wrong. She says I was proud.

ROSIE: Weren't you?

TESS: I was hungry.

Rosie says nothing at all. Tess puts an arm round her, the way she has since Rosie was small, and feels how little there is of her under the big jumper. Her face does two things at once, pride and worry, and she keeps both of them to herself.

TESS: Drink your tea.

Rosie holds it to her mouth, for the warmth.

### Scene 15. A tug-yard shed, a river somewhere, the same night.

Thirty seconds of a place we can't put a name to. Corrugated iron, rain loud on it. A tug's lights through the open doors, rocking on black water.

From a beam hangs an old salvage steelyard: a long iron arm, a hook, a canvas sling. A skinny LAD of seventeen sits in the sling with his knees up and his feet off the ground, grinning. An OLD MAN in a cap, seventy, slides the brass weight along the arm, a notch, another, until the arm floats level.

OLD MAN: Fifty-nine!

The shed erupts. A GIRL of thirteen bangs a saucepan with a spoon. Men whoop. The lad punches the air from the sling.

A WOMAN in her forties, with a waterman's tide book under her arm, stands by the doors and doesn't cheer. She looks at the lad's thin arms. Somewhere overhead, a baby starts to cry. She turns her head towards it, and goes up the stairs.

### Scene 16. The weighbridge office, Friday. Hops six and seven.

**Hop six. Friday, 05:30.** Rosie comes back from the black and sits down hard on the bottom stair with the painting in her lap. "Straight?" "Straight." The window on the right. The cutting. Six.

Tess crouches in front of her.

TESS: That's it. Done.

ROSIE: One more.

TESS: You're white as that tub.

She unwraps a glucose sweet from her pocket, the kind Peg keeps everywhere, and puts it in Rosie's hand.

TESS: Sugar. Go on.

Rosie crunches it.

ROSIE: Better. I'm a new woman. I'm a new woman every three seconds.

TESS: I'm serious.

ROSIE: So am I. One more. This afternoon, before his plane. For Terry's year.

Tess looks at Peg. Peg looks at the order book, and the thin envelope in the drawer.

PEG: Let her, Tess.

**The day between.** Mixers turning in the yard. The phone ringing with banks. At four the phone rings with Peg's man, and Peg takes it on the step with her back to the kitchen.

PEG: (on the phone) He's still here till seven? ...Then tell him to wait by his phone.

**Hop seven. Friday, 16:30.** The gate chained. All of them in the office now: Peg, Danny, Tess, Terry, Liam, Shaun, Kieran in his dressing gown.

Rosie in her rig. The painting against her chest.

TESS: Three. Not a tenth more.

ROSIE: Not a tenth.

*The black.* The glow. The linen, the layers, the woman at the wrong window, the painted-out hand. Rosie brings her face close to the woman's.

ROSIE: (a whisper, to her) Come on. Come round.

The count. 2.8. 2.9. She faces home.

*The office.* The gasp.

DANNY: Straight?

ROSIE: Straight. Straighter than you.

Laughter, thin. Everyone leans in. The window is on the right.

Rosie's eyes go to the cutting on the wall. She looks at it a little longer than the other six times. Then she looks at the painting and makes a face, a comic, sorry face, for the room.

ROSIE: She likes it that way.

Peg's phone rings. She listens. She says "Right," and "Thanks, Stan," and puts it down on the table.

PEG: He's gone to the airport.

### Scene 17. The weighbridge office and yard, Friday, 18:55.

Dusk over the estuary, the water gone to pewter, the bridge lights coming on across the river. The family on the office step with their tea. A plane climbs out over the water, heading east, its lights blinking.

DANNY: That's him.

PEG: You don't know that.

DANNY: It's a plane, Mum. He's on a plane.

TERRY: Bon voyage.

Nobody laughs. They watch it go until it's a light among the others.

Inside. The painting on the table, the woman at her wrong window, the brass balance in her hand. Peg takes the tea towel.

PEG: Put her away.

Rosie wraps the canvas in a clean pillowcase. For a moment she holds it against her, as she held it seven times in the black.

ROSIE: (to the painting) Just you and me, then.

DANNY: What?

ROSIE: Talking to her. She's had a long week.

Danny clips a wrist unit on, stands beside the green safe, and takes the parcel in his right hand. He shuts his eyes. His hand, and the painting with it, goes into the safe door the way a hand goes into water, and he feels about in the dark inside, blind, patient, like a man looking for his keys in a coat pocket. He lays her down. His hand comes back empty. Nobody in this family has used the key to that safe in years.

Later. The office dark again. Rosie alone at the dresser, where Terry's newspaper still lies folded at the advert he looked at on Kieran's birthday: RESTORATION. SEVENTY PER CENT. OBVERSE. She tears it out along the fold and puts it in her pocket.

She picks up the office phone and dials the number on it.

ROSIE: (low) Hello. I'd like to price a Restoration. For my uncle. (listens) Terry Marlow. It's his birthday. (listens) Tomorrow's lovely. Ten's lovely.

She puts the phone down and looks at the cutting on the wall. Then she takes it down, and pins it back up, straighter.

## Act four: Harley Street

### Scene 18. A tube station, central London, Saturday, 09:40.

Rosie, in her good coat, on a long escalator going up. Along the wall the adverts slide past: a musical, a bank, a holiday. Then a poster that stops her eye: a young soldier in fatigues walking down a plague ward without a mask, beds on both sides, nurses behind him in full hoods and visors. The soldier is thin and smiling.

IMMUNE. FED. NEEDED. JOIN THE FEVER COMPANY.

Rosie turns her head to keep looking at it as the escalator carries her past.

### Scene 19. The Obverse clinic, Harley Street, Saturday, 09:55.

A Georgian door, black, with a brass plate: OBVERSE. RESTORATION. Inside, white walls, orchids, the hush of money.

Framed on the wall of the hall: a menu, cream card, gold edges, THE OBVERSE ROOM, KNIGHTSBRIDGE, 2009, TASTING MENU £800. Beside it, a framed front page of a financial paper: OBVERSE TRIPLES ON ARMY DEAL. And a large portrait, softly lit: a woman in her fifties in a white coat, calm, direct, and under it, I WAS CURED HERE. DR HELEN OSEI.

On the door to the inner corridor, a printed sign, very plain:

PATIENTS MUST NEVER TOUCH STAFF IN TRANSIT.

The RECEPTIONIST, thirties, immaculate, smiles and types with her right hand. Her left hand rests on the desk beside the keyboard, very still, its colour almost right. It's a good one. Rosie's eyes go to it and away. Nobody mentions it.

RECEPTIONIST: Name of the patient?

ROSIE: Terry Marlow. He's my uncle. It's his birthday.

RECEPTIONIST: Will he be joining us?

ROSIE: He's shy.

RECEPTIONIST: Take a seat. Dr Osei won't be long.

The waiting room is empty. Rosie sits on the edge of a leather chair with her hands between her knees.

### Scene 20. Dr Osei's consulting room, Saturday, 10:05.

A tall window onto Harley Street. A desk with nothing on it but a leaflet and a pen. DR HELEN OSEI, the woman from the portrait, older in person and kinder.

OSEI: Your uncle.

ROSIE: Terry. He's sixty-six. He turned five years ago, on a job. At work.

OSEI: Is he on the Register?

ROSIE: No.

OSEI: He should be. They'll feed him for nothing. They won't ask him how it happened. They never do.

ROSIE: We feed our own.

OSEI: Then he's lucky. Most families can't.

ROSIE: How does it work? What you do.

OSEI: He comes in in the morning and goes home in the afternoon. A clinician is with him in transit the whole way. Near him. Never touching.

ROSIE: And then what?

OSEI: And then, seven times in ten, he goes home and eats his tea.

ROSIE: But what do you do to him?

Osei smiles. She has been asked this a thousand times.

OSEI: That's what the fee pays for. Our method is our own.

ROSIE: And the other three?

OSEI: Go home as they came. We don't pretend otherwise.

ROSIE: Can they have another go?

OSEI: At the same fee.

ROSIE: You had it done. It says in the hall.

OSEI: I sat where you're sitting once. Hungry. It's not painful. I can tell him that much.

ROSIE: How much?

OSEI: A hundred and eighty thousand pounds. Seventy per cent.

Rosie nods as if that's the kind of number she hears every day. She takes the leaflet. Osei watches her fold it in half, and in half again.

OSEI: If your uncle is hungry now, don't make him wait for the money. The Register won't ask him anything.

ROSIE: We don't go to them.

OSEI: Everybody says that. Until they're hungry.

### Scene 21. The Obverse waiting room, Saturday, 10:20.

Rosie comes back through the door into the waiting room, the folded leaflet in her fist, and stops dead.

In the chair by the window, in a long overcoat that is too big for him, sits DS RAY AMBROSE, 54. Thin as a rake handle. On his knee a packet of crackers; in his hand a pocket knife, spreading white lard on one. On the low table beside him, face up, a green card with his photograph on it: HANDEDNESS REGISTER. CLAIMANT.

He finishes spreading. He looks up.

RAY: Miss Marlow.

ROSIE: You following me?

RAY: I have an appointment. I come every spring. To see if it's come down.

ROSIE: Has it?

RAY: Not yet.

He eats the cracker, neatly, catching the crumbs in his palm. It's not a snack. It's his breakfast. It's what he eats.

He tilts the packet towards her, the way anyone offers anyone a biscuit.

RAY: Want one? Don't knock it.

ROSIE: You're all right. Making weight.

RAY: So I hear.

He doesn't put the card away. He isn't ashamed of it. Rosie looks at the card, and at him, and at the knife with the lard on it. She knows exactly what she is looking at. So, now, do we: the mug of vodka on his desk, the crackers, "Lunch, Ray? Or is that it?" It was. It always is.

RECEPTIONIST: (from the desk) Mr Ambrose? Dr Osei will see you now.

RAY: In a minute.

Rosie goes.

### Scene 22. Harley Street, outside the clinic, Saturday, 10:25.

Bright, cold. Taxis. Rosie on the clinic steps, the leaflet in her pocket, saying it to herself the way Tess says numbers, as though saying it might make it smaller.

ROSIE: A hundred and eighty thousand. Seventy per cent.

Across the road, on the corner, a jeweller's: a narrow shopfront, one ring in the window on black velvet. At the kerb outside it a plain grey van with its back doors open. Two GUARDS come out of the shop carrying a steel case between them, and lock it into the van.

Rosie watches the case all the way in. She watches the doors shut, and the guards get in, and the van pull out into the traffic. Her head turns with it. It's the way she looked at the painting, the first night, in the crate.

Behind her, the clinic door opens. Ray, in his big coat, on the top step.

RAY: Tell your uncle it's seventy per cent.

She turns.

RAY: They don't tell you how.

He goes back in. The door closes on the brass plate.

Rosie looks down the street. The grey van's brake lights at the corner, turning left. She pulls up her collar and starts to walk after it.

**END OF EPISODE.**
