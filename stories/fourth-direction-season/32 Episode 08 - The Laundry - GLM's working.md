## Draft by GLM

# Episode 8: The Laundry

*With their secret supply cut off, the Marlows find out who has really been feeding them.*

---

## Cold open

### Scene 1. The weighbridge office, Grays, Sunday, ten past seven in the morning.

Late May. Rain on the blinds. The lorries are parked up; the hatch is down; the public door is bolted. On the counter, where Terry set them a fortnight ago, one clean spoon still stands beside one clean, empty tub.

At the stove PEG (72) fries eggs and bacon, because the house's straight mouths must eat, and the smell of it fills the room. At the table sit the three it can't feed: TERRY (66), ROSIE (22) in Danny's old jumper, and DANNY (41), his right hand still bandaged into a mitten of dressing. KIERAN (12) is at his end of the counter with a bowl of cereal, watching everything.

Between the three sits the last white tub. Its lid is off. Terry, in his yellow gloves, divides it with a teaspoon, level measures, counting aloud the way he counts everything now.

TERRY: ...four. *(tap, tap on the second bowl)* ...four. *(tap, tap on the third)* ...and four.

PEG: (not turning round) That's nobody's dinner, Terry.

TERRY: It's a third each, Peg. It's fair.

Twelve level spoonfuls apiece. Rosie picks up her spoon. Danny looks at his bowl a moment. Then he slides it, slowly, across the table to his daughter.

ROSIE: Dad.

DANNY: I'll sort it.

He gets up, takes his coat off the hook, and goes out into the yard, and the door swings shut behind him on the smell of bacon. Rosie looks after him. Then she pulls the bowl back and eats, a spoonful at a time, making it last.

Sixpence stands in the middle of the floor and turns his head from Terry to Rosie to the door Danny went through, growling his low steady note at each in turn.

TERRY: Morning to you too.

At the counter, Kieran has stopped eating his cereal. He looks at Danny's empty chair, and at his cousin making two bowls of nothing last, and he doesn't say anything either.

Peg dishes the eggs onto two plates. The pan pops. Rosie eats her third and her fourth spoonful, and the rain comes down the window, and nobody at that table can think of one thing to say.

**MAIN TITLE: SECONDS**

## Act one: Go again

### Scene 2. The weighbridge office, the same morning, eight o'clock.

Terry opens the fridge to put the milk back, and stands looking in. The shelves are full: eggs, bacon, a joint for tomorrow, butter, a bowl set with beef dripping, his bottle of gin. On the side behind him sits his blue enamel pot, empty, lid off, the spoon dry in it.

TERRY: Full fridge. (He shuts the door.) Nothing in it for half of us.

He spoons dripping onto a cracker and eats it standing, and takes a nip of gin in his tea. Rosie does the same, without either of them commenting on it, the way you copy a man at the next desk. Danny is out in the yard with the crews.

Peg comes in from the yard with eggs in her apron, and Rosie stands up. She has been waiting for the room to be quiet.

ROSIE: Nan. Unlock the safe.

PEG: No Marlow crosses till I say.

ROSIE: Ten seconds. Down the pit, where nothing can go wrong. You counting. Terry timing. Seven in ten says I'm at this table tomorrow eating a fried egg, and you're watching me.

PEG: Ten seconds is what did it.

ROSIE: It's what undoes it.

PEG: Nine turned you, and you want ten. (She puts the eggs in the bowl, one at a time.) Your uncle went in on two seconds eleven times and come out no worse. Two seconds is the only number this family has ever counted and come home on. That's what I know, girl. Ten seconds I know an' all. I know a girl that went in on nine.

ROSIE: The book said—

PEG: The book burned. (She cracks the last egg.) You'll go again proper. Hops, like your uncle. That cure's ours, and it's never cost us a rig yet.

Rosie looks at her grandmother, and there is nothing left in the house to argue with, and they both know it.

**The yard, later.** Rosie catches Danny by the vans, out of the window's line, and keeps her voice low.

ROSIE: Me and you could hold on. In there. Down the pit, one go. You'd take my wrist, I'd take yours. One of us comes back straight, and it's over.

Danny looks at her a long moment. Then he raises the bandaged hand, the mitten of dressing, into the space between them.

DANNY: Coin says which of us comes back with one hand. (beat) Not you.

He goes off towards the batching plant, calling something to the crews, and leaves her standing between the vans with her own hands tucked under her arms.

### Scene 3. The flooded gravel pit, Monday, ten to six in the morning.

Mist on the water, the colour of the sky. The pontoon rocks. Rosie stands on it rigged, in Terry's belt with the strap knotted short twice, the brass disc at her throat. PEG is on the pontoon in her coat, the safe key on a bootlace round her neck, and the glucose meter in her apron pocket, which she brings everywhere now. TERRY is on the shore with the stopwatch, white to the lips from the walk down.

Peg folds a tea towel and ties it over Rosie's eyes.

ROSIE: It's two seconds, Nan.

PEG: You'll do it proper or not at all. In how you train. Out how you train.

PEG: Two. Face home. Out.

Rosie flexes her fingers once, the way every Marlow does, and steps off the world. There one moment; then mist where a girl stood.

TERRY: (from the shore) One. Two.

The gasp. Rosie back on the planks, blindfold still on, facing where she came from.

PEG: Straight?

ROSIE: Straight.

PEG: Again.

And again. The hops run like a rosary through the grey morning: two seconds, face home, out, straight, again. Peg warms Rosie's hands between them between goes. Nobody offers her anything to eat between the hops, because there is nothing to offer, and the absence of it is louder than Tess's meals ever were. Seven. Nine. Eleven. Rosie's lips move under the blindfold, counting the count she can't hear.

Fourteen. She comes back onto the planks and stands swaying, and Peg unties the tea towel, and the morning is suddenly very bright on the water.

Peg takes the meter out of her apron, pricks Rosie's finger without asking, and squeezes the bead onto the strip. They wait. The beep.

**LO.**

Peg looks at the little screen a long time. Then she puts it away as if it were a clock that reads the hour wrong.

PEG: There.

TERRY: (from the shore) Fourteen.

PEG: Again tomorrow.

Rosie folds the tea towel into a square and hands it back to her grandmother like a wage packet.

ROSIE: Nothing.

TERRY: No. Nor me.

Rosie looks out over the pit, at the flat grey water going nowhere, and flexes her fingers once. No tingle. Nothing at all.

### Scene 4. The weighbridge office, Monday evening.

Supper cleared. Terry ties off the bin liner and lifts it out, and something in it clinks. He puts it back down, opens the liner over the bin, and digs.

Two rounds of sandwiches in foil, made by him that morning. An apple. All of it untouched, buried under tea leaves and eggshell.

He stands with the apple in his gloved hand a long while. Sixpence watches him from the basket. Terry looks up the stairs, where a boy's feet have just gone over the landing.

He dries the foil on the towel, rewraps the sandwiches, sets the apple on top, and covers the parcel with a clean tea towel on the side.

**Tuesday morning.** Kieran comes down for school. Terry is at the stove with his back to the room. Peg is at the hatch. Nobody looks — and while nobody looks, the parcel goes from the side into Kieran's school bag, between the exercise book and the PE kit.

At the door Kieran feels the weight of it, opens the foil a crack, and looks at the bin. Then at Terry's wide yellow back at the stove. He closes the bag, and goes out to the bus, and neither of them has said a word.

## Act two: The van

### Scene 5. Tilbury High Street, Tuesday, ten to seven in the morning.

A launderette on the corner, plate glass steamed white, a sign: DILYS — SERVICE WASHES. Across the road, in a lay-by, sits Tess's car. It has sat in lay-bys for a fortnight. A foil blanket is folded on the back seat; her toothbrush stands in the cup holder; two parking tickets live under the wiper, unpaid. TESS (38) is in the driver's seat with a thermos, the pencil behind her ear.

At seven sharp Dilys unlocks, raises the shutter, and comes back with the plain white van. She loads in six empty crates and pulls out into the traffic.

Tess starts the engine and follows.

Through Tilbury's back streets, past the ferry, to a parade with a bookmaker's in the middle of it. Beside the bookmaker's, an alley; up the alley, an iron staircase; at the top of the stairs, a green door.

Dilys parks, goes up the iron stairs, knocks. The green door opens a hand's width — we see nobody — and Dilys goes in. She comes down twice, a stack of white plastic tubs in her arms each time, batch codes printed on the lids, and stacks the crates in the van.

Tess's lips move. *Number fourteen. Top flat. Green door.*

She expects the van to turn for Grays. It doesn't. It turns east along the river road, and Tess follows it out of Tilbury, through Purfleet, to a yard behind palisade fencing at the edge of the marshes: a long plain warehouse, a gatehouse, green-overalled staff, and a sign.

**HANDEDNESS REGISTER — REGIONAL STORES.**

The van pulls up at the gatehouse. Dilys hands the two crates in through the hatch. A man in green signs a clipboard. The van comes out empty and goes back towards its washes.

Tess sits in the layby opposite with the engine ticking, watching lorries go in and out — one every ten minutes or so — and her lips move the whole time, and this time the numbers do come off them.

TESS: (to nobody) One gatehouse. Two cameras on the fence.

She turns the car round and drives back to Tilbury, and parks where the launderette van was, at the bottom of the iron stairs.

## Act three: The flat

### Scene 6. The flat above the bookmaker's, Tilbury, Tuesday, eleven in the morning.

Tess climbs the iron stairs and knocks on the green door.

It opens. DS RAY AMBROSE (54) stands in it in his shirtsleeves and braces, thin as a rake handle, unhurried, as if he has been expecting this knock for five years.

RAY: Miss Marlow.

TESS: You knew I'd come.

RAY: I knew somebody would. (He steps back.) Mind the boxes.

The flat is one room and a kitchen, and it is as tidy as an evidence store. The bed is made square. On the windowsill, a window box with three tomato plants, staked. Along one wall, stacked in rows, a dozen white tubs, their batch codes to the front. By the sink, a crate of vodka. In the fridge door, visible as he fetches her nothing to drink, a block of lard with a butter knife standing in it. On the table, squared to the edge: seven buff files, fanned like cards; a building-society passbook; and a folder with one word on the tab, in his own hand. **MARLOW.**

Tess stands in the middle of the room and looks at the tubs, and her lips don't move at all, which is worse.

TESS: You're the Laundry.

RAY: Since your uncle turned. (He gestures at the table.) You've earned the sitting-down.

She doesn't sit. He does, and turns the folder towards her, and opens it.

Columns in his hand, date by date, kilo by kilo, going back twenty-four years. FRANK, in older ink: 78.0, 77.4, 76.1, down and down a page and a half, to 64.4, and then nothing. ROSIE, on the next page: 44.0, 43.6, 43.1, 41.8 — and then a blank column, week after week of it.

RAY: That's where she went inside, where I couldn't see her weigh. (beat) Three weeks after Mayfair, your mother's order doubled. I knew somebody else was eating in that house before you did.

TESS: And you never said.

RAY: Would it have worked?

Tess has no answer to that, so she goes at it sideways.

TESS: Dilys.

RAY: Dilys carries for half of Tilbury. She thinks I'm a man who buys too much of his dinner. She's never asked a question in her life. (He squares the seven buff files.) These are the ones that did it. Claimants. All seven dead. When the police lost the Register list in 2019, we handed our files back. I kept these. (beat) Dead men don't eat.

TESS: So you fed their dinners to my family.

RAY: I sold them. There's a price for it. Three hundred a day, going rate. Your mother paid it.

TESS: And the money?

He slides the passbook across. She opens it. The name on it: T. MARLOW. The column of figures under it runs down the page and onto the next, deposits, fortnight by fortnight, five years of them. No withdrawals. Not one.

RAY: Never touched a penny. It was never mine. It's your uncle's dinners, so it's in your uncle's name, and he never signed anything and owes nobody anything.

TESS: Why charge us at all?

RAY: Because your mother would sooner starve than owe anybody. (beat) That's the first thing I ever learnt about your family, Miss Marlow. I learnt it in 2002.

TESS: Marrable Street.

RAY: My first case. I was twenty-nine. (He turns the folder back to the page of older ink, and his finger goes down it, not reading, remembering.) The wall read sixty-four three, and it went on reading him a month. A little less every day. I stood in that street more nights than your mother knows, waiting for whoever had done it to come back for something. Nobody came back. There was nobody to charge. He'd just run out, a hundred foot under a road, and I watched your dad's weight spread for a month. (He closes the folder.) I wasn't watching it again.

The room is quiet. Out on the sill the tomato plants shift in the draught from the window.

TESS: You took my niece's rig off her on the A13.

RAY: I took it to keep her out of the black. Not to starve her. (beat) I'd have taken yours too, if you'd been the one driving.

TESS: And your spinner. The one that turns crossers. Six months and looks like a diet.

RAY: There's no such person. There never was. I hoped there was, for a year. (dry as paper) A man I could charge. It'd be a comfort.

TESS: Twenty-four years of our weights in your hand, and you never showed a soul.

RAY: It's evidence.

TESS: Of what?

He has no answer. He squares the folder again instead, edges to edges, and looks at it.

TESS: What happens now?

RAY: Tuesday week they walked me out of Victoria. Audit. The kitchen ran the batch codes off the lids, and the codes ran back to seven claimants dead since before the pandemic. Gardening leave. They'll charge me when it's typed up, and they'll be right. (He nods at the wall of tubs.) Everything with a code on it goes back Thursday. You watched it go, this morning, I'd think. The gatehouse at Purfleet. (beat) After Thursday I'm nobody's laundry.

TESS: There's three of them starving in Grays.

RAY: The Register feeds anybody who walks through the door. Anybody. Nobody asks how you turned. Nobody asks anything.

TESS: They won't walk.

RAY: I know. (beat) Your mother's order. A month up front, she left it on the dresser for Dilys. It's still on the dresser. Dilys was told to stop.

Tess looks at the wall of tubs that are going back. Ray follows her eyes. Then he gets up, goes to the fridge, and takes out one white tub. On the lid, in biro, in his hand: R. AMBROSE.

RAY: Mine. Today's. That one's legal, anyhow.

He holds it out. She takes it. It weighs nothing, a pound of porridge in a plastic tub, and she holds it in both hands like a tray of glass.

TESS: Five years. You never once told us to stop crossing.

RAY: People don't stop because you say.

At the green door she stops, and looks back at the window box.

TESS: You can't eat those.

RAY: No.

TESS: Then why grow them?

RAY: They need the watering. (beat) How is she?

TESS: Hungry.

He nods once, as if that were the number he had been waiting for all morning, and shuts the door. Tess goes down the iron stairs into the alley, carrying the detective's dinner.

## Act four: More

### Scene 7. The weighbridge office, Tuesday, eight in the evening.

The yard gate is shut. Inside: PEG at the stove, DANNY at the table with his bandaged hand flat in front of him, ROSIE on the bottom stair in the big jumper, SHAUN (26) on the settle with his stick, KIERAN on the camp bed by the safe with a comic he isn't reading. On the dresser, squared and untouched for a fortnight, the envelope of fifties waits for a van that isn't coming.

A knock. Terry looks at Peg. Peg looks at the stove.

PEG: (after a moment) Let her in, then.

Terry unbolts the back door. TESS comes in with the cold on her coat, and the white tub in her hands. She puts it down in the middle of the long table, in front of everybody, and Peg takes it — because it is food, and food is Peg's — and sets it in front of Rosie without a word about where it came from.

TESS: The Laundry is Ray Ambrose.

The kitchen takes that in the way a room takes bad news: in shifts.

TESS: Turned in 2015. Seven seconds, carrying a kid out of a building. When the police handed the Register list back in 2019, he kept seven files. Claimants, all dead. Their rations. He's been claiming them and selling them on at the going rate for five years. (beat) The money's in a building society in Grays. In Terry's name. He never spent a penny of it. (beat) They suspended him Tuesday week. Audit. Everything left went back through the gatehouse at Purfleet this morning. I followed the van.

TERRY: Five year. (He looks at the fridge, at his own shelf.) Five year, and my name's on it. You bought my dinners off a dead man's plate, Peg.

PEG: I bought them off a laundry.

TESS: He said you'd sooner pay a stranger than take a gift. He was right.

DANNY: He sat in this kitchen. (quiet, even) He stood at our safe and talked about a bullet, and he's been feeding us since.

SHAUN: He's a copper. They're not supposed to—

DANNY: He took my girl's rig on the A13. And put her dinners through our letterbox.

ROSIE: (from the stair, a laugh with nothing in it) He charged us.

TESS: Your nan would rather owe than be beholden.

Silence. Peg wipes her hands on her apron, goes to the fridge, and opens it, and stands in its light. Eggs. Bacon. A joint for tomorrow. Butter. Milk. Shelves of it, and not one thing in the whole box that three people at that table can eat.

TESS: (flat, like a number) We never fed our own.

Peg shuts the fridge. Then she does the small thing first: she takes the clean spoon and the clean empty tub off the counter — the ones Terry set out a fortnight ago, waiting for a delivery — opens the dresser drawer, and lays them inside, and shuts it. Everyone in the room watches her admit it.

Then she picks up the chalk.

On the board, under LIAM 62.4 and its star, in her hand: **REGISTER DEPOT — PURFLEET.**

PEG: You stood at their gate today.

TESS: One gatehouse. Two cameras on the fence. A lorry in and out every ten minutes. It's rations in cases, on pallets, in a shed with no windows.

PEG: Long crossings, then. A fortnight Friday. We'll feed our own.

The word *long* goes round the table like a dropped glass. Rosie stands up on the stair.

ROSIE: You know what long does.

PEG: I know what empty does. (beat) I've buried both.

ROSIE: There's a door in Whitechapel. It's free. It asks nothing—

PEG: We don't go to them.

ROSIE: Nan—

PEG: They wrote to this house once before. Food in one hand and a police interview in the other, and I put it in that drawer, and I'd post it back to them tomorrow. (She looks at Rosie, and her voice doesn't rise, which is the worst of it.) I've fed this family off one bridge for thirty year, girl. Off my own scales and my own jobs. We'll not be beholden to a building with a counter in it.

TESS: Mum. Ten seconds down the pit—

PEG: You don't plan for this family. Said and done.

PEG: Hands up, them that's for the depot.

Danny's good hand goes up first.

DANNY: I said I'd sort it.

Shaun's hand. Terry's gloved hand.

TERRY: I'll drive. (beat) Nobody's sending me in at five and a half seconds. But I'll drive.

From the camp bed, Kieran's hand comes half up off the comic. Peg looks at it. It comes down again. Nobody asks him. Rosie's hand never moves. Tess isn't asked at all.

PEG: Carried.

She hangs the safe key round her neck on its bootlace, and Tess, watching her mother's face do exactly what it did when the Twitch was on the radio, picks up her coat.

At the door Tess stops once.

TESS: The Register feeds anybody. He said the words I'd say, so I'll say them: walk her in.

PEG: Out, Tess.

Rosie, alone at the table with the detective's dinner, pulls the tub towards her, peels the lid, and eats.

### Scene 8. A car park by the river, Grays, Tuesday, eleven at night.

Tess's car, engine off, lights off, the river talking to itself beyond the wall. The orange glow of the town on the clouds. She has the phone in her hand and the leaflet on her knee with the Register's number on it, and she dials.

TESS: (into the phone) Is that the night line? (beat) Aye. I want to report a turned woman. Unclaimed. She's starving. (beat) No, she'll come. She's past saying no. (beat) Marlow Aggregates. The public weighbridge, Grays. (beat) No. No name of mine to give.

She rings off. She sits with both hands on the wheel, and her lips move, and no number comes off them, the way no number came at the pit when her son asked how long.

TESS: (to the windscreen) You're not going in the black for a box of porridge.

### Scene 9. The yard, Marlow Aggregates, Wednesday, ten past six in the morning.

A green minibus comes up the lane and turns in the gate, HANDEDNESS REGISTER and a small printed hand on its side. Three crew in plain green smocks get out: two women, one man, a cool bag, a clipboard. No police. The CHIEF (50s, kind, no nonsense) knocks at the office door, and when Peg opens it, doesn't come in.

CHIEF: Somebody rang the night line. We don't take nobody that won't come, Mrs Marlow. There's a bed, there's a kitchen, and no lock on the door either way.

Behind Peg, at the table, the family has assembled itself out of the morning: Danny, Terry, Shaun, Kieran in his school shirt. Rosie is already on the stairs in her coat. She heard the diesel.

She looks at the chalk board. PURFLEET. Then at Peg.

PEG: You'll be fed.

ROSIE: Aye.

Rosie kisses Danny's cheek. She touches the top of Kieran's head, the way Tess does, which makes nobody look at anybody. At the gate, past the green minibus, stands Tess's car, and Tess beside it, come to see it done.

Rosie stops level with her.

ROSIE: Nine point four, Tess.

Tess takes it the way you take a slap you've been owed for years, standing still, hands at her sides. The minibus rolls out of the gate, over the big bridge; the printer chatters and prints its weight; and at the office window a boy with an exercise book watches it go.

### Scene 10. The Register ward, Whitechapel, Wednesday afternoon.

A converted warehouse by the railway: high windows, beds with lockers, a kitchen you can smell from the door — gravy, and something bread-like, and hot fat. Every door in the place stands open or swings on a spring. There are no locks anywhere a person goes.

Except one. At the end of the corridor, a plain steel door with a keypad, and a small green light above it. The pantry.

A NURSE (30s, tired, cheerful) walks Rosie in with a folder.

NURSE: No locks, see. You can walk out the day you can feed yourself out there, and not a day sooner. (She hands over a small device, a meter with a lancet, in a box.) Yours to keep. Finger, mornings. It tells you if you're eating. First number'll frighten you. Don't let it.

Rosie turns the T-meter over in her hands, and pockets it like something valuable.

ROSIE: Who sees all this? The list. Who gets it?

NURSE: Police don't get the list, love. Not since 2019. (beat) A court can, if there's ever a case. That's the whole of it.

Rosie stands very still. Two years of a family's one reason, gone in a sentence, and she doesn't say a word about it either.

In the next bed, a FIREMAN, DEN (50s), sits with both hands bandaged to the fingertips, holding a teaspoon in the join of his thumbs like a man in cuffs.

DEN: Leeds. Went in for a lad off a tower-block stair. Seven seconds, both of us under a slab. (He raises the bandaged hands.) Burns unit tried grafting me three times. Nothing of theirs'll grow on me now. Wrong-way flesh. So it's dressings, and dinner, and patience.

He is going home on Friday. His wife has had the pantry lesson; his first ration box is on the chair, packed and labelled. He talks about his allotment the whole way through the meal: the leeks standing, the greenhouse glass he reglazed one-handed, first asparagus next spring, if he's still fed come winter.

Later: the ward is short-staffed, a man fitting in bay four, the nurse running. Rosie watches Den try and fail to peel the tape off her own knuckles with two spoon-hands. She gets up. She gowns, and gloves, from the box on the wall.

NURSE: (passing, not stopping) For him, love. Not for you.

Rosie unpicks the dressings on Den's burned hands, cleans, dresses, and tapes them down, quick and light and exactly right, the way the surgeons do it in the videos she has watched four thousand times.

DEN: Steady hands.

ROSIE: Show-off, me.

### Scene 11. The weighbridge office, Grays, Wednesday night.

Dark, the printer's green light. Kieran stands on the little bridge in his pyjamas, tears off the ticket, and reads it by the glow. 38.3.

In the exercise book, in the second column, the one headed *me*, under 38.6, he writes it. Book under the pillow. Face to the safe.

## Act five: Two weeks on

### Scene 12. The Register ward, Whitechapel, two weeks later, mid-morning.

Sunlight down the high windows. Rosie, in a t-shirt with her sleeves pushed up, stands on the ward's platform scale by the door while the nurse moves the weights.

NURSE: Forty-six two.

Rosie's lips move.

ROSIE: Ten point eight.

NURSE: That's kilos, girl.

ROSIE: I know what I said.

There is colour in her, and weight on her, and she carries the trays for two men who can't use their hands. Den's bed is stripped and made square. On her locker, propped against a water jug, a postcard: a field of leeks. On the back, in a bandaged printing: *FIRST BOX OPENED. LEEKS STAND WELL. DEN.*

A RECRUITER (50s, good suit, lanyard, kind and brisk as a travel agent) sits on the edge of Rosie's bed with a folder open on his knee.

RECRUITER: Fever Company. You'll know the poster. (beat) You can't catch what they've got, and they can't catch you. There are wards on three continents nobody has walked down since the turn. We pay proper. We feed you for life. There's a pension.

ROSIE: Where?

RECRUITER: Where we send you. It's a company, love, not a club.

ROSIE: Do your people get their ten seconds?

RECRUITER: In turn. The Army buys them, same as the rations.

ROSIE: (beat) Leave the card.

He leaves the card. She puts it in the pocket of her coat, which hangs on the bed end, and doesn't look at it again.

### Scene 13. The ward common room, that night.

The ward telly, sound up for once, because half the ward has gathered. Beds wheeled in, the nurse with her tray stopped mid-round.

On the screen: a press conference. Behind the scientists, a chart of jagged lines, regular as fence posts.

TV NEWSREADER: ...the gravity group at Reading, the same team that heard March's silent merger, now says the tremors in the Thames estuary since April were not events at all, but strokes. Read together on the estuary's instruments, they spell a molecule. A sugar — of the other hand.

TV SCIENTIST: (mild, astonished, on a riverbank with a cylinder behind him) It writes, and then it stops, and then it writes again. And the first stroke — the first stroke was on the fourth of April.

**Intercut: the weighbridge office, Grays.** The radio on the counter, the same story in radio words. Danny, at the table with tea in his good hand.

DANNY: April the fourth. That's the morning the cups walked off Mum's dresser.

PEG: (counting the float) Then somebody next door's got a lot to say for themselves.

Kieran, at his end of the counter, writes on a fresh page of the exercise book: *4 APRIL — FIRST STROKE.* He looks up at his graph on the wall: the flat line, the step, the circled wobble. He looks at it a long time, the way you look at a face you half know, and then the news moves on to football.

**The ward, lights out.** The beds, the breathing, the pantry's green light down the corridor. Rosie's phone lights up on the pillow, and she reads it under the blanket.

**KIERAN:** *Nan's doing the depot tonight. They won't let me go.*

Rosie sits up. In the dark she sits a long moment, still as a sum being done. Then she is out of bed. The T-meter goes into her coat pocket. The coat goes on over her pyjamas. She looks back once, at Den's stripped bed, and at the ward door — which has no lock, and never has had.

She walks out through it.

**Last shot:** her bed with the blanket thrown back. The corridor beyond, dark, and the small green light over the pantry going on blinking, the only lock in the building, keeping the food.

**Cut to black.**

**END OF EPISODE 8**

## Cold open

### Scene 1. The weighbridge office, Grays, Sunday, ten past seven in the morning.

Late May. Rain on the blinds. The lorries are parked up; the hatch is down; the public door is bolted. On the counter, where Terry set them out on Friday, one clean spoon still stands beside one clean, empty tub.

At the stove PEG (72) fries eggs and bacon, because the house's straight mouths must eat, and the smell of it fills the room. At the table sit the three it can't feed: TERRY (66), ROSIE (22) in Danny's old jumper, and DANNY (41), his right hand still bandaged into a mitten of dressing. KIERAN (12) is at his end of the counter with a bowl of cereal, watching everything.

Between the three sits the last white tub. Its lid is off. Terry, in his yellow gloves, divides it with a teaspoon, level measures, counting aloud the way he counts everything now.

TERRY: ...four. *(tap, tap on the second bowl)* ...four. *(tap, tap on the third)* ...and four.

PEG: (not turning round) That's nobody's dinner, Terry.

TERRY: It's a third each, Peg. It's fair.

TERRY: Morning to you too.

At the counter, Kieran has stopped eating his cereal. He looks at Danny's empty chair, and at his cousin making two bowls of nothing last, and he doesn't say anything either.

Peg dishes the eggs onto two plates. The pan pops. Rosie eats her third and her fourth spoonful, and the rain comes down the window, and nobody at that table can think of one thing to say.

RAY: The Register feeds anybody who walks through the door. Anybody. Nobody asks how you turned. Nobody asks anything.

TESS: They won't walk.

RAY: I know. (beat) Your mother's order. A month up front, she left it on the counter for Dilys. It's still there. Dilys was told to stop.

Tess looks at the wall of tubs that are going back Thursday. Ray follows her eyes. Then he gets up, goes to the fridge, and takes out one white tub. On the lid, in biro, in his hand: R. AMBROSE.

RAY: Mine. Today's. That one's legal, anyhow.

He holds it out. She takes it. It weighs nothing, a pound of porridge in a plastic tub, and she holds it in both hands like a tray of glass.

TESS: Five years. You never once told us to stop crossing.

RAY: People don't stop because you say.

At the green door she stops, and looks back at the window box.

TESS: You can't eat those.

RAY: No.

TESS: Then why grow them?

RAY: They need the watering. (beat) How is she?

TESS: Hungry.

He nods once, as if that were the number he had been waiting for all morning, and shuts the door. Tess goes down the iron stairs into the alley, carrying the detective's dinner.

## Act four: More

### Scene 7. The weighbridge office, Tuesday, eight in the evening.

The yard gate is shut. Inside: PEG at the stove, DANNY at the table with his bandaged hand flat in front of him, ROSIE on the bottom stair in the big jumper, SHAUN (26) on the settle with his stick, KIERAN on the camp bed by the safe with a comic he isn't reading. On the counter, squared and untouched since Friday, the envelope of fifties waits for a van that isn't coming.

A knock. Terry looks at Peg. Peg looks at the stove.

PEG: (after a moment) Let her in, then.

Terry unbolts the back door. TESS comes in with the cold on her coat, and the white tub in her hands. She puts it down in the middle of the long table, in front of everybody, and Peg takes it — because it is food, and food is Peg's — and sets it in front of Rosie without a word about where it came from.

TESS: The Laundry is Ray Ambrose.

The kitchen takes that in the way a room takes bad news: in shifts.

TESS: Turned in 2015. Seven seconds, carrying a kid out of a building. When the police handed the Register list back in 2019, he kept seven files. Claimants, all dead. He's been claiming their rations and selling them on at the going rate for five years. (beat) The money's in a building society in Grays. In Terry's name. He never spent a penny of it. (beat) They suspended him Tuesday week. Audit. I followed Dilys this morning. What the van could carry went back through their gatehouse at Purfleet. The rest goes back Thursday.

TERRY: Five year. (He looks at the fridge, at his own shelf.) Five year, and my name's on it. You bought my dinners off a dead man's plate, Peg.

PEG: I bought them off a laundry.

TESS: He said you'd sooner pay a stranger than take a gift. He was right.

DANNY: He sat in this kitchen. (quiet, even) He stood at our safe and talked about a bullet, and he's been feeding us since.

SHAUN: He's a copper. They're not supposed to—

DANNY: He took my girl's rig on the A13. And put her dinners through our letterbox.

ROSIE: (from the stair, a laugh with nothing in it) He charged us.

TESS: Your nan would rather owe than be beholden.

Silence. Peg wipes her hands on her apron, goes to the fridge, and opens it, and stands in its light. Eggs. Bacon. A joint for tomorrow. Butter. Milk. Shelves of it, and not one thing in the whole box that three people at that table can eat.

TESS: (flat, like a number) We never fed our own.

Peg shuts the fridge. Then she does the small thing first: she takes the clean spoon and the clean empty tub off the counter — the ones Terry set out on Friday, waiting for a delivery — opens the dresser drawer, and lays them inside, and shuts it. Everyone in the room watches her admit it.

Then she picks up the chalk.

On the board, under LIAM 62.4 and its star, in her hand: **REGISTER DEPOT — PURFLEET.**

PEG: You stood at their gate today.

TESS: One gatehouse. Two cameras on the fence. A lorry in and out every ten minutes. It's rations in cases, on pallets, in a shed with no windows.

PEG: Long crossings, then. Wednesday fortnight. We'll feed our own.

The word *long* goes round the table like a dropped glass. Rosie stands up on the stair.

ROSIE: You know what long does.

PEG: I know what empty does. (beat) I've buried both.

ROSIE: The clinic woman said it to my face, Nan. The Register asks nobody nothing. There's a bed, there's a kitchen—

PEG: We don't go to them.

ROSIE: Nan—

PEG: They wrote to this house once before. Addressed to Frank, it was. I put it in that drawer and I never opened it, and I'm not starting on their word now. (She looks at Rosie, and her voice doesn't rise, which is the worst of it.) I've fed this family off one bridge for thirty year, girl. Off my own scales and my own jobs. We'll not be beholden to a building with a counter in it.

TESS: Mum. Ten seconds down the pit—

PEG: You don't plan for this family. Said and done.

PEG: Hands up, them that's for the depot.

Danny's good hand goes up first.

DANNY: I said I'd sort it.

Shaun's hand. Terry's gloved hand.

TERRY: I'll drive. (beat) Nobody's sending me in on my seconds, mind. But I'll drive.

From the camp bed, Kieran's hand comes half up off the comic. Peg looks at it. It comes down again. Nobody asks him. Rosie's hand never moves. Tess isn't asked at all.

PEG: Carried.

She tucks the bootlace with the safe key back under the neck of her apron, and Tess, watching her mother's face do exactly what it did when the Twitch was on the radio, picks up her coat.

At the door Tess stops once.

TESS: The Register feeds anybody. He said the words I'd say, so I'll say them: walk her in.

PEG: Out, Tess.

Rosie, alone at the table with the detective's dinner, pulls the tub towards her, peels the lid, and eats.

### Scene 8. A car park by the river, Grays, Tuesday, eleven at night.

Tess's car, engine off, lights off, the river talking to itself beyond the wall. The orange glow of the town on the clouds. She dials.

TESS: (into the phone) Is that directory? I want a number. Handedness Register. It'll be a night line. (beat; then, to a new voice) Is that the night line? (beat) Aye. I want to report a turned woman. Unclaimed. She's starving. (beat) No, she'll come. She's past saying no. (beat) Marlow Aggregates. The public weighbridge, Grays. (beat) No. No name of mine to give.

She rings off. She sits with both hands on the wheel, and her lips move, and no number comes off them, the way no number came at the pit when her son asked how long.

TESS: (to the windscreen) You're not going in the black for a box of porridge.

### Scene 9. The yard, Marlow Aggregates, Wednesday, ten past six in the morning.

A green minibus comes up the lane and turns in the gate, HANDEDNESS REGISTER and a small printed hand on its side. Three crew in plain green smocks get out: two women, one man, a cool bag, a clipboard. No police. The CHIEF (50s, kind, no nonsense) knocks at the office door, and when Peg opens it, doesn't come in.

CHIEF: Somebody rang the night line. We don't take nobody that won't come, Mrs Marlow. There's a bed, there's a kitchen, and no lock on the door either way.

Behind Peg, at the table, the family has assembled itself out of the morning: Danny, Terry, Shaun, Kieran in his school shirt. Rosie is already on the stairs in her coat. She heard the diesel.

She looks at the chalk board. PURFLEET. Then at Peg.

PEG: You'll be fed.

ROSIE: Aye.

Rosie kisses Danny's cheek. She touches the top of Kieran's head, the way Tess does, which makes nobody look at anybody. At the gate, past the green minibus, stands Tess's car, and Tess beside it, come to see it done.

Rosie stops level with her.

ROSIE: Nine point four, Tess.

Tess takes it the way you take a slap you've been owed for years, standing still, hands at her sides. The minibus rolls out of the gate, over the big bridge; the printer chatters and prints its weight; and at the office window a boy with an exercise book watches it go.

### Scene 10. The Register ward, Whitechapel, Wednesday afternoon.

A converted warehouse by the railway: high windows, beds with lockers, a kitchen you can smell from the door — gravy, and something bread-like, and hot fat. Doors stand open or swing on springs.

At the end of the corridor, one plain steel door with a keypad, and a small green light above it. The pantry.

A NURSE (30s, tired, cheerful) walks Rosie in with a folder, talking without slowing. Halfway along she keys the pantry door open, goes in, comes out with a tray, and lets the door click shut behind her without once thinking about it — the only lock in the building, and it is hers to work.

NURSE: Bed's that one. Kitchen's through there — eat at four in the morning if you like, nobody'll stop you. You can walk out that door the day you can feed yourself out there, and not a day sooner. (She hands over a small device, a meter with a lancet, in a box.) Yours to keep. Finger, mornings. It tells you if you're eating. First number'll frighten you. Don't let it.

Rosie turns the T-meter over in her hands, and sets it on the locker shelf, in the middle, where she can see it.

ROSIE: Who sees all this? The list. Who gets it?

NURSE: Police don't get the list, love. Not since 2019. (beat) A court can, if there's ever a case. That's the whole of it.

Rosie stands very still. Two years of a family's one reason, gone in a sentence, and she doesn't say a word about it either.

In the next bed, a FIREMAN, DEN (50s), sits with both hands bandaged to the fingertips, holding a teaspoon in the join of his thumbs like a man in cuffs.

DEN: Leeds. Went in for a lad off a tower-block stair. Seven seconds, both of us under a slab. (He raises the bandaged hands.) They tried donor mesh on me three goes. Somebody else's skin won't take on me — wrong-way flesh, they said — and I'd nowt of my own to spare. So it's dressings, and dinner, and patience.

He is going home on Friday. His wife has had the pantry lesson; his first ration box is on the chair, packed and labelled. He talks about his allotment the whole way through the meal: the leeks standing, the greenhouse glass he reglazed one-handed, first asparagus next spring, if he's still fed come winter.

Later: the ward is short-staffed, a man fitting in bay four, the nurse running. Rosie watches Den try and fail to peel the tape off his own knuckles with two spoon-hands. She gets up. She gowns, and gloves, from the box on the wall.

NURSE: (passing, not stopping) For him, love. Not for you.

Rosie unpicks the dressings on Den's burned hands, cleans, dresses, and tapes them down, quick and light and exactly right, the way the surgeons do it in the videos she has watched four thousand times.

DEN: Steady hands.

ROSIE: Show-off, me.

### Scene 11. The weighbridge office, Grays, Wednesday night.

Dark, the printer's green light. Kieran comes down the stairs in his socks and stops, and looks up at the chalk board: LIAM 62.4, and the star beside it, nobody having wiped it.

Then he steps onto the little bridge in his pyjamas, bare feet on the steel, tears off the ticket, and reads it by the glow. 38.3.

He sits on the edge of the camp bed and opens the exercise book. In the second column, the one headed *me*, under 38.6, he writes it. Then he looks from the star on the board to the number in the book and back, a boy doing a sum nobody set him. Book under the pillow. Face to the safe.

## Act five: Two weeks on

### Scene 12. The Register ward, Whitechapel, Wednesday 10 June, mid-morning.

Sunlight down the high windows. Rosie, in a t-shirt with her sleeves pushed up, stands on the ward's platform scale by the door while the nurse moves the weights.

NURSE: Forty-six two.

Rosie's lips move.

ROSIE: Ten point eight.

NURSE: That's kilos, girl.

ROSIE: I know what I said.

There is colour in her, and weight on her, and she carries the trays for two men who can't use their hands. Den's bed is stripped and made square. On her locker, propped against the water jug, a postcard: a field of leeks. On the back, in a bandaged printing: *FIRST BOX OPENED. LEEKS STAND WELL. DEN.*

A RECRUITER (50s, good suit, lanyard, kind and brisk as a travel agent) sits on the edge of Rosie's bed with a folder open on his knee.

RECRUITER: Fever Company. You'll know the poster. (beat) You can't catch what they've got, and they can't catch you. There are wards on three continents nobody has walked down since the turn. We pay proper. We feed you for life. There's a pension.

ROSIE: Where?

RECRUITER: Where we send you. It's a company, love, not a club.

ROSIE: Do your people get their ten seconds?

RECRUITER: In turn. The Army buys them, same as the rations.

ROSIE: (beat) Leave the card.

He leaves the card on the bed. She looks at it a moment. Then she opens her locker, stands the card inside on the shelf, and shuts the door on it.

### Scene 13. The ward common room, that night.

The ward telly, sound up for once, because half the ward has gathered. Beds wheeled in, the nurse with her tray stopped mid-round.

On the screen: a press conference. Behind the scientists, a chart of jagged lines, regular as fence posts.

TV NEWSREADER: ...the gravity group at Reading, the same team that heard March's silent merger, now says the tremors in the Thames estuary since April were not events at all, but strokes. Read together on the estuary's instruments, they spell a molecule. A sugar — of the other hand.

Rosie's hand goes out to the locker shelf, to the T-meter. She turns it over in her fingers. *A sugar, of the other hand.* She looks at the little machine that reads her blood, and she puts it back on the shelf.

TV SCIENTIST: (mild, astonished, on a riverbank with a cylinder behind him) It writes, and then it stops, and then it writes again. And the first stroke — the first stroke was on the fourth of April.

TV NEWSREADER: Tomorrow's papers have already settled on a name for it.

A mock-up front page fills the screen: **COME ROUND FOR TEA.**

Down the ward a patient says it out loud — "Come round for tea" — and somebody laughs, and somebody else tells him to shush, and the nurse laughs into her hand. Rosie doesn't.

**Intercut: the weighbridge office, Grays.** The radio on the counter, the same story in radio words, and at the end of it: "...a name the morning papers have already settled on." Danny, at the table with tea in his good hand.

DANNY: April the fourth. That's the morning the cups walked off Mum's dresser.

PEG: (counting the float) Then somebody next door's got a lot to say for themselves.

Kieran, at his end of the counter, writes on a fresh page of the exercise book: *4 APRIL — FIRST STROKE.* He looks up at his graph on the wall: the flat line, the step, the circled wobble. He looks at it a long time, the way you look at a face you half know, and then the news moves on to football.

**The ward, lights out.** The beds, the breathing, the pantry's green light down the corridor. Rosie's phone lights up on the pillow, and she reads it under the blanket.

**KIERAN:** *Nan's doing the depot tonight. They won't let me go.*

Rosie sits up. In the dark she sits a long moment, still as a sum being done. Then she is out of bed. The T-meter comes off the locker shelf and goes into her coat pocket. The coat goes on over her pyjamas. The recruiter's card stays on the shelf where she stood it, beside Den's postcard. She looks back once, at the stripped bed, and at the ward door — which has no lock, and never has had.

She walks out through it.

**Last shot:** her bed with the blanket thrown back. On the locker shelf, the field of leeks and the recruiter's card standing where she left it. Down the corridor, dark, the small green light over the pantry goes on blinking, the only lock in the building, keeping the food.

**Cut to black.**

**END OF EPISODE 8**

## Plants and payoffs

**Paid off here, from episodes 1 to 7**
- **The Laundry.** Dilys's Tuesday van, "Your whites, Terry" / "Bless you, Dilys" (1, 2, 5); the batch codes on the lids (1); "He'll want it, Peg. He always does" / "He'll get it" (2); the clean spoon and tub set out for a delivery (7); the month-up-front envelope still squared on the counter (7). The Laundry is Ray: the van, the thinness, the Register card and "what was left of him" (1), the lard and vodka (1, 2) all planted. What he keeps on the family is what a man could keep from a ledger and a gatehouse: dinners out, money in, and the dates the mouths changed — the seventh of April (after the meter), a fortnight Tuesday (after the grab) — so he knew before they did, and said nothing, which is his flaw and theirs in one.
- **"We feed our own" (1, 3, 4, 6)** pays off as Tess's "We never fed our own", said in front of a full fridge. Peg's cover action — putting the waiting spoon and tub away in the drawer — is the pride breaking.
- **Ray's puzzlement that Rosie looked well (5):** the doubled line in his ledger. **His 2002 first case (1, 3)** becomes the shut back of the folder and the month he watched; the figure stays in his head, where episode 9's phone call is waiting for it.
- **The spinner (3, 4, 7):** cut here, as agreed — "There's no such person. There never was."
- **Terry's eleven hops (1, 2, 6, 7):** fourteen more, run lovingly by Peg at the pit, the meter answering LO. The family's cure was always the same, and it never was one.
- **"I gave you nine" (6)** returns as Rosie's parting shot at the gate: "Nine point four, Tess."
- **Peg's "No Marlow crosses till I say" (7):** she says it — for hops only — and wears the safe key on a bootlace from this episode on.
- **The grip (2, 4, 6):** "Me and you could hold on, in there", and Danny's ruined hand refusing: "Coin says which of us comes back with one hand. Not you."
- **The estuary events: the quake (4), "another next-door event" (5), the metronome spacing (7)** pay off as strokes that began on 4 April, read by the team that heard March's silent merger (1), and named by the tabloids: COME ROUND FOR TEA.
- **The Fever Company poster and "Not had a cold since '21" (2)** become the recruiter; **Obverse's Army rations (2)** stand behind "the Army buys them, same as the rations."
- **Rosie's surgeon videos (1, 2)** become the dressings on Den's hands — the thing she wants, done for nothing, on a ward.
- **Osei's "The Register won't ask him anything" (2)** is what Rosie throws at Peg, and what the ward makes true.
- **The 2019 letter in the drawer (1, 5)** is answered by a nurse in one line: "Police don't get the list, love. Not since 2019." The family doesn't hear it; Rosie does, and says nothing.

**Planted here, for later**
- **The T-meter, hers to keep (9):** on "a sugar — of the other hand" her hand finds it and turns it over; it leaves with her in her coat pocket. The instrument that will settle what the crab is, and what two meters say about one drop of blood.
- **The ward's one locked door (9, epilogue):** custody by pantry, built into the building before anyone says it; the nurse keys it without thinking. Rosie's unlocked walk-out is caused by an idea, not a door.
- **The depot recon (9):** gatehouse, two cameras, a lorry every ten minutes, cases on pallets — and Peg's chalk: PURFLEET, Wednesday fortnight, "long crossings, then". Shaun, Terry driving, Danny's "I said I'd sort it."
- **Ray suspended, the passbook, the shut 2002 papers (9, 10, epilogue):** his number will go on the roll; the money in Terry's name stays untouched; whatever the back of that folder holds, Tess will have to ring him to get it — "What did my dad weigh?"
- **The message: a sugar of the other hand, first stroke 4 April, COME ROUND FOR TEA (9, epilogue):** Rosie pockets the T-meter while the ward sleeps; nobody says the connection, and the family files the date under "next-door event" while the answer to Liam's two hundredths sits in the same sentence.
- **The recruiter's card (epilogue):** stood inside her locker, and left there when she goes — the road not taken, kept.
- **Rosie's 46.2, and "Ten point eight":** the lightest courier's arithmetic lives on, and her seconds have grown.
- **Kieran's 38.3**, taken with his eyes on LIAM 62.4 and its star, and his page *4 APRIL — FIRST STROKE*, the first thing he has ever written that isn't homework (9: the log proves the step; 10: he writes what the adults remember).
- **Terry's rewrapped lunch:** he knows a fourth person in the house is refusing food, and says nothing, as he never said anything about the pot.
- **Tess alone with her sums (9, 10):** the call she makes without asking Rosie; her lips moving with no number on them, twice.

**Knowledge at the end.** The family knows the Laundry is Ray, that he is turned, that the money sits in Terry's name, that the Register feeds anybody. Rosie alone knows the police can't read the list. Everyone knows the message began on 4 April and what the papers call it. Only the audience knows about Kieran's column, the rewrapped lunch, the shut back of Ray's folder, and where Rosie has gone.

**Numbers used.** Canon only: £300 a day, five years; seven claimants; 2019, 2015, 2002; fourteen hops of two seconds; 38.6 to 38.3; 9.4; 46.2 on the ward scale ("ten point eight"); LO. New, and logged: the ledger's dates — one mouth from 2021, two from Tuesday 7 April, three from Tuesday a fortnight before the visit (12 May); the return — two crates Tuesday, the rest Thursday; Ray walked out Tuesday week (19 May); the vote's "Wednesday fortnight" = Wednesday 10 June, which dates scenes 12 and 13 and the depot raid; twelve spoonfuls each. Talk only: a month up front.

**Gaps.** *Closes:* who fed them; why the van stopped; what the estuary events were writing, when it began, and what the country calls it. *Opens:* will the depot raid work, and what it will cost; where Rosie is going on the last train; what next door is, and what it wants (a wonder).
