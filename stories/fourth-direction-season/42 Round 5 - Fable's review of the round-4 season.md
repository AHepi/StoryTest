# SECONDS, round 4: editorial review

*Read: the owner's brief, the second feedback, the notes on The Catch, the master plan (version 3), and the whole season in `round-4/full-season.md`, start to finish. Every quotation below is copied from that file. Where I name a scene I use the season's own scene numbers. I read a script; nobody has watched a film of it, so anything I say about pace is judged from the page.*

A few words I cannot avoid. A **plant** is a small detail put in early so that a later surprise is fair; its **payoff** is the moment it is used. A **cold open** is the scene before the title. An **action line** is the description between the dialogue, which the camera has to turn into a picture. A **beat** is one small moment inside a scene.

---

## 1. Verdict

SECONDS is a ten-episode crime season about an Essex family of thieves who can step off the world for a few seconds, whose one boast is that "Marlows come back straight", and who never write anything down. Its best crosser is starving at the family table while the family cheers every kilo she loses. The season follows that one lie through concrete, a war with the family across the river, a dead boy, a lying scale, a founder's hidden turn, and a sea eight metres above the kitchen lino.

It achieves what the owner asked for. The core message is never said and is everywhere in the events: the family's kindest habits (the lucky breakfast, the cheer, the drawer, "we feed our own") are what kill it, and the way out is not one clever heroine but a whole family, and the family across the river, laying their numbers side by side. The twists are fair; I checked every one against its plants and found them planted. Every invented law from The Fourth Direction is true on screen and does plot work, and I found no scene that breaks one. The world is ordinary and works. Nothing echoes The Catch. No banned word reaches the screen.

**Is it finished?** As a season of scriptments, yes: the spine, the reversals, the characters and the ending all hold, and the numbers add up down to the tenth. What remains is five substantive faults, none of which moves a scene, and a handful of line-level quibbles. The most important fault is at the very end: Terry chooses exile over a cheaper cure he reads aloud himself, and the page never weighs the two. That is one exchange to write. After that, I would send this to a writers' room.

## 2. How it keeps the owner's rules

### The core message, shown and never stated

The message ("a culture that never questions anything dooms its people") lives in things that happen, not in things said:

- The cheer. Rosie's weight goes 44.0, 43.6, 43.1, 41.8 across three episodes, each number chalked with a star ("That's a Marlow"), and only at the meter does the family learn it was starvation.
- The drawer. Three letters, unopened: the Ministry's 2001 offer that would have fed Frank, the 2019 letter saying the police can no longer read the Register, and the April order to re-verify the scales. Each would have saved someone. Each is found too late, by someone opening a drawer.
- The lucky breakfast. "Everything in you counts, breakfast too" is said in episode 1 by Peg, and in episode 5 Peg puts the plate in front of Liam after he has been weighed: "The camera stays at the table. He eats every bite. Sixpence's tail slows, and stops."
- The ferry. Two families, twenty-five years, and one sheet of paper between them: "Not one under three seconds. Not one, either side of the river, not in twenty-five years."
- The control group. The Aherns keep the same code with one difference (Maggie opens her post), and at the end Declan is still cheering a thirteen-year-old on a bathroom scale with the front page under it, unread. "Always dooms" is earned by showing the same habits in a second family.

The nearest anyone comes to saying the theme is Terry in the loft, "Nobody ever asked why," and Maggie on the ferry, "Nobody counted her, Tess. That's what I've come to say." Both are ordinary sentences about particular people, and both are grief rather than a lecture. (Terry's line is repeated by Tess later; see quibble 2.)

The brief also warned against teaching the opposite by accident. It does not. Tess is wrong three times on screen (Mayfair as one 9.4-second trip, Liam's two tenths, handing Rosie to the ward alone), and asking is never punished: Rosie breaks "face home" and finds the food; Kieran's homework proves the scale lied.

### The twists are fair

I checked each of the sixteen twists in the plan's schedule against the season as written. All are planted before they land, and none is given away early beyond what the plan intends (sharp viewers ahead, ordinary viewers level). The strongest plant-and-payoff chains:

- **Rosie turned since Mayfair** (lands ep 3, scene 16). Planted: the crisp packet looked at "a beat too long" (ep 1, sc 1); Sixpence's growl, "He's jealous. She's been away" (ep 1, sc 3); the jar she cannot open (ep 1, sc 13); "Goes through me like water, love. Since I turned" said by Terry over a cake Rosie then reaches for (ep 1, sc 13); seven glances at the catalogue cutting (ep 2); the lard "for cold-water swimming" (ep 3, sc 11); the dodge from Tess's hand (ep 3, sc 10).
- **The cure is time** (ep 6). Planted: the surgeon at three seconds, "She's done four thousand. Never turned" (ep 1, sc 10); Terry's "Eleven times I went again. Two seconds a go. Hungry every time after" (ep 1, sc 8); seven three-second hops that do nothing (ep 2).
- **The family's own scale killed Liam** (ep 9). Planted: Kieran's test weight going from 20.00 to 19.78 the morning of the quake (ep 4, sc 1); "It's chipped. Tip it up and look" (ep 4, sc 11); Mick's "Your bridge has had me two hundred light" (ep 4, sc 11); the council envelope into the drawer (ep 4, sc 11); Osei's "Sixty-one kilos, seven hundred grams" against "Point nought on my bridge" (ep 6, sc 8); Peg's brass balance, "Never told me a lie" (ep 1, sc 12).
- **Frank turned and hid it** (ep 10). Planted: "Your grandad was fifteen stone when the black took him" said directly under the painted rule A FIFTH OF A SECOND A KILO UNDER A HUNDRED (ep 1, sc 6), a sum any viewer can do; Rosie's falling curve; the jam jar Peg opens laughing while Rosie "watches her laugh and says nothing" (ep 1, sc 13), replayed as Frank's look in ep 10, sc 3; Ray's "I met what was left of him" (ep 1, sc 18).

The twists are also hard to swap. If Frank's secret were anything but a hidden turn, "make weight" would still stand; if the Laundry were anyone but Ray, "we feed our own" would not turn inside out; if a spinner had really turned Aoife, the war would not fold back onto the family's own concrete.

One detail deserves praise for being right in a way that is easy to get wrong. The little bridge lies by 1.1 per cent about everything standing on it, yet the three minus tickets (−59.0, −46.2, −58.6) all print the *true* mass, and the steelyard and the ward scale agree with them. That is correct: a body in the black pulls the plate up with its own gravity, which the dig next door does not change, so the lying scale tells the truth only about people nobody can see. Tess says exactly this: "Those two are the only true numbers it's printed since April, and they're both of people nobody could see" (ep 9, sc 11).

Two places lean on the rules without quite breaking them; both are listed below (substantive 3, quibble 1).

### No echo of The Catch

I checked the season against the owner's list. No mint and no taste or smell test that settles anything (Danny's "What's it taste—" is cut off on purpose in ep 9, sc 9). No backwards print seen through a turned character's eyes: Rosie's "New roll's printed backwards, Nan" is a spoken joke, and the camera then shows Mick's ticket "the right way round". No dishes, flask, mould or growth: mirror life is gloves, gel, "HAND: T", gowns and a drain. No kind figure from next door, no ship, no child's drawing, no toy with a letter. No hands meeting on glass. No lift or lift engineer; jobs are "dips". No gravity needle showing a visitor live: every such reading is a ticket found in the morning, and the episode 6 ambush is played blind on the wall clock. The bite has no light and no gauge and is never called a reserve or a spare. The one faint echo is a single word, "Charge is sound" (quibble 3).

### No banned word

I searched the season for the owner's list and their relatives. "Question" appears only in its ordinary sense ("Then comes the question this family asks before it asks whether you're hurt"); "settled" only as "Tomorrow's papers have already settled on a name for it". No conjecture, criticism, error, refute, falsify, fallible, explanation, authority, optimism, infinity, "nothing is settled", "question everything", "the problem of". Nobody makes a speech about asking questions; the questions on screen are "How much?", "Why's it a kilo out?", "What did my dad weigh?", "How do you know it's not the weight?"

### Not a dystopia

The world is pleasant and works. Trading Standards hangs a certificate with a little hammer. The Register is a health service that feeds anyone who walks in and asks nothing; a fireman goes home with a ration box and a postcard of leeks. The BBC reports next-door events between the weather and the football. A Post Office clerk warns a girl kindly about a form. Only the two crossing families, across one river, never check, and they are the only people who cross for long.

## 3. What works best

- **The ritual and its death.** "Straight?" "Straight." is asked after every return for eight episodes, and it is the family's whole belief in two words. In episode 9 it dies on screen: Peg's meter reads LO on Shaun, and he says, "I knew before we were out the marsh. I said it anyhow." In episode 10, when Ray comes back, "Nobody asks him anything" and Kieran holds out the meter instead.
- **The lucky breakfast** (ep 5, sc 13). The audience has known since episode 1 that "everything in you counts, breakfast too". Peg's love is the murder weapon, and the scene never says so: "Liam picks up his fork. The camera stays at the table. He eats every bite."
- **The two hundredths** (ep 9, sc 12). The sum is done on the back of the unopened letter: "The lie on its own, he makes it. The breakfast on its own, he makes it... Either one, he makes it. Both, he doesn't. By two hundredths." Then Peg rubs out 62.4 and writes 65.1: "There's your number, love." The world's change and the family's habit kill the boy together, which is exactly what the brief asked for.
- **The loft** (ep 7, sc 4). Family stories come apart one at a time ("That's Southend pier, that photo"), and Lee's weights on the roll go down and back up: "It's on the paper, Terry. It was on the paper the whole time." Then Tess's hand stops on the box marked 02 and puts the lid back on. That is the culture in one gesture.
- **The ferry** (ep 7, sc 7 to 11). Two weighers, one sheet, and the first thing a Marlow ever writes on purpose is written with Maggie's pencil because "Mine's sharp." Maggie's "Nobody counted her, Tess. That's what I've come to say" is the season's plainest grief.
- **Ray.** Everything about him is particular: the lard "It's all that sticks", the ledger of dinners ("It's what there was to count"), the tomatoes he cannot eat ("They need the watering"), and the joke at the one moment the family is holding its breath: "Eleven year I've wrote back to front so you lot could read me. Give us a fortnight."
- **The crab and the two meters** (ep 9, sc 9). "Two meters. Same blood. Both of them right." Next door is proved by an instrument and a man's own hunger, never by taste, and Terry "pushes his empty plate out into the middle of the table... the way a man asks for more."
- **The baking tray** (ep 10, sc 9). The door is found with flour, a ruler, tap water and a salt pot, by three adults laying three memories side by side: "Our field. It never fell. It walked out of their sea onto our field."
- **Small perfect lines.** Terry on the Twitch: "Somebody next door's dropped the good china." Tess on why she counts when nobody can hear: "So someone's counting." Rosie on the Deep: "I didn't go to be turned back, Kiz. I went shopping." Peg at the police counter, of the painting: "Mind, her window's on the wrong side. She came back like that. She's staying like that."
- **Danny's vote** (ep 10, sc 14). "Burn." Then he looks at Kieran's second column, "A boy's careful pencil, going down all spring, in a house that never noticed," and says, "No. Give." The reason is on the page and nobody speaks it.

## 4. The problems, most serious first

### SUBSTANTIVE 1. Terry chooses exile over a cheaper cure he reads aloud himself, and the page never weighs the two

*Episode 10, scene 8; with episode 7, scene 12, and episode 9, scene 10.*

**What is there.** At the night of writing Terry reads his own odds: "Here, a five-second hop: one in six, each go. There: five in six, better if I'm quick. A handshake: one in two, and a hand." He refuses the handshake ("I cook with these"), rolls a die, pockets it, and goes next door.

**The problem.** For a turned man, a five-second hop at home costs nothing but the risk of running out: a slide leaves him as he is, a turn cures him, and he can go again. Tess said so out loud in episode 7: "We've got rigs. Rosie. Tonight. Terry at five, and again, and again." Terry heard her and answered, "Eleven goes, that was. Eleven. I could've been eating at this table five year since." So by his own reading, six goes in the pit gives him about a two-in-three chance of eating Peg's fry-up at his own table; one step next door gives him five in six of being fed among strangers, with no sensible way back. He takes the second, and nobody at the table, in a season that has just taught everyone to count, asks "Why not six goes at five, Tel?"

**Why it matters.** This is the season's use of the owner's required law, "exile is a cure", and the one character who takes it does so against the better bet he has just read out. A careful viewer will feel the seam. The decision was made emotionally in episode 9 ("That crab's the first thing that's ever moved my number... I'm going, Peg"), before the odds were written, and the page never revisits it once they are. His nature (he "gives up gently", he is the family's quiet shame) can carry the choice, but only if the two options are set against each other where the audience can see them. The die is a lovely beat, but a die is a coin, not a reason.

**Fix.** One exchange in scene 8, after he reads his odds. Give the hop route its real cost and give exile its real pull, in Terry's own words. For instance: Tess: "Five seconds, six goes. Two in three you're eating here by Monday." Terry: "Six goes to the last of my count, love, at my age, with your mother counting, and the rigs go to the police Sunday night. Or one go, and I land the right way round." And the want underneath it, in a cook's plain sentence rather than a speech: "Five year I've been the wrong hand at this table. Over there I'm not." Then the die. Peg's "Terry. Turned. Twenty twenty-one" already sits in this scene; let it come *before* his answer, so that even acknowledged, he still goes, and the choice gains weight. (A smaller version of the same gap: Ray gets one go at "About four in ten" in scene 10, and nobody says what happens if it does not come up. One line, "And if it don't, we go again before the warrant," covers it.)

### SUBSTANTIVE 2. Nobody ever says who sent the bullet, and the threat-then-plea order is never squared

*Episode 4, scenes 9, 13 and 14; episode 5, scenes 1 and 2; episode 6, scene 4; episode 7, scene 11.*

**What is there.** Friday at 3 a.m. Maggie says, "Ten seconds under a road, Joe. Let them count that. (beat) One of these nights it'll be more than they've got." That night, at 02:04, someone stands in the black by Peg's safe and leaves a bullet standing on the painted balance pan. Saturday morning Maggie rings Tess: "The box I'll buy back. Name a price and I'll row it over tonight." The bullet is later tied to Joe by weight and tow, but not to a sender. In episode 6 Maggie says, "A thing in that safe gets read," which implies she knows the route worked. On the ferry Tess asks straight out, "And the boats? The bullet?" and gets, "Con's dead and your boy's dead and my girl's a fridge door. Nobody's square."

**The problem.** The page holds two readings and settles neither: Maggie sent the bullet (she knows the safe gets read; she sends Joe by the same route with a letter two weeks later), or Declan ordered it and Joe carried it (Declan is the one who says "Then we'll have it back off them" and "Not enough"). Under the first reading Maggie sends a bullet to stand a foot from a child's bed at 2 a.m. and then rings at 8 a.m. to buy pills back politely; nobody, least of all Tess, remarks on it. Under the second, the "one right person" on the Ahern side is clear of it and the page should say so. The Ahern kitchen scenes never mention the bullet at all.

**Why it matters.** Maggie is the season's mirror for Tess and the only adult who changes across the river. Whether she sent a bullet into a boy's bedroom is not a small thing to leave to inference, and a viewer who is tracking the war's tit-for-tat (a bullet, then a plea, then Con dead, then the boats) will notice the one link nobody owns.

**Fix.** One line on the ferry, where Tess already asks. Either: "Dad's. Joe carried it. I rang you six hours after to buy back what he'd just threatened you for. That's the house I keep." Or, if the writers want Maggie to own it: "Mine. I sent a bullet to a boy's bed and rang for pills at breakfast. I've not squared that either." Either reading works; the page must pick one.

### SUBSTANTIVE 3. Danny's "early tingle" does not hold as a clue to the lying scale

*Episode 6, scenes 1 and 2; episode 9, scene 11.*

**What is there.** The tingle is taught as coming "about two seconds before you're done" (Rosie, ep 5, sc 11; Tess, ep 6, sc 2: "It comes two seconds before you're done"). After the grab Danny says, "Two point four. (beat) It was fizzing before I ever touched him... Tingle came early. That's the grab." In episode 9, when the flour proves the scale lies, Danny pays it off: "The bridge has had me at two point four since April." Tess: "Two point two." Danny: "I was fizzing before I ever touched that lad." The episode notes call this "the sum the audience can already do."

**The problem.** The sum goes the wrong way. With a believed budget of 2.4 seconds the tingle starts at 0.4 seconds; with the true 2.2 it starts at 0.2. Either way it starts before Danny could possibly have reached Joe, hovered "one beat, two beats", and grabbed. So "fizzing before I ever touched him" is what he would feel whether the scale lied or not, and no crosser can tell 0.2 from 0.4. Worse, the cold open tells us he had already crossed six times on the same sum: "Six nights he has stepped in at four past by the clock and found the black empty. Six crossings, two seconds each, off the plate, and nobody told." On every one of those nights the fizz came at once too. He should know it always does.

**Why it matters.** It is presented twice as evidence, once as a wrong guess and once as a payoff, and a viewer who has been taught to count (which is the whole point of the season) will catch that it proves nothing. The flour on the brass balance already proves the lie completely, so the tingle line adds a false clue to a true one.

**Fix.** Cut "Tingle came early. That's the grab" from episode 6 and "I was fizzing before I ever touched that lad" from episode 9. Keep Danny's "The bridge has had me at two point four since April" / "Two point two", which is the true and chilling sum (he had been crossing with a fifth of a second to spare for seven nights and nobody knew). If the writers want a physical beat in episode 6, let Tess do the arithmetic on the six nights instead: "Six nights on two point four you never had."

### SUBSTANTIVE 4. Kieran knows the Nobody Tide to the minute, which contradicts episode 1 and blunts the Ahern books

*Episode 7, scene 5; against episode 1, scene 3; and episode 10, scene 9.*

**What is there.** Episode 1 establishes that the Nobody Tide is not in the ordinary tables: Mick says, "River's at Nobody low, Peg. Ferry's sat on the mud and the tables say high water." The plan says the watermen shrug at it and only the Aherns keep tables for it, and the whole of episode 10's door depends on Maggie's books being the record. Then in episode 7 Tess asks, "When's Nobody low tonight?" and Kieran answers, "Ten past eleven. She's on the mud by then. Swims about midnight." Asked how he knows: "It's my homework. The tide's in my graph. (beat) The real tide's in the tables, but the tables don't say which moon it runs to."

**The problem.** His graph shows no tide (a flat line, a step, and a wobble; in episode 2 he admits "Not yet"), and "the tables" have just been established as blind to this tide. A twelve-year-old predicting to the minute a tide the Port cannot predict is a contradiction between two scenes, and it quietly undercuts the one thing the Aherns bring to the finale. (In episode 10, scene 9, "Next Nobody low's five fifty-two" is fine, because Maggie's book lies open on the table in front of him.)

**Why it matters.** It is a small scene, but it touches a world rule that the ending uses, and the scene's real job (Kieran lending Tess the −59.0 ticket, "You'll bring it back") does not need the tide at all. The deckhand already gives Tess the times at the pontoon: "She takes the mud at ten past eleven, and she won't swim till midnight."

**Fix.** Cut Kieran's tide lines and Tess's question; open the scene on "I want your minus-fifty-nine. Just for tonight." In episode 10, let him read it off Maggie's book aloud ("Maggie's book says five fifty-two") so his knowledge has a source on the table.

### SUBSTANTIVE 5. Peg knows the Army's delivery day with no source

*Episode 8, scene 7.*

**What is there.** After Tess's recon ("One gatehouse. Two cameras on the fence. A lorry in and out every ten minutes"), Peg chalks the raid: "Long crossings, then. Wednesday fortnight. That's when the Army's lot goes in. Shed's full then."

**The problem.** Nothing on the page tells Peg when the Army consignment arrives at a Register depot she has never been to. Tess did not see it; Ray would not help a raid; Con, who might have known the boats, is dead. The date sets the raid, which sets Rosie's walk-out, the depot's wall log, and the investigators' Saturday visit, so a fair amount hangs on a fact that arrives from nowhere.

**Why it matters.** It is knowledge without a cause, in a family whose whole tragedy is not checking where its numbers come from. A careful viewer will ask, "How does she know that?"

**Fix.** Give it to the hatch. Lorry drivers eat bacon rolls in Peg's kitchen every morning and haul to every yard in Thurrock. One line from Mick in episode 8, scene 7 or earlier: "Purfleet's shut to us Wednesday fortnight, Peg. Army's in. Whole shed." Then Peg's "That's when the Army's lot goes in" is her using the one thing she has always listened to.

### QUIBBLE 1. Tess steps out on the pavement from the basement (ep 6, sc 6)

She drops a floor by count ("0.4... 0.9... 1.3... 1.7. The cabinet"), takes the binder at 2.2, and then: "Home is one pull, the strongest thing in the world. She faces it and steps out onto the pavement." No trip back up. Every other crossing returns "the way she came" and takes as long; Osei's wall reads her at 2.6, which leaves four tenths for a climb that took her over a second going down. Nothing depends on it, but the season's own crossing grammar makes it noticeable. Fix: "2.2... up the way she came, 2.5. She faces home and steps out onto the pavement."

### QUIBBLE 2. "Nobody ever asked why" is said twice (ep 7, sc 4 and sc 11)

Terry says it in the loft, at the dust, and it is the season's closest approach to a stated theme; it lands because it is grief. Tess repeats it on the ferry ("Nine. Nobody ever asked why."). Once is enough; the second time it starts to sound like the moral. Cut Tess's.

### QUIBBLE 3. "Charge is sound" (ep 6, sc 5)

Terry checking Tess's rig: "Charge is sound. Collar's sound. You want the strap seen to after." The rig having a "charge" is the one word in the season that faintly recalls The Catch's charge reserve. The plan bans the word for the bite, not the rig, so this is not a breach; but the word does no job the strap and collar are not already doing. Fix: "Belt's sound. Collar's sound."

### QUIBBLE 4. Terry's 70.6 is never printed on screen (ep 10, sc 8; epilogue, sc 18)

The epilogue frames "Terry's last ticket — 70.6 — and under it, in Peg's hand: 71.4 on Maggie's. Went next door." At the night of writing only "TERRY: Seventy-one four" (the steelyard) is spoken, and only Kieran steps from the board onto the little bridge for a second number. Fix: after the steelyard, "Terry steps onto the little bridge. 70.6. Kieran writes them both."

### QUIBBLE 5. "Two years of a family's one reason" (ep 8, sc 10)

An action line, so it never reaches the screen, but it is wrong: the nurse has just said "Not since 2019", which is seven years, and the family's rule is twenty-five years old. Fix: "Seven years of a family's one reason" or "Twenty-five years of a family's one reason".

### QUIBBLE 6. Osei "took me ten seconds deep" (ep 6, sc 8)

"One of their clinicians took me ten seconds deep, on a rig this house had borrowed off a rescue crew." Nobody can take anyone through the black (the grip rule; the protocol sheet says "No contact"). The line reads as guiding by hand. Fix: "one of their clinicians sent me in for ten seconds and looked in twice."

### QUIBBLE 7. Shaun's second leg goes into a shed already flooded (ep 9, sc 5)

His first eight-second leg trips the wall ("Through the shed wall above him comes the roar") and beads fill the room. Peg sends him again ("Once more, son"), and the second leg is not shown at all; he simply "lands with the last case". Pulling through a room of settled beads is exactly the thing the season has taught us costs seconds. Nothing depends on it, but one line in the black ("The beads are to the plate now. He goes through them like a man wading") would keep the rule visible.

### QUIBBLE 8. The first stroke is a mountain and the other forty are "too small to feel" (ep 4, sc 1; ep 5, sc 4; ep 7, sc 6; ep 8, sc 13)

The April dig shakes Thurrock and takes a mountain's worth of rock; every later stroke is "Too small to feel", and Kieran's weight sits flat at 19.78 with a hair's wobble. The message is meant to stay a wonder, and it does, but the size gap between stroke one and the rest is never even remarked on by the scientists who count them. One radio phrase in episode 7 ("the first by far the largest, and quieter since") would make the seam look intended.

### QUIBBLE 9. "The knuckles of his one hand" (epilogue, sc 17)

Danny has two hands, one missing four fingers. The line reads as if he has one hand. Fix: "his right hand".

### QUIBBLE 10. Ray's overnight stay is unmarked (ep 10, sc 4 to sc 8)

He arrives at the kitchen door mid-morning Saturday and is on Maggie's steelyard at two in the morning. Nothing says he stayed. One clause in scene 7 ("Ray, who has not left, at the far end") fixes it.

### QUIBBLE 11. Tess's account of what the rolls prove is the season's one speech-shaped scene (ep 10, sc 13)

It is needed (the vote is not real until the family knows the rolls are the key to the vaults' logs and the Register's claim dates), and Tess lays it out "the only way she knows, which is objects", which helps. It is still the longest run of explaining in the season. Taste: trim by a third and let Maggie or Ray carry one of the three pieces of paper, so it is a conversation rather than a summing-up.

## What I could not settle from the page

- Whether Terry's die roll is meant to be read as *deciding* (a six and he stays for hops) or as a private joke. If it decides, substantive 1 half-answers itself and the fix is only to let the table see the two options before the roll.
- Whether the bullet's sender is meant to stay open. By the test I use (each reading held by something on the page, nothing settling it) it is a working open question, but it is one with a cost to Maggie's character, so I have reported it.
- Pace. Episode 2's seven hops and episode 10's night of writing are the two stretches most likely to sag in a cut; on the page both are varied enough (the false alarm, the meals, the steelyard arriving at 2 a.m.) that I would not touch them before a table read.
