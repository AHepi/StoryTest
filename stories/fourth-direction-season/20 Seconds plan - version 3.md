## 1. Title and logline

**SECONDS**

**Logline.** An Essex family of thieves who can step through walls has one proud boast: Marlows always come back straight, never mirror-turned. Then their best crosser starts starving at the family table while they cheer every kilo she loses. The woman who keeps the family's numbers in her head must find out what is turning them. The answer is the seconds she has squeezed out for them. The only proof is the thing the family swore never to keep, paper, and someone else's paper to check it against. And ever since crossers could come home from anywhere, the one place a turned Marlow could eat has been one world over, under their feet.

The title means the seconds a crosser gets, second helpings at Peg's table, and a second go, the family's cure. Nobody ever says so.

## 2. Genre

**Lead: Crime.** The criminals are the heroes, inside a family firm. The pursuer looks like less than he is.

- **Promise, from the Crime profile.**
  - *Crimes worth watching:* the Mayfair dip, the Marrable Street diamond room, the hand through the Ahern cold store, the Ahern tugs set adrift with every knot undone, the Obverse records room, the raid on the Register depot, and a last job into the Marlows' own stolen field. Each shows a particular Marlow mind at work.
  - *A plain-looking master pursuer:* DS Ray Ambrose, thin, vodka at his desk, taken for a drunk. He reads pour contracts, stakes out a basement, takes a rig at a traffic stop, and adds seconds to every job he leans on.
  - *A long fight between equals:* the Marlows against Ray, and against the Aherns across the river.
  - *Deception on both sides:* Rosie hides her turn; Ray hides that he feeds the family; Maggie hides her tip to the police and what Joe was doing; Kieran hides his hunger; Peg hides, even from herself, what Frank weighed.
  - *The line crossed:* Ray feeds thieves on dead people's rations; Tess goes to "them"; Danny reaches for a boy's heart and chooses something slower, and it costs him the hand.
  - *A reckoning paid in kind:* the family that kept nothing on paper is undone, and saved, by paper. At the end they could burn the rolls, lose the weighbridge and stay free. They hand the rolls over.
- **The guarantee broken: the family table is safe.** It breaks early and from inside. In episode 3 the table where Rosie is applauded turns out to be where she is starving. In episode 5 the audience watches Peg pile Liam's plate after he has been weighed, knowing since episode 1 that everything in you counts. After that every meal carries a threat, and the audience feels it at the time.
- **The genre's question:** what, if anything, would exempt someone from the rules everyone else keeps? The Marlows' unspoken answer is "People like us look after our own": no police, no clinics, no letters, no paper. The ending shows that the exemption was their cage.

**Guests.**
- **Gangster**, all season: the firm, the code at the table (Peg serves breakfast while Danny plans a war), a rival family, turncoats (Tess, then Ray), a successor (Kieran, with a pencil).
- **Detective:** Ray hunts a "spinner" who does not exist; Tess hunts the thing that turns her family. The clues are fair. The answer to "who is to blame?" is no one person, only habits.
- **Horror**, in single scenes: running out inside concrete, the flat stump, Danny's hand coming back without its fingers, a minute in black water with no up, the boy sent out two hundredths of a second short.
- **Science Fiction:** one hinge technology, the rig, and its traffic jams, followed all the way out, including one world over.

## 3. The world

### 3.1 The normal

Grays, on the Thames estuary, in 2026. Spring into summer. Container cranes at Tilbury, the QEII bridge in the haze, lorries, gravel, gulls, the Nobody Tide slapping at pontoons.

Thirty-one years ago, in 1995, something arrived in a field on Mucking Marsh. It left no crater and no trail, and it was already flickering in and out of the world. The field was the Marlows' tenancy. The Ministry fenced the field, took the object, and paid Peg and Frank Marlow enough to buy a gravel pit and a public weighbridge in Grays. Peg keeps the Ministry's receipt framed on the office wall, as a grievance: "One object, found wet, sand adhering." From the object came the **rig**: a padded belt, a collar and a wrist unit. It takes you off the world for a few seconds. Crossing is now a trade like plumbing: surgeons, rescue crews, couriers, and thieves.

Marlow Aggregates is a gravel pit, a concrete plant and a public, certified weighbridge. The weighbridge office is Peg's kitchen. It has two certified scales, and both print onto the same audit roll: the big bridge outside, which weighs lorries in twenty-kilo steps, and the platform scale in the office, which the family calls **the little bridge**. It weighs sacks, loose loads and people, to ten grams. Both print a ticket by themselves whenever a load settles on them, day or night, so that lorries can weigh after hours; at night their displays are dark and they only print. Peg's safe stands against the office wall at the end of the little bridge's plate. By day the family sells concrete to the City. By night they "dip": they cross into vaults and take things out. A big dip comes four or five times a year; concrete pays the bills.

### 3.2 What changed, and what that changed (departures and ripples)

**Departure 1: walls mean nothing, so weight means everything.**
- Gold sits under the City as dust in concrete. The concrete is often **Marlow Mix**, poured by Danny's day crew. The City buys it because thieves designed it: Danny sells by day what the family knows by night ("I know what slows a crosser down"). So the longest jobs in London are the ones the Marlows built, and not only the Marlows' jobs.
- Locksmiths became weightmen. Vaults have **weigh-walls**, which feel a body in the dark within four metres and log its mass and its time. So that licensed couriers and surgeons passing next door don't set off every vault in the City, a wall floods the goods with tungsten beads only when its reading stays up for more than three seconds. Short visits slip under; one long visit always trips it.
- Most jobs took two seconds in Frank's day. Now they take eight or nine.
- Police don't bother with cuffs on a crosser who has a rig. They take the rig.
- A thief's craft includes the knot and the seal: a crosser leaves a banded, knotted, lead-sealed crate exactly as it was, and empty, and can undo any knot without touching either end.

**Departure 2: people come back turned.**
- About forty thousand Britons have come back mirror-turned. Most turned in the unlicensed years before 2014, when anybody with a rig crossed for as long as they liked and nobody wrote down for how long. Turned, a body gets nothing from ordinary food or medicine, and no ordinary germ can touch it. It starves in about six months unless fed mirror food.
- **Licensed crossers.** Crossing surgery (taking a tumour out of a child without a cut) began only in 2014, licensed and held to three seconds from its first day, because the year before, in a private trial, a surgeon ran out with a child's tumour in her hand. Rescue crews were licensed and held to three seconds the same year; before that they crossed as volunteers and logged nothing. The limit was set as a margin against running out. Nobody counted what else it did: licensed crossers hardly ever turn. No hospital file holds a before and an after. The trade thinks them soft.
- **The Register** (the Handedness Register) is a health service people are glad of, run like a diabetes clinic. It feeds the turned free mirror rations and asks no questions, least of all how long anyone was in the black, since for most claimants the answer would confess a crime. A turned body's own gut germs are mirror life. In thirty years no mirror outbreak has been recorded, and nobody is sure why; the Register's modest rules rest on that record: claimants carry a Register hand gel, never share towels, and don't handle food for sale. Since the Register Privacy Order of 2019, the police can no longer read the list, though the Register still obeys a court. The Marlows don't know this. The letter is in Peg's drawer, unopened.
- **Passports** read HAND: C (common) or HAND: T (turned). Many countries refuse HAND: T travellers, for fear of mirror life. Airports test every traveller with a sugar strip at the gate, because a photograph can't show handedness and a strip can.
- **Obverse plc** began in 2009 as the first mirror kitchen, a Knightsbridge restaurant at £800 a meal. Now it makes the Register's rations under contract, and the Army's rations for the **Fever Company**, turned volunteers recruited from the Register who walk unmasked through plague wards. Its share price tripled on the Army contract. It sells a private cure, **Restoration**: £180,000, seventy per cent success, method confidential. Its face is Dr Helen Osei, a clinician who was cured by Restoration herself. A clinic in Lisbon sells it at half the price, to anyone who can fly there.
- **Handshakes.** Two turned people who close a hand on each other in the black always bring one of them back straight, a coin toss which, and the one who comes back straight leaves the holding hand behind in the black. It is done in back rooms. Everyone in Grays knows a one-handed man who "shook on it". Most turned people would rather be fed free than put a hand on a coin.
- **Background law, never named on screen.** A Convention of 2012 bans stepping out onto another leaf and carrying seed or living cultures through the black, with twenty years for a breach. It followed an affair in 2011 when an army carried a rival's seed bank through the black to turn it, and the seed came back turned, and so did the soldiers. A law of 2016 makes looking inside a body a search, after ten years of courts over police lookers who saw a ring in a man's stomach and could not see why he had swallowed it. On screen these are only a line or an anecdote.

**Departure 3: the neighbours, felt only as weight.**
- Everyone knows there is a world a hair's breadth away, because it can be weighed. Kieran's school poster is a map of dark matter labelled "The neighbours' galaxies: found by weighing, never by looking", with a tagline, "Sixty years we pointed dishes the wrong way."
- The BBC reports "next-door events": a black-hole merger with nothing in our sky, a gravity blip over the North Sea. A still mass next door is felt only close by. A sudden, violent event sends out a ripple that is felt everywhere.
- Thames watermen shrug at the **Nobody Tide**, a slosh on no moon's timetable. The Aherns, who run tugs, keep tables for it. It is the neighbours' moon.
- Crossers map the next world by its pull, in the dark: **the Hill**, a mountain felt over Mucking Marsh, and **the Deep**, a soft, wide pull under Grays. Far off on the other side there is a third, faint tug, **the Far**, another world beyond ours. The saying is "The Hill takes you; the Deep keeps you." It dates from the years before bites: whoever followed the Hill stepped out inside rock, and whoever followed the Deep stepped out into black water and was never seen again. Nobody goes looking for the Far. Some of the lost did.

**The main departure carries the theme.** A world where your safety depends on one number you cannot feel (your seconds, your hand, your weight) is a world that punishes anyone who will not write that number down and check it against someone else's.

### 3.3 The rules of crossing, as the audience learns them (shown, never lectured)

- **The black.** Between worlds it is black. The only light is the glow of your own field, and it dies with the field. Within arm's reach you see the inside of everything: the inside of a wall, a guard's ribs, a painting in its crate, a beating heart. Beyond that, nothing. Radio can't cross, so you are alone with your count.
- **The budget.** On a Marlow rig, every kilo under a hundred buys a fifth of a second. Everything inside your field counts: your cargo, the air in your lungs, the breakfast in your stomach. Your fingertips tingle as the time runs out. Stay past it and you don't come back: you become a weight in an empty room, slowly spreading.
- **The wall.** A weigh-wall floods after three seconds.
- **The coin.** Any crossing can bring you back turned, and you can't feel it happen. What the odds are, and what changes them, nobody outside Obverse knows.
- **Facing home.** The only thing that tells you which way is home is the tug of home. Follow a different tug and you step out one world over.
- **The bite** (taught in episode 5). Since 2016 every rig carries one by law: a brass disc on the collar with a tab you bite. Once you have stood a full minute on a world, any world, biting the tab fires a tight, blind field once more, for half your seconds. In the black it does nothing. It has no light and no gauge; nobody calls it a reserve.

### 3.4 Everyday surplus (taken for granted, never explained)

Phones have a "turned mode". The ticket printer chatters like a till. Peg's chalk board of everyone's weight hangs beside Frank's one photo, a big man, and the framed Ministry receipt. Old newspaper cuttings, framed: police forensics on Marlow jobs, "clean translation, no rotation". Peg is diabetic and pricks her finger every morning; her glucose meter lives in the cutlery drawer. Peg's old brass shop balance and its brass weights, from the farm, weigh her baking. Terry cooks in yellow washing-up gloves, a family habit nobody explains. The dog, Sixpence, an old lurcher, has always growled at Terry. Dilys's launderette van comes on Tuesdays with Terry's white tubs; the family calls the supplier "the Laundry". The tubs have batch codes printed on the lids. Lorry drivers eat bacon rolls in Peg's kitchen while they wait for tickets. Estuary mud, fog, the Tilbury ferry, a one-handed man on it. Posters on the tube: "IMMUNE. FED. NEEDED. Join the Fever Company." A minute's silence is held beside a lost crosser's weigh-wall reading, as the lost are mourned now.

## 5. The show's own new guesses

The Fourth Direction's own stipulations are all accepted (many leaves carrying worlds; the field holds atoms for seconds, longer for less mass; gravity felt at human scale; the neighbours' life is the other hand). On top of those, the show makes **six** guesses of its own. They are declared here once. No writer may add a seventh, on screen or off. Guesses 4, 5 and 6 were revised in place for version 3; none was added.

1. **Drift grows with time.** The chance of coming back turned rises with every second in the black. The table: 2 s, none ever recorded; 3 s, 1 in 5,000; 4 s, 1 in 12; 5 s, 1 in 6; 6 s, 1 in 4; 7 s, 1 in 3; 8 s, 1 in 2; 9 s, 3 in 5; 10 s, 7 in 10. Between two rows, take the nearer one, or say "about". Nobody on screen ever gives a reason for the shape; only counting reveals it, which is the book's point and the season's. (For writers only, if a reason is ever needed: a slow, steady roll in the dark, like a boat swinging on its mooring, so almost nothing turns until the roll nears a quarter turn, and the odds pass a half as it nears half a turn.)
2. **Budget.** On a Marlow rig, every kilo under a hundred buys a fifth of a second. Everything inside the field counts. Clinic rigs buy more; nobody outside Obverse knows how much.
3. **The cargo.** Whatever rides inside one field (a painting, a crab, a case of rations, a breakfast) turns or slides with its carrier, always. So nobody can turn a thing without turning with it.
4. **Next door, here.** Under Grays the neighbours have a sea whose surface stands about eight metres above our ground. Over Mucking Marsh a mountain rises out of that sea, an island: the Hill. Where the Hill's shore meets our ground level, at the low water of the neighbours' tide (which runs to the same moon as the Nobody Tide), is the Marlows' old field. On 4 April the neighbours dig a mountain's worth of rock out of their sea floor under Grays and Gravesend. With less rock beneath them, Grays and Gravesend scales read 1.1% light from then on (the source's own dial, applied here); London, further off, reads true. The sea's surface and the Hill's shore do not move. *(Revised in version 3. In version 2 the neighbours took the top off the Hill, but a mountain eight kilometres off and above Grays pulls a Grays scale sideways and slightly up, so removing it would have made the scale read heavier, not lighter.)*
5. **The bite.** Standard on every rig since 2016, by law: a brass disc on the collar, fired by biting its tab. Once a crosser has stood a full minute on a leaf, any leaf, it fires once, for half the crosser's seconds. Its field is a tight one that holds only the crosser and whatever touches them, so it can fire in water, and it is blind. It cannot fire from the black. *(Revised in version 3: it was "the spare", with a light, and could fire at once. The full minute keeps the Deep deadly for nearly everyone; the new look keeps it clear of any reserve indicator.)*
6. **The grip.** When two crossers, each wholly in the black in their own field, close a hand on each other's bare flesh, they come back opposite hands to each other, whatever the drift: if they went in alike, one of them comes back changed, a coin toss which. The one who changes leaves behind in the black whatever of them was holding or being held, cut off at a clean plane as in a partial return. A touch, a pinch or a brush of fingers slips and does nothing, and so does a grip on cloth, so the price is never less than the fingers of a hand. A hand sent from a body still on the leaf can't grip anyone in the black: its field is joined to the leaf, and it slips. A line or pole from outside a crosser's field is cut where her field ends and gives her no up, so nobody can be tethered through the black. *(New in version 2; tightened in version 3 so that a fingertip pinch, a sent hand or a tether can't become a cheap or certain cure.)*

**Derived, with nothing new added.**
- Radio can't cross, so the crosser is alone.
- A mirrored canvas can't be a forgery.
- Glucose can't revive a turned body, and a straight glucose meter reads LO on turned blood, because its enzyme never crossed. A meter made for turned blood (the Register's T-meter) reads LO on ours.
- A field fired in water fills with water and can't carry you, because everything inside the bubble crosses. Before bites, whoever stepped out into the Deep drowned ("the Deep keeps you"). Since bites, a crosser who steps out there must live a full minute in black water with no up before the bite will fire, and almost nobody can. Stepping out inside the neighbours' rock kills you ("the Hill takes you").
- The field's glow is the only light in the black, and it dies with the field. A crosser who steps out next door is in whatever light is there, and the camera never goes there.
- A hand sent through a wall stays fixed to a body still on the leaf, so it has an up and never turns. It is also blind, because the eye has to be inside the bubble to see. Thieves send hands to take by feel; surgeons must go in bodily.
- In the black a knot has a way out it never had, so a crosser can undo any knot without touching either end.
- A turn can't be felt. Afterwards the turned person sees the world reversed at once, but no looking can tell her whether she flipped or the world did. Anyone else can tell by comparing her with something that never crossed: a meter, a catalogue photo, a heartbeat beside their own. A turned reader sees print the right way round only in a mirror. A turned person who has trained herself to write so that straight people can read it will write in mirror script the day she is cured.
- A weighbridge or platform scale measures weight, the pull on what stands on it, so a mountain's worth of rock taken from under it next door makes it lie. A platform scale also feels a body standing in the black over it: the body pulls the plate up, so the scale prints the body's true mass as a minus. A rig counts mass, a beam balance or steelyard compares mass, and a weigh-wall feels a body's own pull, so none of those lies.
- A still mass next door is felt only close by, because its pull fades with the cube of distance. A sudden, violent event sends out a ripple that travels and is felt everywhere, as the silent merger was. (The source's own examples force this: a merger next door heard by detectors, and a test next door felt by every gravimeter on Earth.)
- Mirror germs can't be recognised or fought here. How well they could grow here nobody knows: in thirty years no outbreak has been recorded, and that record, not physics, is why the world's rules are modest. A whole mirror food web (sea water, weed, a crab) is what the scientists fear most. To the neighbours, our life is the mirror life.
- Because cargo turns with its carrier, a thief can't turn an enemy's food without turning with it, and food could be turned only by carrying it on long crossings, which licensed rigs never make. So mirror food is built by chemistry, as far as anyone knows. What Obverse does is left open.
- The step onto the neighbours' shore is itself a crossing, so it is itself a toss, and its odds depend on how long it takes. A crosser finds a tug she has followed before faster than one she never has. A turned crosser who turns on the way arrives straight, starving there and a risk to them.
- Nobody can guide another crosser through the black by the hand, because the grip would turn one of them.
- The neighbours' sea rises and falls to the same moon that makes the Nobody Tide.
- Because there are more leaves than two, the black has more than one tug besides home, and the pull of a leaf further off is faint.

**On screen.** Guess 2 and the lore of the Hill, the Deep and the Far appear in episode 1. Guess 1 is glimpsed in episodes 1 and 2 (the surgeon on TV who has "never turned"; Terry's eleven failed hops; the painting's seven) and stated in episode 6 (the protocol). Guess 3 appears in episode 1 (the painting turned with Rosie) and episode 4 (Tess's "Then you'd be eating it"), and pays off in episode 9 (Shaun's case). Guess 4's Hill and Deep are lore in episode 1; the April dig is episode 4; the sea is episode 9; the door is worked out on screen in episode 10 from records shown in episodes 1, 7 and 9. Guess 5 is taught in episode 5 and used in episodes 9 and 10. Guess 6 is planted in episode 2 (the clinic sign and the receptionist's hand), stated in episode 4 (Tess and Rosie), paid in episode 6 (Danny's fingers), refused in episode 8, and priced on the roll in episode 10.

## 6. The culture that never questions: the Marlows

The Marlows are warm, funny, loyal and admired in Grays. Their code is spoken, never written, and every rule was right once.

| Rule | Why it was right | How it grows the doom |
|---|---|---|
| **Nothing on paper** | Paper is evidence; its fibres even show police whether a thief turned | No pattern across years can be seen, so each turning feels like one person's bad luck. The vaults' weigh-walls log every body's time and mass, but a wall logs a body, not a name, and only the family's rolls could put names to them. Since only thieves cross for long, and thieves keep nothing, nobody in the country has read what long crossings do |
| **Never go back twice** | In Frank's day a second visit walked thieves into a police stake-out, so a Marlow takes the whole haul in one trip | A weigh-wall floods only when a body lingers past three seconds, so one long trip trips the wall that two short ones slip under. And since the odds of turning grow with time, one nine-second trip turns far more people than three short ones. Admired, and now it costs twice |
| **Make weight** | Lighter means more seconds; Peg made it law after Frank "ran out because he was fifteen stone" | Thinness is cheered, so a starving crosser looks like the most devoted one. It doesn't cause the turning; it hides it. And a crosser made to weight is sent with no real margin (Frank, Liam) |
| **Lightest goes longest** | The lightest has seconds to spare | The best and youngest get the longest jobs |
| **Face home** | It keeps you on your own world; before bites, whoever followed another tug stepped out into rock or sea and never came back | Since the bite came in (2016), a crosser who followed the Deep and could live a minute in black water with no up could have bitten home, and the Marlows are the one family that trains for exactly that, blindfold in the pit. | **Blood pays blood** | For twenty years it kept rivals away | A turning with an enemy nearby becomes a war |
| **Official letters go in the drawer** (Peg's own rule, never stated) | They were always bad news: the Ministry, the field, the bailiffs | One habit, three letters: in 2001 the Ministry's offer, addressed to Frank, that would have fed him; in 2019 the news that the police can't read the Register; in April 2026 the order to re-verify the scales |

**The doom, step by step.**
1. When most jobs took two seconds (1998 to about 2010), almost nobody turned. Police forensic reports said "clean translation" and Peg framed the cuttings. "Marlows come back straight" became the family's pride. Frank turned on a four-second job in 2001, bad luck at one in twelve. He knew at once, as every turned crosser does, and hid it, because in the family a turn meant you had failed to face home. Peg saw only a husband "off his food", "his sugar", a jam jar he couldn't open. A Ministry letter addressed to him, offering screening and a place on its small ration scheme for turned crossers, came with a police interview, and Peg put it in the drawer unopened; he never saw it. He starved for six months. Peg weighed him at 64.4 kilos (10 st 2), pencilled "7.1" on his ticket, and sent him on a seven-second job with a tenth to spare. At seven seconds a starving man was still at the tray. She remembered him as fifteen stone and made "make weight" law.
2. Families like theirs got so good that the City moved to weight. Danny sold what the family knew by night as Marlow Mix by day. Every year's pour added seconds to the family's own jobs, and to its rivals'.
3. The family answered with its rules, more carefully and more lovingly: never go back twice, so each trip got longer; make weight, so each crosser got lighter; lightest goes longest. Tess grew proud of squeezing out tenths, and of margins twice as careful as Peg's.
4. Long jobs turn people. Each turning read as a failure to face home, so the turned hid it (Frank, Rosie) or were fed at home (Terry), which meant more jobs.
5. With no paper, nobody saw that every turned Marlow had been on a long job. The family's cure, "go again" with short hops, never worked, and nobody counted. Nor did anyone else: crossing surgery has been held to three seconds since its first day, and the Register never asks how long.
6. The concrete reached the rivals. A Marlow floor made an Ahern job nine seconds long; Aoife turned; the Aherns blamed a Marlow spinner, and blood paid blood: Con, Shaun's leg, Liam.
7. Then the world changed again, one world over. The neighbours dug a mountain's worth of rock from under Grays; the family's own scale began to lie; a letter saying so went into the drawer; a boy was sent out with two tenths to spare and a fry-up inside him.
8. And ever since the bite came in, ten years ago, the one place a turned Marlow could eat lay one world over, in the Deep every Marlow had felt on every crossing, and the Marlows were the one family trained to live a minute in black water. "Face home", a rule made for the years without bites, made it unthinkable. It cost Terry five years.

**Why "always" is earned.** Frank shows the code was already killing its founder twenty-four years ago: shame made him hide his turn, the drawer kept the Ministry's offer from him, "make weight" arithmetic sent a starving man with a tenth to spare, and the family's memory turned his death into the lesson that killed Liam. The Aherns keep the same code with one difference: Maggie opens her post. She filled in a Register form for her turned uncle, and her father put it in the stove. One right person, overruled. The Aherns wrote down every job's seconds for twenty-five years, for the tug pickups, and never who turned; the Marlows remembered who turned and wrote nothing down. Either family could have seen it. Neither ever laid its numbers out, and together they are too big to call luck. In the last image a girl is cheered on a bathroom scale while the front page that would save her lies under it, unread. The world always changes eventually, here twice in one spring. A family that cannot change its mind was only waiting.

**One right person is not enough.** Tess works it out on the ferry in episode 7. Rosie stands with her at the table, and both are overruled. Alone, Tess makes things worse: trusting her own sums again, she hands Rosie to the ward. Only when the whole family writes its crossings down together and checks them against the Aherns' books and the steelyard, in episode 10, does anything change. The door is found by three adults laying their memories side by side, and the rolls go to the police by the family's vote, not by Tess's decision.

**Asking is not punished.** The one time Rosie breaks "face home", she finds the food, and her crab settles what next door is. Kieran's homework log saves the family. Tess is wrong often (Mayfair's one long trip, Liam's margin, the ward), so the heroine is plainly not always right.

## 7. Characters

Eight principals. The inner circle, where care must run deepest, is Tess, Rosie, Peg and Danny.

**TESS MARLOW, 38, the weigher.** She keeps every number in her head. The family's lightest crosser from fourteen to twenty-six, she never turned; jobs were short then. Now she plans every dip. Single mother of Kieran.
- *Want:* keep the family fed and whole; this season, keep Rosie working and get her well.
- *Wound:* the week her father died, Peg made the fourteen-year-old the family's crosser, and made her light. She has counted every mouthful since.
- *Flaw:* she trusts the numbers in her head and never checks them against anything outside it, and she answers every loss with a tighter number. "Near enough isn't," she says, and she has never written a number down. "Margins are for people who can't count."
- *Secret:* she planned Mayfair as one 9.4-second visit when two short ones would have done, because one trip is the rule and tenths are her pride.
- *Levers:* competence (the pleasure of watching her plan), interiority (we see her mouth move as she counts), love for Kieran and Rosie, and the pencil behind her ear that she never uses.
- *Arc:* she keeps the numbers in her head; she writes them down (episode 7, the back of a ferry timetable); she checks them against the Aherns' books and a steelyard, writes every turned person's odds on the roll where they can read them, and hands the pencil to Kieran (episode 10).
- *Voice:* numbers instead of feelings. Short sentences, tenths, no swearing in front of Kieran. "How much?" "Near enough isn't." When frightened she gets more exact.

**ROSIE MARLOW, 22, Danny's daughter, the lightest adult crosser.**
- *Want:* to be the best ever. Secretly, to take tumours out of children like the surgeons in the videos she watches. After Mayfair, to fix herself before anyone knows.
- *Wound:* she has been praised for being hungry since she was twelve.
- *Flaw:* pride that hides weakness, and passes the habit on.
- *Secret:* she has been turned since the cold open of episode 1. She saw it at once in the print on a crisp packet, and has lied ever since.
- *Levers:* competence, humour, courage; from episode 3, the audience re-reading every cheer; from episode 5, her guilt.
- *Arc:* she hides; she pitches the longest job to pay for a cure; she coaches Liam into her own habits and times his last runs; she tells the table the truth and stands with Tess (episode 7); she walks out of the ward to find food nobody can cut off; she chooses to stay turned, and serves her sentence on the fever wards.
- *Voice:* quick and jokey, turns everything into a boast. "Show-off, me." When she is lying she gets funnier.

**PEG MARLOW, 72, the founder.** Frank's widow. Diabetic. Runs the weighbridge and the kitchen.
- *Want:* a family that is together, proud and owes nobody. Underneath: her field back.
- *Wound:* the Ministry took her farm, and the black took Frank.
- *Flaw:* certainty. She puts official letters in the drawer unopened, and remembers what she needs to.

## 8. The ten episodes

Season time: early March to late June 2026, with an epilogue in December. Three major reversals: episode 3; episodes 6 and 7; episodes 9 and 10.

### Episode 1: "Straight"
*The Marlows pull off the cleanest dip of their lives and get back a painting nobody believes is real.*

**Cold open.** 3 a.m., Mayfair, early March. Rosie, 44 kilos, steps off the world beside a private bank's art store. Three hundred a day." Terry, cooking in yellow gloves, eats his tub, a nip of gin and a spoon of dripping: "Fat and spirits. It's all that sticks." **Act two: the painting.** *The Assayer's Wife* lies on the table: a woman at a window with a balance. In the catalogue cutting Peg pins to the wall, the window is on the left. On the table it is on the right. Nobody looks; the camera lets the audience. Danny's day crew, Liam among them, pours Marlow Mix into a bank's vault floor. Seventy per cent."): "One day." Rosie: "I'll price it for your birthday." ### Episode 2: "The Assayer's Wife"
*To sell the painting by Friday, the Marlows must make it look like itself, and Rosie secretly uses every attempt as a cure.*

**Cold open.** 04:17. Every gravimeter on Earth twitches, and goes on twitching for a few seconds. In a hundred City basements every weigh-wall floods its vault with beads at the same moment. The BBC: "a next-door event". In the dark bathroom at Grays, Rosie steps on the scale: 43.1. She smiles. ### Episode 6: "Ten Seconds" (second major reversal, part one)
*Danny gambles his hand to turn an enemy, and Tess breaks into a clinic for a cure and finds a clock.*

**Cold open.** A week later. Danny stands rigged in the dark office beside the safe, watching the wall clock, with Kieran asleep on his camp bed behind him. It is a stakeout, and Danny has done the sum: the Ahern night tow passes the Grays wharf at 02:04 every night, and whoever came off it once will come off it again. The scale's display is dark at night; there is nothing to watch but the clock. At 02:04 by the clock he steps into the black, blind, on a guess; if nobody is there, he has spent a crossing for nothing. By his glow he finds Joe Ahern mid-crossing beside the safe: Joe's ribs, his beating heart within reach of Danny's hand, an envelope inside Joe's jacket. Danny's hand hovers at the heart. Then he closes it on Joe's bare wrist and wrenches him round. The tingle comes early. He's back. "Got him." He raises his right hand. The four fingers are gone, cut clean across the knuckles, a flat plane, bloodless for a second.

**Act one: breakfast.** Peg's meter on Danny: LO. Sixpence growls at Danny. Terry dresses the hand, in his gloves, and slides his pot across without a word. Danny: "Tingle came early. That's the grab." The coin chose Danny, so Joe came back straight and whole: in the safe, delivered after all, is an envelope addressed to Tess. Peg hands it over unopened: "From them." Tess pockets it. The Laundry's bill trebles. Kieran's test weight column: 19.78, 19.79, 19.77, 19.78; he circles the little pattern. His second column: 38.9.

**Act two: the records room.** Tess decides to rob the cure. Rosie has no rig and Danny is maimed, so Tess crosses for the first time in twelve years: 61.0 kilos on the little bridge, a 2.5-second hop into Obverse's records room on Harley Street. The black comes back like an old song. By her glow she reads the spine labels inside a locked cabinet and takes one binder out.

**Act three: the protocol.** In the car she reads: "Transit 10.0 s. Expected restoration 0.70." Below it, a table: the odds of turning, second by second. None ever recorded at two seconds. One in five thousand at three. One in twelve at four. Seven in ten at ten.

**Act four: the sums.** Next morning Tess walks into Harley Street and puts the binder on Dr Osei's desk. "How long have you known?" "Since 2011. It cured me." Osei doesn't call the police. "That price pays for the Register's kitchens. That secret feeds forty thousand people. Put it back." Tess keeps it. At the office, silently, she runs the season through the table. The surgeons' three seconds, set on crossing surgery's first day as a margin against running out, and nobody counted what else the limit did: "Never turned." Terry's eleven two-second hops. The painting's seven three-second hops. Marrable Street's ten seconds, a seven-in-ten chance of curing Rosie that came up wrong. Mayfair's 9.4, which she could have split in two.

**Twist.** The cure is nothing but time, and so is the curse. That is why surgeons hardly ever turn, why the short hops never cured anyone, and why Rosie, sent in for 9.4, turned.

**Hook.** Tess pricks her own finger with Peg's meter: 5.4. Straight. She looks at Rosie. "I gave you nine."

**Gaps.** *Opens:* what's in Maggie's envelope; whether Tess will tell the family. *Closes:* Restoration's method; why Rosie turned; what the grab did.

### Episode 7: "Two Counts" (second major reversal, part two)
*Two enemy weighers meet on the ferry and lay their families' secrets side by side, while at home Peg sends a one-legged man into the black.*

**Cold open.** The Nobody Tide slaps the Tilbury pontoon under no moon. On the ferry at Nobody low water, Maggie sits with a waterman's tide book open at the Nobody columns. The envelope said: "Ferry. Nobody low water. Thursday. Just you."

**Act one: the loft.** Before she goes, Tess climbs to what nobody had ever read: twenty-five years of audit rolls in the loft, one ticket for every job-day weigh-in. The rolls give the dates and the weights; Tess's head gives the seconds of every job she planned; Terry's memory gives who turned. On the loft floor they name names, and the family's stories come apart: "Gary went to Spain" (he couldn't; HAND: T; he's on the Register in Leeds), "Uncle Vic's heart", "Maureen's nerves". And two that mended: cousin Lee's bad winter, "off his food till Easter, right as rain after the Canary Wharf job". Nobody ever asked why. Intercut, all episode: Peg, planning without Tess ("near enough"), sends Shaun on his first job since the leg, eight seconds, to pay the Laundry. Down in the office Kieran writes 38.6 in his second column, and nobody is there to see it. In Tess's car, on the way to the ferry, a scientist on the radio says the estuary's next-door events since April have a pattern.

**Act two: the Ahern side.** On neutral water Maggie brings the Ahern count: their tide books time every job to the second, for the tug pickup, and nobody ever wrote down who came back turned. Joe felt a weight at 3:12 at Mayfair and flinched; Maggie had sent him to watch the Marlows work, and he never came within six metres of Rosie. Tess lays down Kieran's 02:04 ticket: minus 59.0. Maggie: "Fifty-nine. That's my Joe." Joe came back from Danny's grab straight and whole, and still made his drop: this invitation. And Maggie tells what the Marlows never knew: the police car on the basement next to Marrable Street was her call.

**Act three: the graph.** On the back of a ferry timetable the women draw the first graph either family has ever made: seconds along the bottom, turned or not. The Ahern books hold the seconds; the Marlow memories hold who turned. Eleven Marlows and seven Ahern crossers turned in twenty-five years. Nobody under three seconds. Most over eight. And two who mended, both after a later long job. Intercut: as they pencil "most over eight", Shaun's wrist unit reads 8.0. He comes back. "Straight?" "Straight." He is. Then they come to Aoife: nine seconds, in a bank vault. Tess: "Why nine? What floor's in that bank?" Maggie names the bank. Tess knows it. Danny poured its vault floor in 2024, and the Marlow vans the Aherns saw that week were his day crew, back to pour the floor below. Maggie, folding the timetable: "All these years I've had half of this in a drawer."

**Twist.** Nobody spun anybody except Danny, who spun himself. What turned Aoife was a Marlow floor, poured by day, legally. Con died in a war about concrete.

**Act four: the stove.** At the table Tess lays down the timetable and the Obverse binder: "Ten seconds. Seven in ten. We can cure them here." Rosie stands with her and tells the table the truth: "Nothing took hold of me. I thought I'd faced wrong, and I was ashamed." Tess tells Danny what his floor did to Aoife. Danny, not Peg, turns on Tess: "And Liam? Did your sums do Liam too?" The count can't explain running out, and Tess has no answer. Danny: "You went to them." Peg feeds the timetable and the binder to the office stove. Then she carries a box of rolls toward it, and stops at Pinnock's certificate on the wall: without the rolls, no licence, no weighbridge. She puts the box back. She takes every rig into her safe: "No Marlow crosses till I say."

**Hook.** Tess is put out of the house. Next morning the Laundry van doesn't come.

**Gaps.** *Opens:* who is the Laundry, and why has it stopped; why Liam ran out (pressed hard now). *Closes:* who is turning the Marlows; the Ahern war's cause; who made Ray's "call".

### Episode 8: "The Laundry"
*With their secret supply cut off, the Marlows find out who has really been feeding them.*

**Cold open.** The last white tub, split three ways at Peg's table with a teaspoon: Terry, Rosie, Danny. Danny pushes his share across to Rosie. "I'll sort it." It is all the forgiveness he will ever say.

**Act one: go again.** The pantry is empty; Terry, Rosie and Danny turn grey. Rosie asks Peg for ten seconds. Peg: "Ten seconds is what did it." Rosie to Danny, low: "We could hold on, in there." Danny holds up his ruined hand: "Coin says which of us comes back with one hand. Not you." Peg's answer is the family cure: fourteen two-second hops for Rosie on the pit's pontoon, blindfolded, facing home, Peg with the rig key round her neck. Nothing. In the office bin, Kieran's school dinner, uneaten. His second column: 38.3.

**Act two: the van.** Tess, living in her car, waits outside Dilys's launderette on Tuesday and follows the van to where it picks up the white tubs: a flat above a bookmaker's in Tilbury.

**Act three: Ray's flat.** Tess knocks. Tubs, vodka, lard, and a folder of every Marlow's weight since 2002 in his own hand. Ray has been suspended: an audit has traced the batch codes of seven dead claimants' rations to him. "I watched your dad's weight spread for a month. I wasn't watching it again." The money the family paid is in an account in Terry's name; he never touched it. He never once asked them to change. Tess: "Twenty-four years of our weights in your hand, and you never showed a soul."

**Twist.** The Laundry is Ray. For five years the man hunting them has fed them on dead people's food.

**Act four: more.** Tess tells the family: "We never fed our own." Peg's pride breaks, and she answers with more: raid the Register's depot at Purfleet, long crossings, in a fortnight. The family votes with her. Sure of her own sums, Tess rings the Register's night line to keep Rosie off the raid. A crew in plain green takes Rosie to the Register ward in Whitechapel: no locks, only food. Rosie, as they take her, to the one person who stood where she stood: "Nine point four, Tess." A nurse, handing her a T-meter: "Police don't get the list, love. Not since 2019." In the next bed a fireman, turned on a rescue in Leeds, is going home on Friday with his first ration box, and can't stop talking about his allotment. When the nurse is busy, Rosie changes the dressings on his burned hands, and is good at it.

**Hook.** Two weeks on, Rosie is fed and strong. A Fever Company recruiter sits on her bed and offers good pay and rations for life, on wards nobody else can walk; she keeps his card. On the ward TV: the tremors in the estuary have spelled out a molecule, a sugar of the other hand, read on the estuary's gravimeters by the team that heard March's silent merger. A scientist: "It started on the fourth of April." Front pages: "COME ROUND FOR TEA." Rosie looks at the T-meter the nurse gave her. Her phone lights with a text from Kieran: "Nan's doing the depot tonight. They won't let me go." Rosie looks at the unlocked door, and walks out. The last shot is her empty bed.

**Gaps.** *Opens:* what's next door; will Peg's raid work; where Rosie is going; what the April quake has to do with the message. *Closes:* who fed them; why the van stopped.

### Episode 9: "The Deep" (third major reversal, part one)
*The family raids the state's pantry while Rosie breaks the family's oldest rule and finds what it hid.*

**Cold open.** Raid night. Peg does the numbers now, on the chalk board. Shaun asks for the longest leg himself: "My leg weighed five and a half. Eleven tenths, Nan. I'm worth more without it." Kieran begs to go: 38.0 kilos on the little bridge, 12.4 seconds, the lightest Marlow ever. The table cheers the number. Peg's chalk says yes. Danny looks at the ticket, then at the boy: he was 40 at his birthday. "You've been making weight." Liam did too. "Not him. Not after Liam." It is Danny's first no. Peg takes the rigs out of her safe and hangs them on the rack, and the crew takes all but one. They leave Kieran asleep in the office. On the last train, Rosie gets off at Grays.

**Act one: two crossings, one clock.** Intercut. At Purfleet the family takes sealed Register cases out through bead floors. Shaun's leg is eight seconds with a 3-kilo case; the chalk said 9.0 seconds. At Grays, Rosie lets herself into the office. The one rig left on the rack is Tess's old one. She steps onto the little bridge and, for the first time in her life, does not face home. In the black she feels three tugs: home; the Deep, soft and wide below; and far off on the other side, faint, the Far. She follows the Deep for two seconds, and steps out one world over into black water: cold, salt, no floor, her own held breath. Her field is gone; a field can't carry you out of water, and the bite won't fire for a minute. She breathes out a little, the way the pit taught her, feels which way her bubbles go, and swims after them, eight metres up, in the dark. The screen stays black. She breaks the surface of a sea we never see, into air we never see, takes one breath of it, and holds it. Something brushes her face. The minute is up. She bites the tab: a tight, blind field. In the black she drops eight metres by count, the way you drop a floor into a vault, and lands on the little bridge drenched, with weed in her hair and a crab in her hood. The printer chatters. Kieran wakes. She gasps the breath out, and it is gone. "It's sea. Under us. All of it."

**Act two: the cases.** Dawn. The van comes back. Terry, on a two-second leg, is as turned as when he left, as everyone knew he would be. Peg asks Shaun: "Straight?" "Straight." Peg's meter, in front of everyone: LO. The cases go on the table. Sixpence growls at every case but Shaun's. Kieran dabs Peg's meter on Shaun's porridge: a normal reading. Shaun's case turned with him: sealed, unopened, and now the wrong hand for every turned mouth in the house. Two weeks of food, and a case of porridge only the straight can eat. Peg: "Bad luck." Kieran writes it on the roll: "Food turns too."

**Act three: the other hand.** Sixpence growls at Rosie's wet jumper the way he growls at Terry. Rosie takes out the T-meter the nurse gave her and pricks Terry's finger: 2.8, a starving man's number. "Nothing else for an hour, Uncle Terry." Terry cooks the crab and eats it slowly, before he opens a tub. An hour later Rosie pricks him again. The T-meter reads 5.6. Peg's own meter, on the same drop of blood: LO. Two meters, two answers, and both are right. Terry holds out his plate for more. Nobody says what it tasted of. That night he tells Peg he is going. Peg: "Get rid of that jumper before they come." Danny pulls up the drain grate for her, and Kieran wrings the sea out of Rosie's jumper into it.

**Act four: the step.** Tess arrives; the ward rang her as next of kin. Kieran points at his graph on the office wall: the step on 4 April, there all along, and his own second column beside it. Tess: "How do you know it's not the weight?" Kieran: "Nan says it's chipped." Tess sets Peg's five-kilo bag of flour on the brass balance against the brass weights: five kilos exactly. On the little bridge: 4.95. It was never the chip. She lays Kieran's minus-59.0 ticket beside Maggie's "fifty-nine": the only true weight on the roll since April belongs to the boy nobody could see. She opens the dresser drawer: the April Trading Standards letter ("a local gravity change of minus 1.1 per cent beneath the estuary; re-verify within 28 days") and the 2019 Register letter.

**Act five: the hundredths.** Danny unfolds his printout: 65.1. Tess, from the roll: 62.4, plus 1.5 of notes, 63.9. Danny: "Why's it a kilo out?" The sum, on the back of the April letter: the scale read Liam 0.7 light, and he ate half a kilo after he was weighed. Tess: "Either one, he makes it. Both, he doesn't." Two hundredths of a second.

**Twists.** Next door is the other hand: the Deep is a sea full of food for the turned. And the family's own scale, lying since the neighbours dug under Grays, killed Liam, together with Peg's plate.

**Hook.** Tess rings Ray. "What did my dad weigh?" "Sixty-four kilos."

**Gaps.** *Opens:* what Frank really weighed, and why the legend says fifteen stone; what went down the drain (a wonder); whether Terry can reach next door. *Closes:* why Liam ran out; what the Deep is; whether next door is food.

### Episode 10: "Paper" (third major reversal, part two)
*The family reads its own record for the first time, and must decide who goes where, and whether to burn the record or give it away.*

**Cold open.** 2002, as Peg remembers it: a big Frank on the little bridge at dawn, joking while the ticket prints.

**Act one: Frank.** In the loft, Frank's tickets: 78.0 kilos the week after his four-second Farringdon job, then down every week for six months to 64.4 the morning he died, with Peg's own pencil beside them, "12 st 4" and "10 st 2", and on the last, "7.1". It is Rosie's curve. The cold open replays as it was: a thin man who can't open the jam jar, Peg laughing as she opens it for him, and Frank watching her laugh, and saying nothing. Rosie knows that look. Ray, suspended, at the kitchen door: 64.3 kilos on the Marrable Street wall, on a job planned at seven seconds. "At seven he was still at the tray. Starving men are slow." From the drawer Peg takes a 2001 envelope addressed to Frank from the Ministry: free handedness screening for anyone who has crossed, and a place on its ration scheme for turned crossers, "applicants will be interviewed by police". She opens it herself. Then she picks up the pencil and writes beside the ticket: "Frank. 10 st 2. Turned." At noon two Register investigators come to the weighbridge. The Purfleet depot's weigh-walls logged three bodies in the black, each to the hundred grams, cargo and all. They would like the family's weights. Peg has wiped the chalk board. "The audit rolls, then, Mrs Marlow. We'll be back Monday with a warrant."

**Twist.** The family's founding lesson was a memory that had turned. Frank wasn't too heavy. He was turned and starving, and he hid it, as Rosie hid hers. Peg never knew, and she sent him with a tenth to spare.

**Act two: the night of writing.** All night the family writes every crossing they remember onto the rolls in pencil, knowing that every line is evidence the investigators will take on Monday, and the cluster grows. Maggie crosses the river with her tide books, her steelyard and Joe, and weighs everyone again on the steelyard. Tess, for the first time, writes each turned person's odds on the roll, where they can read them. Terry reads his: "Here, a five-second hop: one in six, each go. There: five in six, better if I'm quick. A handshake: one in two, and a hand." He holds up his yellow gloves: "I cook with these." He takes a die from Kieran's board game: "Six, I stay hungry. Anything else, I get my tea." Tess: "Rosie did it in three. You'll take longer." Peg writes beside his name: "Terry. Turned. 2021." It is the first time she has said the word about him. Ray's number goes on the roll, and nobody reads it out. Shaun: "I've given it a leg. I'll wait till someone counts it for me." Rosie reads hers and doesn't answer. Danny, 89.0 on the steelyard: neither. Then the door, from three grown-ups laying their memories side by side. Peg, at the framed receipt: "It came up wet, sand on it, and the creek dry when it should have been full. Your grandad said the tide had gone out and forgot to tell the moon." Maggie turns her tables back to that morning in 1995: Nobody low water, to the minute. Rosie: "There's a sea under Grays. I swam up it." Peg builds it on her baking tray: a heap of flour for the Hill, Kieran's ruler laid across for our ground, water from the tap up to the height Rosie swam. Maggie tips the water down to Nobody low water, and the wet edge on the flour meets the ruler at one place. Peg puts the salt pot there. "Our field. It never fell. It walked out of their sea onto our field." Kieran writes it down. Tess hands him the pencil. Next Nobody low water: 05:52.

**Act three: 05:52.** Intercut, the same minute in two places.
- *Grays.* Tess and Kieran with Ray and Maggie. The little bridge gives Ray 8.4 seconds, Maggie's steelyard 8.3. A month ago they'd have sent him for 8.4. They send him for 7.5, and tell him the odds. He goes.
- *Mucking Marsh.* The Ministry's fence and hut, and set in the fence the Marlows' old gate. Peg's old key still opens its padlock; thirty-one years and nobody changed the lock. Peg stands on her field for the first time since 1995, on the wrong side of the law. Rosie scouts first: stripped and washed at the standpipe, lungs emptied, three seconds along the Hill's pull, a step onto a shore we never see. The screen stays black for a minute. Then the bite, and she is back on the grass with sand on her feet. "What did you see?" She doesn't answer. She walks off along the fence and lets her last breath out on her own. Then Terry: stripped, Peg with the soap at the standpipe, backs to camera, not a word; lungs emptied; 70.6 on the little bridge the night before and 71.4 on Maggie's steelyard, so five seconds and a bit to find a tug he has never followed. Danny holds the gate. There is no goodbye at the fence. Terry counts himself down, like any job, and steps off the world.
- *Grays.* Ray comes back. Nobody asks "Straight?". Kieran holds out Peg's meter. Ray signs the roll: mirror script. Faces fall. Ray laughs: for eleven years he has written backwards so the straight world could read him. The meter: 5.1. He eats a bacon sandwich slowly.
- *Mucking.* The camera stays on the empty grass by the fence, and nobody looks at any instrument. Rosie: "I'm staying as I am. Can't catch anything. There's wards that need that." Danny: "I'm done making weight. I'll go to them."

**Act four: what the rolls are.** Sunday at the bridge. Tess lays out what the rolls prove. Their dates and weights are certified and printed. On each of those dates a vault's own weigh-wall logged a body's mass and its seconds in the black, to the hundred grams. The Register's claim dates say who turned, and when. Where a turned Marlow went on working, the rolls show Frank's curve. Put together, it is the drift, on other people's paper as well as the family's, and nobody can call it made up. The binder is ash; this is all there is. And without the rolls, nobody can be charged for the depot, or for twenty-five years of dips. Danny opens the office stove: "Burn them tonight. We lose the bridge. We stay free."

**Act five: the vote.** Round the table, each says it aloud. Danny said burn. Then he looks at Kieran's second column on the wall, and says give. Kieran: give. Rosie: give. Shaun: give. Maggie puts her tide books on the pile. Peg carries the boxes to the van herself and drives them to Grays police station before Monday; the office stove stays cold. At the door the desk sergeant takes the rig off Rosie's belt, as police do. Tess walks in first and holds out her wrists.

**Ending hook.** The epilogue (section 12): in December the neighbours send a second message, a sugar of *our* hand, and fainter than the first. Last shot: the Grays outfall at Nobody low water.

**Gaps.** *Closes:* what Frank weighed and why; whether Ray can be cured; where a turned person can go; whether the Marlows will be caught, at what price. *Leaves open, as wonders:* what Rosie saw, and what her last breath smelled of; whether Terry arrived straight or turned; what the neighbours mean; what went down the drain.

## 12. The ending

**Epilogue, December.** Four short scenes, no dialogue longer than two lines.
1. **The front page.** "THE TEN-SECOND CURE: A GRAVEL FIRM'S RECORDS". The drift table is public, worked out from the rolls, the vaults' own wall logs and the Register's claim dates. ## 13. Rules the physics must never break

1. **Nothing crosses between leaves but gravity.** No light, sound, radio or smell from next door, except what a crosser physically carries back inside a field.
2. **Next door is never shown.** Not through Rosie's eyes, not in flashback, not in a dream, and not by any lamp: the only light in the black is the field's own glow, and it dies with the field. While a crosser stands next door the screen stays black. We see only what comes back: water, weed, a crab, sand, a breath.
3. **Sight in the black is glow-lit and arm's length.** Inside that circle, every inside is visible; outside it, black. A sent hand sees nothing.
4. **The budget is a fifth of a second per kilo under a hundred, on a Marlow rig.** Everything inside the field counts: cargo, air, breakfast, and water if you fire in it. Nobody outlasts their budget and comes back.
5. **Running out is dispersal.** No body is ever found. What remains is a weight, read by instruments, spreading slowly.
6. **A turn cannot be felt.** Afterwards the turned person sees the world reversed at once, and no looking can tell her whether she flipped or the world did. Anyone else can tell by comparing her with something that never crossed: Peg's meter, the T-meter, the catalogue photo, the airport strip, a heartbeat beside their own. Never by a look in a mirror, and never by a taste or a smell.
7. **The drift table in section 5 is law.** Every crossing's odds come from it, and outcomes must be believable at those odds: no long runs of failure at good odds, or of success at bad ones.
8. **Cargo turns with its carrier.** Nobody can turn a thing without turning with it.
9. **The grip.** Two crossers wholly in the black, a closed hand on bare flesh: they come back opposite hands, a coin toss which one changes, and the one who changes leaves the holding or held part in the black. A touch, a pinch, a grip on cloth or a sent hand does nothing. Nothing tethers a crosser. Nobody can guide anyone through the black by the hand.
10. **A turned body gets nothing from ordinary food or drugs and catches nothing ordinary.** It lives on water, spirits, fat and mirror food, and starves in about six months without mirror food. Ordinary drugs don't fit it.
11. **A turned person's own germs are mirror life.** How dangerous they are here nobody knows; in thirty years no outbreak has been recorded, and the world's modest rules rest on that record. This shows only as law, borders, gel, gloves, gowns and risk. It is never shown as a growth, a dish, a flask or a mould.
12. **No wall, lock, seal, knot or skin holds a crosser with a rig.** Take the rig. Knots slip in the black.
13. **Weigh-walls flood when their reading stays up for more than three seconds within four metres.** Short visits slip under. The Twitch, a ripple lasting seconds, tripped them all.
14. **Gravity from next door.** A still mass is felt only close by, fading with the cube of distance; a sudden, violent event sends a ripple felt everywhere. The April dig makes Grays and Gravesend scales read 1.1 per cent light and London's not at all. Weighbridges, platform scales and bathroom scales lie; balances, steelyards, weigh-walls and rigs do not. A platform scale feels a body standing in the black over it as a pull upward, and prints its true mass as a minus.
15. **A field fired in water fills with water and cannot carry you.** Only the bite can fire there, and only after its minute.
16. **The bite fires once, for half the seconds, only after a full minute standing on a leaf.** Its field is tight and blind. It has existed since 2016. It has no light, no gauge and no display, and nobody calls it a reserve or a spare.
17. **Facing home keeps you on your own world. It does nothing about turning.**
18. **Stepping out next door is a crossing, and so a toss, at the odds for however long it takes.** A tug you have never followed takes longer to find. A turned person who slides arrives where the food fits; one who turns arrives straight, starving there and a risk to them. Whoever goes goes stripped, washed and on empty lungs. Stepping out on another leaf on purpose is a crime: twenty years.
19. **The neighbours' life is the other hand.** Turned people can eat it. Straight people cannot.
20. **No new rules.** If a scene needs one, change the scene.

The world is ordinary and works: inspectors inspect, nurses nurse, the Register feeds the turned and a fireman goes home with his ration box, the police investigate crimes. Only the crossing families, across one river, never check, and they are the only people who cross for long.

**Words that never reach the screen.** No character or caption may use, as ideas: conjecture, criticism (as a principle), error correction, refute, falsify, fallible, "good explanation", "hard to vary", growth of knowledge, static or dynamic society, tradition of criticism, authority (as a lecture topic), optimism (as doctrine), "nothing is settled", "question everything", "the problem of", infinity. Nobody makes a speech about asking questions. The words in this plan's scaffolding ("the culture", "the doom", "the code" as an abstraction) stay in this plan. ## 15. What changed from the pitch, and why

Written for the owner. Each entry says what changed from pitch A and why. Entries marked **(v2)** came in with version 2 (the finding behind each is in `51-revision-log-r1.md`); entries marked **(v3)** are new or rewritten in version 3 (see `71-revision-log-r2.md`).

**The big changes, from making every law true**

1. **Next door is now food for the turned.** In the pitch, Rosie followed the Deep, found a sea and nothing else, and the book's ending was tested and failed. Now the neighbours' life really is the other hand. **(v3)** It is proved by an instrument and a man's own blood, not by taste: Terry eats a crab from the sea, and an hour later the Register's T-meter on his finger has climbed from 2.8 to 5.6 while Peg's meter still reads LO, and the dog growls at the wet jumper. (Version 2 tested weed from a jar of the sea on two meters; that was too close to pitch B's jar of the neighbours' sea matched to the turned elder's blood.) *Why:* you asked for every law, including this one. Followed honestly, it sharpens the season's point: since bites came in ten years ago, the one place a turned Marlow could eat lay one world over, and a rule made in the years before bites made it unthinkable.
2. **Exile is a real cure, and someone takes it.** In episode 10 Peg, Maggie and Rosie lay three memories side by side (the framed Ministry receipt, the Nobody Tide tables, the sea under Grays), and **(v3)** Peg builds them on her baking tray: the wet edge meets our ground at one place, the old field, at Nobody low water. Rosie scouts it and keeps her last breath to herself. Terry, who decided the night he ate the crab, goes through Peg's old gate, alone, **(v3)** counting himself down, with Danny holding the gate. *Why:* you asked for "exile is a cure" to be true. It isn't free (the step is itself a coin toss, at the odds for however long it takes), the heroine doesn't go, and the finale isn't about the going.
3. **Terry is not cured in the depot raid, and is now a principal.** In the pitch he came back straight and ate a bacon sandwich. *Why:* on a two-second leg a cure would be a fluke; and he is the right person to go next door: the family's pariah, who went again eleven times, cooks food he can't taste, and has never heard Peg say the word "turned". His phone friendship with Con gives the war's first death a face on the Marlow side.
4. **Mirror life is accounted for and does jobs.** The Register's rules are modest, Terry has always cooked in gloves, HAND: T passports are refused in many countries (so Rosie can't fly to a cheaper cure), Rosie works gowned for her patients' sake, and whoever steps next door goes stripped, washed and on empty lungs. **(v3)** How dangerous mirror germs are is now the world's record (no outbreak in thirty years), not a law of physics, so the sea down the drain is a real unknown; and the Register's sealed "loos" are gone, replaced by a hand gel and no shared towels, because separate toilets looked like a marked minority.
5. **A second message closes the season.** The neighbours spell a sugar of our hand, **(v3)** and fainter than the first. *Why:* the one honest channel, used again; every reading of it grows from rules already on screen, and "fainter" gives the "many leaves" a job.

**Changes to the show's own guesses**

6. **(v2) The drift table is much lower at short times:** none ever recorded at two seconds, one in five thousand at three. *Why:* at higher odds, surgeons crossing every day would turn and turn back often, and the second reversal would be old news.
7. **The cargo rule.** Whatever rides in one field turns or slides with its carrier. *Why:* the painting turned with Rosie; it also blocks cheap mirror food, makes the crab fit Terry, and turns Shaun's stolen case back into ordinary porridge.
8. **(v3) The spare is now "the bite".** A brass disc on the collar, bitten, with no light and no gauge, that fires once only after you have stood a full minute on a world. *Why:* the spare's indicator light and "use it if you're short" line looked like The Catch's reserve bar; and if it could fire at once, Rosie would never have needed to swim up, and anyone who followed the wrong tug since 2016 would have come home soaked, so the sea couldn't be news. With the minute, the Deep still drowns almost everyone, and the Marlows' blindfold training in the pit is exactly what keeps Rosie alive.
9. **(v3) The April change is a dig under Grays, not the top off the Hill.** *Why:* the Hill is eight kilometres away and above Grays, so taking its top off would make Grays scales read heavier, not lighter. Rock taken from underneath makes them read light, and leaves the Hill's shore, and so the door, where they were. Danny's "The Hill's moved" becomes his wrong guess, and Peg's "Hills don't move" is, for once, right.
10. **(v2, v3) A sixth guess, the grip.** Two crossers holding each other turn one relative to the other, and the one who turns leaves the holding hand in the black. **(v3)** Tightened: only a closed hand on bare flesh, only between two crossers wholly in the black; a pinch, a grip on cloth, a sent hand or a tether does nothing. *Why:* otherwise a fingertip pinch, or a straight Marlow's sent hand, would be a cheap or certain cure and the season's hunger would make no sense.

**Fixes the three pitch reviews asked for**

11. **The doom's engine is the concrete, the weigh-wall and "never go back twice".** Marlow Mix sells the family's night knowledge by day; a weigh-wall floods only after three seconds, so the one-trip rule both trips the wall and turns people; Tess planned Mayfair as one long visit when two short ones would have done.
