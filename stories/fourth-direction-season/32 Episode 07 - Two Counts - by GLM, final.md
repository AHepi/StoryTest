# Episode 7: Two Counts

*Two enemy weighers meet on the ferry and lay their families' secrets side by side, while at home Peg sends a one-legged man into the black.*

---

## Cold open

### Scene 1. The Tilbury pontoon, Thursday night, quarter past ten.

No moon. The estuary is a black nothing with lights hung on it — Tilbury cranes, the orange smear of Grays, the QEII bridge strung out like a fairground gone home. The Nobody Tide is running out hard, flat and patient, and off the end of the pontoon the mud has begun to show, black and shining.

The ferry is at her berth with her engines off, riding the last of the water, down by the stern as the river leaves her. One window of the passenger saloon is lit.

Inside, at the table under the window, sits MAGGIE AHERN (44), coat on, a waterman's tide book open in front of her at the Nobody columns, a pencil in its spine, a thermos, two plastic cups. She has been here since the last crossing. The crew are up forward with a telly going blue behind the wheelhouse door.

Headlights swing off the lane and die. A car door. Footsteps on the pontoon.

TESS MARLOW (38) walks out of the dark in her work coat, the pencil behind her ear, a folded white envelope in her fist. She stops at the gangway, looks once at the black water, and boards.

DECKHAND *(leaning out)*: Done for the night, love. Last crossing's gone.

TESS: I'm not crossing.

DECKHAND: Then you want to be aboard, not on my pontoon. She takes the mud at ten past eleven, and she won't swim till midnight.

She goes into the saloon. Maggie doesn't look up. She pours the second cup and slides it an inch along the table.

MAGGIE: Just you.

TESS: Said your note.

Maggie turns the tide book round so Tess can read it, and taps the column with one finger.

MAGGIE: She grounds at ten past eleven. She swims at midnight. That's what we've got.

Tess sits. Out of the window, far off, the last of the river runs out to sea.

## Act one: The loft

### Scene 2. The weighbridge office, Grays, Thursday, 7 a.m.

Breakfast, hatch down, door bolted. The stove going. Three of the family eat from white Laundry tubs this morning — TERRY (66) at the stove end, DANNY (41) with his right hand bandaged into a mitten of dressing, ROSIE (22) grey and bright by turns — and the sight of it has become ordinary in a fortnight, which is its own horror. KIERAN (12) is in his school jumper, cereal ignored, watching the grown-ups. SHAUN (26) stands at the counter on a stick, the stick leaned against the counter, eating a fried sandwich one-handed over the sink.

PEG (72) is at the chalk board with a stick of chalk, and it is Peg's handwriting going up, not Tess's, and everyone can feel the difference.

The board reads: CORBETT'S BOND · SILVERTOWN. Under it, Peg's chalk: YARD WALL · ROOM DEEP · BOX · BACK.

PEG: Eight seconds' work. Corbett's have had the same cash room since the nineties and never done a thing to it. Floor's a hand thick — old pour, beads in it sparse as currants in a bad cake. You'll go through it like river mist. *(beat)* It's not the floor. The box sits fifty foot into the room, under a plate. Fifty foot in, fifty foot back, open black all the way, and every foot of it on your clock.

SHAUN: And I've nine and a bit since the leg.

PEG: Nine six. There.

DANNY: What's the box weigh?

PEG: Steel and fifties. Call it three kilos. *(beat)* Near enough.

Terry's spoon stops in his tub for a second, and goes on. Rosie looks at her plate.

DANNY: Nobody's weighed the box.

PEG: Nobody's weighed it because nobody's had it. Three kilos, Danny, or I wouldn't say it. Terry drives. I count. Shaun goes in.

TERRY: In my belt, then, if I'm driving. *(beat)* Strap won't run short enough for him. We'll put a knot in the tail.

ROSIE: I'd go, but someone's got my rig.

Nobody laughs. Danny's jaw sets at the table's far end.

DANNY: I'll come for the carry.

PEG: You'll come for the ride, you mean. *(She turns.)* Terry. He'll want it front tonight?

TERRY: Fridays as well now, he comes. Since it went to three mouths. He wants a month up front or the van stops.

PEG: Then he'll get a month up front, and half again. *(She caps the chalk.)* Concrete pays the bills. This pays the food.

TESS *(in the doorway to the scullery, coat on)*: The wall floods at three.

PEG: The wall can flood. Let it. She was racing the beads at Mayfair and come home with it.

TESS: That was Rosie.

PEG: Yeah.

TESS: Eight seconds is—

PEG: You've somewhere to be today.

TESS: Yes.

PEG: Then I'll count.

She says it mild as milk, and it lands like a door closing. Tess looks at the board a moment — her mother's hand where hers has been for twelve years — and goes out through the yard. Through the window we see her get into her car and sit in it, not starting it.

KIERAN: Bus, Nan.

PEG: Bus.

He goes, and at the door he looks back at the board too.

### Scene 3. Tess's car, the yard, Thursday, 8 a.m.

Tess sits with the engine off and the white envelope on the wheel. She opens it with her thumbnail: one slit, clean. Inside, a strip of paper, and on it, in square capitals, no signature:

FERRY. NOBODY LOW. THURSDAY. JUST YOU.

She reads it twice. She looks up, across the flat grey of the pit and the river beyond it, at the lights of Gravesend on the far bank.

TESS: *(to nobody)* Right.

She starts the car. The big bridge prints her going out.

### Scene 4. The loft over the office, Thursday, midday.

A hatch in the landing ceiling, a wooden ladder, forty years of dust and a wasps' nest gone to paper. Tess goes up first with a torch. Terry comes after, slow, one rung at a time, a big soft man in his own house's attic, and has to sit on an old trunk for a while to get his breath. Yellow gloves, no hat, dust in what's left of his hair.

Under the eaves, in rows: printer-paper boxes, labelled in Peg's hand — 01, 02, 03, year by year to 26.

TESS: Pinnock said twenty-five years, by law. Nobody's ever read one of them.

TERRY: Why would you? They're tickets.

TESS: Open them.

He opens the first box. Inside, the audit rolls stand on end like logs — wound paper, purple ink gone lilac, going grey at the starts. Tess unrolls one across the boards and pins it down with the brass weights from her mother's kitchen, one at each corner.

TESS: Every job-day weigh-in. *(reading)* Tuesday, March the ninth, '09. Sixty-one kilos.

TERRY: Fenchurch Street. Maureen.

TESS: How long?

TERRY: Six and a half.

TESS: Did she come back straight?

TERRY: You know she didn't.

TESS: Say it.

TERRY: *(beat)* Turned.

Tess works along the roll with her torch, and Terry works along his own head, and between them the family's twenty-five years start coming off the shelf. Terry names the jobs like a man saying grace at a strange table; Tess gives the seconds, because every job that crossed her time as planner is still in her head, and the ones before it she can get out of him a date at a time.

They make two piles of torn ticket stubs on the boards between them. The piles grow. Straight on the left. Turned on the right.

TESS: Maureen. The family says Maureen went odd.

TERRY: Maureen turned on Fenchurch Street and went to them. The Register. Before your time of knowing. And this family's not looked at her since. She's in Southend. Fed. *(beat)* She sends me a card at Christmas.

TESS: You ring her.

TERRY: I ring everybody, love. Somebody's got to.

TESS: Gary. The family's got a photo of Gary at the airport.

TERRY: That's Southend pier, that photo. Gary turned on the Romford job, and Vera put him on the Register, and he went to Leeds where nobody knows what a Marlow is. Never went near a plane in his life. Can't, could he.

TESS: Uncle Vic. His heart.

TERRY: Vic's heart was fine. Vic come off his food in the winter of '09 and died of it in the March, and the family called it his heart, because that's kinder than the other thing.

TESS: Lee.

TERRY: Ah, Lee. *(he shifts; something eases in him)* Off his food all that winter. Right as rain after Canary Wharf.

Tess has the roll. Her torch runs down a column of job-day tickets, and her lips move.

TESS: Lee. November, eighty-one. December, seventy-six. Seventy-four. Seventy-one. January, sixty-eight. February, sixty-six. *(beat)* Canary Wharf, March. April: seventy-one. May: seventy-five.

TERRY: There.

TESS: It's on the paper, Terry. It was on the paper the whole time. He went into Canary Wharf one side of turned and come off it the other.

TERRY: Nobody ever asked why.

TESS: Why?

TERRY: *(gently, at the dust)* He was off his food, love. Then he wasn't. That was enough for us.

Tess looks at the two piles. Her lips move. Eleven stubs in the right-hand pile. She reaches for the next box, and her hand stops on the one marked 02.

TERRY: That's your dad's year.

TESS: I know.

TERRY: You won't find anything in there, love. Your dad never turned. He was too heavy, and the black took him. *(beat)* It's the one thing in this family everybody's sure of.

She has the lid half off. A thousand other numbers are shouting in her head — Maureen's six and a half, Lee's nine and a half, the eleven stubs — and the lid goes back on, and the box goes back in its row, and neither of them looks at it again.

TESS: Canary Wharf. How long?

TERRY: Long. You planned it. You were, what, sixteen?

TESS: Nine and a half. *(beat)* Same as Mayfair, near enough.

She reads Lee's column once more, top to bottom, and stands up into the dust with it filed where she files things.

TESS: Bring the box down. I want them where I can see them.

TERRY: Nobody comes up here but wasps.

TESS: They can come down as well.

Terry carries the box down the ladder for her, one rung at a time, breathing like a bellows at the bottom, and it sits on the dresser for the rest of the day with two piles of stubs in it, straight and turned, and nobody who comes to the hatch asks what it is.

### Scene 5. The weighbridge office, Thursday, 8 p.m.

The yard is dark. The vans are out. Kieran is at the counter with his homework and his exercise book, and the office is quiet the way it is before a job.

TESS: When's Nobody low tonight?

KIERAN: *(without looking up)* Ten past eleven. She's on the mud by then. Swims about midnight.

TESS: How do you know that?

KIERAN: It's my homework. The tide's in my graph. *(beat)* The real tide's in the tables, but the tables don't say which moon it runs to. That's the bit Miss likes.

TESS: Nobody low. Ten past eleven.

KIERAN: Ten past eleven. Want me to write it down?

TESS: *(almost a smile)* No. *(beat)* I want your minus-fifty-nine. Just for tonight.

He looks at her. Then he fetches the book from under his pillow on the camp bed, peels the tape off the ticket with his thumbnail, careful, and hands it over the way you hand over something you've been guarding. 02:04 · LITTLE BRIDGE · −59.0 KG.

KIERAN: You'll bring it back.

TESS: First thing.

KIERAN: *(beat)* Is it about the boy on the tow?

TESS: Something like that.

KIERAN: I've a whole page spare if you find anything out.

TESS: Have you.

She puts the ticket inside her coat, against her chest, and goes out. Kieran watches the door shut, then looks at the gap in his book where the ticket was, and tapes a clean square of paper over the hole to mark the place.

## Act two: The Ahern side

### Scene 6. Tess's car, the river road, Thursday, ten to ten.

Fog off the water, headlights swimming in it. Tess drives with both hands on the wheel. On the radio, the calm BBC voice:

RADIO: …the gravity group at Reading say they have now counted forty-one next-door events in the Thames estuary since the fourth of April, all within a mile of Grays. And the striking thing, they say, is the spacing — regular, like a metronome. The teams say it is too early to say what —

Tess turns it off. She isn't listening anyway. Her lips are moving, going down a column only she can see: *six and a half, turned. Nine and a half, mended. Two, eleven times, nothing.*

The fog closes behind the car.

### Scene 7. The ferry saloon, Thursday, twenty past ten.

Maggie pours the tea. Between them on the table: her tide books, six of them, spines cracked, twenty-five years of them.

TESS: You wanted comparing.

MAGGIE: I wanted your half.

TESS: You've got books.

MAGGIE: Seconds. Every job this family's ever run, timed to the second, for the pickup. A tug stood off a wall is a question waiting to be asked, so we time everything to the second and the tug's never stood longer than it takes to make fast. *(She opens the top book at random: columns of dates, times, figures in pencil.)* What we never wrote down — *(she taps the column where a name would go)* — is who come home wrong. That we kept in our heads. Like you.

TESS: We wrote nothing at all.

MAGGIE: I know. I've watched your fires.

A silence. Out in the black, a ship's horn, twice, a long way off.

MAGGIE: Before anything. Con.

TESS: Con's pills went in my stove before I knew they were pills. In with the tub labels. I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm telling you the shape of it.

MAGGIE: *(a long beat)* He was sat in that chair with your uncle's name on his phone. TEL. Held it in his lap like a hymn book. *(beat)* His own brother thinks the pills are still somewhere. I've not had the heart to say where they are.

TESS: Say it.

MAGGIE: They're ash in Grays. *(She looks at Tess properly.)* You knew the day I rung. Your voice on that phone was flat as a ticket.

TESS: I knew the day you rung.

MAGGIE: And come and met me anyway.

TESS: You asked.

Maggie nods slowly, and lets it be, and it is the first thing either of them has let the other all night.

TESS: Mayfair. Twelve past three. The wall logged two bodies.

MAGGIE: Mine was the second. Seventeen year old, and I sent him. Sat in the dark off your shelf with a stopwatch, timing your girl, so we'd know what a Marlow's made of. *(beat)* He never came within three fathoms of her. He swears it on my mother's grave, and I believe him, because he was frightened, and frightened boys keep their distance.

TESS: She felt him go by.

MAGGIE: He felt her too. Big, he said. Like a lorry up close on a fast road. *(beat)* Nobody touched nobody. And then that second weight in the Mayfair log got called a spinner on both banks, and your family started reaching for coats.

Tess takes the ticket out from inside her coat and lays it flat on the table between the cups. 02:04 · LITTLE BRIDGE · −59.0 KG.

MAGGIE: Fifty-nine. *(beat)* That's my Joe.

TESS: He came off your tow and stood in my mother's kitchen, at my mother's safe, a foot from where my boy sleeps now.

MAGGIE: He'd have finished that drop with his arm off. He's Ahern. *(beat)* Your brother was waiting for him in the dark. The coin took your brother's fingers and let my Joe keep his hand. I've thought about that every night since. I don't know yet if I'm grateful.

TESS: Neither do I.

MAGGIE: *(and now, evenly, watching Tess's face)* The car on your basement. The one that sent your girl the long way round.

TESS: Whose?

MAGGIE: Mine. I rung it from the box on Marrable Street. *(beat)* Ten seconds under a road, Tess. I wanted it to be more than she'd got.

The tide slaps at the hull. Tess doesn't move.

TESS: She'd already turned. Before Joe ever went in. Before your phone call. She turned at Mayfair, on nine point four seconds, and hid it, and we clapped her for getting thin.

Maggie takes that in the way a wall takes weather. Her eyes go down the ticket again, to the minus.

MAGGIE: Nine point four.

TESS: Nine point four.

MAGGIE: *(quietly)* Nine point four she was in that black, and she's alive and fed, and my girl's a reading on a fridge.

She opens the oldest tide book at a page marked with a fisherman's float, and turns it round, though Tess can't read it in the dark; it's a gesture, handing over a grave.

MAGGIE: Aoife did her first nine at nineteen, on the Dunkirk run, and come home straight as a die. Then the bank job, October, and she come home wrong, nursing, and her milk wouldn't feed him. Three days he screamed before any of us understood. *(beat)* She went again so she could feed him herself. Nobody counted her. She went in off our pontoon on her own, and the wall read her a month, less every day.

TESS: Nobody counted her?

MAGGIE: Who? Her dad? Her dad thinks facing home is a charm. She went in alone, in the dark, hungry, with her own sums. *(beat)* Nobody counted her, Tess. That's what I've come to say. Whatever else tonight — nobody counted my girl.

The saloon ticks. The flask sits between them. Tess reaches across and turns the newest tide book around to face herself, and looks down the columns of seconds, and her lips start moving, and this time Maggie watches her do it and recognizes it, because she does it too.

TESS: Then let's count her now.

## Act three: The graph

### Scene 8. The weighbridge office, Thursday, five past eleven.

Dark, but for the printer's small green light. Through the blinds, the van's tail lights go out of the gate and down the lane, and the engine fades off towards the A13. Upstairs, Rosie's light is off.

Kieran comes down the stairs in his socks. He stands a moment in the middle of the office, listening until there's nothing left to hear — no van, no ferry, no river but the far one — then steps onto the little bridge in his pyjamas.

The printer chatters. He tears the ticket off by the green light.

38.6.

He sits on the edge of his camp bed and opens the exercise book. In the second column, the one headed *me*, under 39.2 and 38.9, he writes: 38.6. He looks at the three of them going down. He looks at the graph on the wall with its flat line and its step and its circled wobble, and doesn't add himself to it.

He puts the book under his pillow, lies down, and turns his face to the safe.

### Scene 9. The ferry saloon, Thursday, ten past eleven.

The clock in the wheelhouse door goes ten past. Somewhere under the keel the mud lets out a long sigh, and the saloon settles a degree, and everything in it — the cups, the tide book, the two women — leans a fraction and rights itself. Aground.

Maggie has taken the ferry timetable off the saloon rack — a single printed sheet, GRAVESEND — TILBURY, MAY — and turned it over to its blank back, and flattened it on the table with the flat of her hand. She draws the pencil out of the tide book's spine.

Tess's hand goes up behind her ear, to the pencil that has lived there for twenty years and never once come down. She holds it a second. It has never been sharpened.

MAGGIE: Mine's sharp.

Tess puts her own pencil back behind her ear, and takes Maggie's, and this is the first thing a Marlow has ever written on purpose.

They rule the sheet with the timetable's own printed columns as a straightedge. Down the bottom, Tess writes the seconds: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10.

MAGGIE: Right. Yours first or mine?

TESS: Yours. You can read yours.

MAGGIE: *(running down a column)* September '19. Rotterdam run. Four and a quarter.

TESS: Whose?

MAGGIE: Joe's dad, God rest him. Straight.

Maggie pencils a small tick above the 4.

MAGGIE: March '21. The grain job. Eight and a half.

TESS: Whose?

MAGGIE: Our Brendan. *(beat)* Turned.

A small cross above the 8.

TESS: And ours. Fenchurch Street, '09. Six and a half. *(beat)* Turned. Our Maureen.

A cross above the 6.

MAGGIE: Your girl. Mayfair, March just gone.

TESS: Nine point four. Turned.

And then it finds its rhythm, and the two of them go at it like a couple of women beating carpets: Maggie reads the seconds, Tess gives the verdict — hers straight from her head, the old ones out of what Terry gave her at midday — and the ticks and crosses go up the sheet. Marlow jobs out of a memory; Ahern jobs off paper; and neither woman remarks on which is which, because both of them know.

TESS: *(her finger coming down the left-hand column)* Terry. Two seconds, eleven times.

MAGGIE: And?

TESS: Nothing. Eleven times, nothing. Not turned, not mended. Nothing at all.

Maggie pencils eleven small ticks in a row above the 2, like a fence.

MAGGIE: Poor beggar. Eleven goes and never a tick the other way.

TESS: *(quietly)* He thought it was him.

Maggie looks at the fence of ticks a while. Then she squares the sheet, and the two women bend back over it, and the crossings of two families go up the page tick by tick and cross by cross, and the flask goes cold between them, unremarked.

### Scene 10. A service yard off the North Woolwich Road, Silvertown, Thursday, twenty to midnight.

A high brick yard between warehouses, one lamp, rain in it. The van backed against the wall of Corbett's bonded store. TERRY at the wheel. PEG in the back, kneeling, coat on, the chalk numbers in her head. DANNY in the passenger seat, his bandaged hand in his lap. SHAUN stands at the tailgate on his one sound leg, rigged in Terry's belt, the strap knotted short for a thinner man, the stick already in the van.

PEG: In through this wall. Brick's quick. Then under the old floor and along — fifty foot, and the floor over you's thin as a crust, so it's the distance that's the work, not the going. Box is under a plate at the far end. Steel and fifties, call it three kilos. The wall will go at three. Let it. Box, then back the way you came.

SHAUN: Eight seconds' work.

PEG: You've nine and a bit. *(beat)* I'll count you every tenth.

SHAUN: Right.

DANNY: She can't hear you, Mum.

PEG: I know. *(She takes his wrist unit and zeroes it, her lips already going.)* So someone's counting.

Shaun flexes his fingers once, the way every Marlow does, and steps off the world. There one second, gone the next: a man standing at the tailgate, and then rain falling through the space where a man stood.

### Scene 11. The ferry saloon, and the yard at Silvertown, intercut, ten to midnight and after.

**Silvertown.** In the van, Peg kneels on the floor with her eyes shut, counting aloud, low and even, and it is her daughter's voice and her daughter's daughter's voice coming out of her, generation after generation of it:

PEG: …one… two…

**The black.** Sound drops to Shaun's heartbeat and breath. His glow reaches an arm's length. In it: the inside of the brick wall, lime mortar, a bottle someone bricked up in nineteen-eleven; then down, and the old slab's underside goes over him like a low bridge, and the beads in it hang grey and far between. Easy going. And fifty foot of it. He pulls himself along by small pulls at nothing, and moves as well in here as he ever did, because the black never needed legs.

**Silvertown.**

PEG: …two… three. There's the wall.

Through the brick comes the roar like a tipper shedding its load, and Danny's knuckles go white on the dashboard, and Terry's lips move in time with his mother's.

**The ferry.** The sheet is crowded now. Tess counts the crosses with her finger, twice, to be sure.

TESS: Eleven of ours.

MAGGIE: Seven of ours.

TESS: And look at it. *(Her finger travels the bottom of the sheet.)* Not one under three seconds. Not one, either side of the river, not in twenty-five years. And the crosses — the crosses live up here.

Her finger moves up the sheet, from the 8 towards the 10, where the crosses crowd.

**The black.** 4.0. 5.0. The box comes into the glow: small, sunk under a floor plate, and through its steel walls, lit like everything inside the glow, bricks of fifties in clear wrap. The beads are falling all round him now, out of the top of his light, silent, filling the room above. He reaches through the plate and takes the box.

**The ferry.** Maggie, reading where the crosses sit, pencils a slow line along the top of the 8 column as she says it:

MAGGIE: Most over eight.

Tess looks at her watch, not the sheet.

TESS: Our Shaun went into the black at ten to midnight. On eight seconds of work.

MAGGIE: And?

Tess doesn't answer. Her eyes come back to the sheet, to the crosses crowding above the 8.

**The black.** Close on Shaun's fingertips: the fizz starting under the nails. The wrist unit reads 6.9. The tingle is a roar in his nails now; he is still under the floor with the box against his chest; the beads are up to the plate above him. He turns for home, finds the one pull that means it, and goes.

**Silvertown.**

PEG: …seven…

DANNY: Mum.

PEG: …eight… Nine. He's got it.

Terry, at the wheel, talking to the windscreen: "Come on, son. Come on, son."

The air where Shaun stood takes him back with a gasp, on the tailgate, the box in his arms, rain on his face.

SHAUN: Straight.

PEG: *(her lips stopping)* Say it proper.

SHAUN: Straight.

DANNY: *(low, his good hand going to the box)* Meter him, when we're home.

PEG: He said straight.

A beat. Rain on the van roof. Terry, who dressed the leg and the hand, looks at the first-aid box under the seat, and doesn't reach for it. Nobody says anything else, and the van breathes out anyway.

Terry claps Shaun's shoulder. Danny takes the box off him with his good hand and his knee.

PEG: Eight four. I said eight, and he took eight four, and he's stood here.

**The ferry.** The deck shudders. The engines turn over somewhere below. Out in the dark the tide has come back under the hull, and the ferry lifts, an inch, then another, off the mud, a whole building waking up.

MAGGIE: She's swimming.

They don't stand. There's still the sheet.

MAGGIE: And the mended?

TESS: *(pointing)* Ours is Lee. Off his food all one winter, right as rain after Canary Wharf. Nine and a half. Nobody ever asked why.

MAGGIE: Brendan. Ours. Same, after Zeebrugge. A nine, off the book. *(She circles one of her own crosses, and looks at it a while.)*

TESS: *(beat)* Ten seconds mends seven in ten.

MAGGIE: Whose number's that?

Tess pulls the corner of something out of her inside pocket: cloth-bound, OBVERSE stamped in the corner.

TESS: The clinic that sells it. Hundred and eighty thousand a head. I borrowed their book.

MAGGIE: *(a breath of a laugh with no humour in it)* Good. *(She writes 7/10 over the 10 column, and looks at the sheet as a whole.)* Twenty-five year. Two families. One page.

TESS: Aoife. Where's Aoife?

Maggie takes the pencil back and puts her two marks in: a tick over the 9, and, next to it, a cross over the 9.

MAGGIE: A nine at nineteen, straight. The bank, October. Turned.

Tess stares at the two nines sitting side by side.

TESS: Same seconds. One each way. Coin. *(beat; and then, slowly)* Why nine?

MAGGIE: What?

TESS: That vault. She'd walked it since she was fifteen, you said. Every job in it six seconds, in and out, all her life, tide or no tide. Why does it come up nine in October?

MAGGIE: Because — *(and she stops, because she has never once asked it either)* — they'd had work done. There's a floor under the floor now. She said it coming out. She said, "it's grown a cellar, Mum." The vans were there all week beforehand.

TESS: What vans?

MAGGIE: Yours, Tess. MARLOW on the side.

Tess goes very still, and we go with her:

**Flash — a bank yard on Cheapside, this March.** Rain on a hoarding. Danny with the facilities manager, Liam behind him holding a tube of drawings. DANNY: *"Half this street. Did a vault further down in '24, they had us back last autumn for the floor underneath. Two floors to get through, not one. That's a long time to be nowhere."* And the facilities man laughs, and Liam's ears go pink, and Danny drinks his tea.

**The ferry.**

TESS: Mynors.

MAGGIE: Mynors. Cheapside. Two doors down from the one that flooded.

TESS: My brother poured that floor. Twenty-four. And his crew went back last autumn and put another one under it. *(beat)* He told us he'd poured it. Twice. Two floors now, he said. Long time to be nowhere. He was pleased.

MAGGIE: *(folding the timetable in half along its crease, careful, like something she means to keep)* Nobody spun anybody.

TESS: Except Danny. And the only one Danny ever spun was himself.

MAGGIE: My uncle died of a war about concrete. *(She puts the folded sheet in her tide book, and the tide book in her bag.)* All these years I've had half of this in a drawer.

They stand at last. At the rail the black river runs past. On the far slip at Gravesend a single torch is bobbing — a boy and a bicycle, waiting.

TESS: Your boy.

MAGGIE: He waits up now. *(beat)* He doesn't cross, Tess. Not while I've got him. Nobody of mine crosses till somebody's counted it.

TESS: And the boats? The bullet?

MAGGIE: Con's dead and your boy's dead and my girl's a fridge door. Nobody's square. *(beat)* But nobody spun anybody, and that's the first true thing I've had off that river in a year. It'll do to be going on with.

TESS: My mother will burn this.

MAGGIE: I know your mother's stove. It's famous on both banks. *(She taps her bag.)* My half's in here. Whenever, Tess.

The ferry noses out into the channel. Tess stays aboard till the Tilbury side, and walks off down the empty pontoon to her car with the ticket in her coat and the folded shape of nothing at all, because everything worth carrying tonight is in her head.

## Act four: The stove

### Scene 12. The weighbridge office, Friday, one in the morning.

The van is back; the yard gate is shut. In the office, under the lamp, Peg squares a brick of fifties with a rubber band, and sets it in the float tin, and the tin is full for the first time in a month. Shaun is on the settle with his leg up, eating. Terry is at the stove. Danny sits at the table with his bandaged hand flat in front of him like an exhibit. Rosie sits at the stairs' foot.

Kieran, woken by the van, is on the stair above Rosie, in the dark of the turn, and nobody has sent him back to bed.

Tess comes in. She has mud on her shoes. She doesn't sit. She takes the ferry timetable out of her coat, unfolds it on the table, and turns it round to face them: the ticks, the crosses, the fence of eleven above the 2, the two circles, 7/10 over the 10. Then she lays the Obverse binder beside it.

TESS: Every job both our families have run in twenty-five years. Their half off their tide books, our half out of Terry and me. Seconds along the bottom. Who came back turned above.

PEG: I'm not interested in their jobs.

TESS: Eleven of ours turned, Mum. Seven of theirs. Not one under three seconds, either side, not in twenty-five years. Most of them over eight. Terry's eleven goes are on there — two seconds, eleven times, and nothing, nothing turned and nothing mended, because there's nothing at two seconds to have. *(beat)* And two that mended. Both after a long job, after.

PEG: Floors don't turn people—

TESS: Nobody said floors—

PEG: —and Marlows come back straight. Always have. Police said so. In print.

Peg says it to the binder, not to Tess, and it is the wall of her, and everyone at the table can hear the mortar falling out of it even if she can't.

TESS: Ten seconds in the black mends seven in ten. That's their book — that's what the hundred and eighty thousand buys, ten seconds under their roof, and they've known it fifteen year. We've got rigs. We can cure them here. Rosie. Terry. Danny. Tonight if we—

ROSIE: *(standing up)* She's right, Nan.

PEG: Sit down, girl.

ROSIE: In a minute. *(beat)* And there's something else right, and it's mine, and I've been sat on it since March. *(She looks round the table: her dad, Terry, the stair where Kieran is.)* Nothing took hold of me in the black.

The room goes still.

ROSIE: At Mayfair. There was never anybody there. Their lad was six metres off with a stopwatch, timing me, and I never knew him from a pull in my gut. I turned because I was in there nine point four seconds. I knew it the minute I looked at a crisp packet in that van. And I said something took hold of me because I thought I'd faced home wrong, and I was ashamed. *(beat)* That's all it ever was. Shame. And you all clapped. *(beat)* And when it's been counted proper, and checked against somebody else's, I'm going first.

Terry looks down at his yellow gloves. Peg looks at the stove. Danny looks at his daughter as if a bone were setting in him.

TESS: And Mynors.

DANNY: What about Mynors.

TESS: Aoife Ahern's bank. It was six seconds, Danny, all her life. She'd walked it since she was fifteen. Then it grew a floor underneath. Her six came up nine, and she came home wrong, and her milk wouldn't feed her baby, and she went in again on her own and nobody counted her, and she's a reading on their fridge. *(beat)* And the police car that sat on our basement — the one that sent Rosie in the long way, ten seconds under a road — their weigher asked for it. Rung from a phone box on Marrable Street. She told me last night, on the water.

DANNY: *(very quietly)* Who poured it.

TESS: You know who poured it. Your crew. Twenty-four, and again last autumn. Your vans were parked on that street the week she crossed. You told us about it. You said it was a long time to be nowhere. You were pleased.

Danny lifts his bandaged hand off the table an inch, and sets it back down.

DANNY: Best job we ever did. *(beat)* And it's four fingers cheaper than the last one.

He turns on Tess, and it is not Peg who turns, it is Danny, exactly as it always had to be.

DANNY: And Liam? Did your sums do Liam too?

Tess's lips move. Nothing comes off them. The whole family watches the weighing machine fail to give a number.

DANNY: You're the one that's never wrong. Two tenths, you said. Near enough isn't, you said. So which is it? *(beat)* You knew what eight seconds is. You had it on your lips at this table this morning, and you went and sat on a boat instead, and let her chalk it.

TESS: I don't know.

She has never said it in her life. She says it again, because it is all there is.

TESS: I don't know, Danny.

DANNY: And you went to them. *(He flips the binder with the back of his good hand.)* Their book. Their numbers. Her ferry. You went to them, and you come back here and put it on my mother's table.

Peg stands up. She takes the ferry timetable in both hands, and the binder under her arm, and crosses the room to the stove.

TERRY: Peg.

She opens the little door. The fire is going. She feeds the timetable in first, the ticks and the crosses and the two circles, and then the binder, and stands and watches it catch, and the light of it moves on her face.

TERRY: *(quietly, to the stove, not to her)* Eleven goes, that was. Eleven. I could've been eating at this table five year since.

Peg stays by the stove until there's nothing left but edges curling, and then she does the other thing. She goes to the dresser, where the box of stubs has sat since midday with its two piles in it, straight and turned, and she picks it up, and turns to the stove with it.

And stops.

On the wall beside the stove hangs Pinnock's certificate, in its frame, dated last year, the little crown stamp. She reads it, all of it, top to bottom, in the firelight: the licence, the roll, the law of twenty-five years.

If the rolls burn, there's no licence. If there's no licence, there's no weighbridge. And the weighbridge, whatever else it is, feeds them.

She puts the box on the dresser. Not in the stove. On the dresser.

PEG: Not them.

She doesn't explain. The certificate does it for her.

Then she goes to the steel rack by the back door and takes the rigs in, one at a time, in front of everyone. The old one with the cracked strap, off its hook. Terry's belt, off the chair where Shaun dropped it, the strap still knotted short for a thinner man. Last, Peg stops in front of Danny and holds out her hand.

Danny looks at it. Then he unbuckles his own belt one-handed, awkward, laying the straps out so it doesn't fall, and gives it to her.

She kneels at the safe — the printer chatters out her weight under the counter, and nobody tears it off — lays the three rigs inside on top of *The Assayer's Wife*, shuts the door, and turns the brass key. The clack like a knuckle.

PEG: No Marlow crosses till I say.

She stands, and turns to Tess, and her voice doesn't rise at all, which is the worst thing in the room.

PEG: And you can sleep in your car.

DANNY: Mum—

PEG: We don't go to them. Five and twenty year, we've not gone to them, and it's kept us. She went. And she's sat at my table with their book, and a ferry ticket, and told her brother he turned a mother. *(beat)* Out, Tess. And you won't plan for this family again.

Tess looks round the kitchen once: the board with her mother's chalk on it, the settle, the stove dying down, the grey smear on the board where Rosie's column was and the older name showing through under it. On the stairs, Kieran has gone rigid in the dark.

TESS: *(quietly)* Yeah.

She takes her coat off the hook. Going past the stair she stops, and takes the 02:04 ticket out of her coat, and puts it into Kieran's hand, and folds his fingers over it.

TESS: First thing, I said.

KIERAN: *(not able to look at her)* Mum.

TESS: Bed.

She touches the top of his head once, and goes out through the yard. Through the window they see her car's lights come on. It rolls across the big bridge, and the printer chatters and prints her going, and nobody inside says a word, and the ticket the family's whole war turned on sits folded in a twelve-year-old's fist.

Kieran looks at the stove, at the last edge of the timetable curling to nothing. Then he opens his exercise book, tapes the −59.0 ticket back into its place, closes the book, and puts it under his pillow.

### Scene 13. The weighbridge office, Friday, half past nine in the morning.

Grey light. The hatch is up; the day is running as normal, because a weighbridge is a public thing and lorries don't know. Mick leans in for his ticket and a bacon roll, and reads the ticket, and frowns.

MICK: Still got me two hundred light, Peg.

PEG: Then you've had two good days.

He goes. Terry stands at the window with the envelope of fifties squared on the counter, ready, and the old tubs washed and stacked upside down on the drainer to be swapped. He is in his yellow gloves. The clock goes round the hour and past it.

TERRY: She's late.

PEG: She's never late.

Terry waits. Sixpence gets stiffly out of his basket, growls at him — the low steady note, five years of it — and lies back down.

TERRY: I know. I know.

Peg lifts the phone and rings the launderette. We hear Dilys small down the line.

PEG: Dilys. It's Peg Marlow. Our whites. *(listens)* What d'you mean, he's not rung? *(listens; and her voice goes flat)* No. No, he rings you.

She hangs up. She looks at Terry. Terry opens the fridge: the shelf of white tubs, three mouths deep and no more. He counts with his eyes, the way everyone in this family counts everything now, and shuts the door.

TERRY: Nine days. Less, there being three.

He takes one clean spoon down from the rack and sets it beside one clean empty tub, out of habit, ready for a delivery, and then stands looking at the two of them side by side on the counter, and doesn't put them away.

Out in the yard the lorries come and go, and the printer chatters, and the bridge keeps its law, and nothing comes up the lane.

**Cut to black.**

**END OF EPISODE 7**

## Plants and payoffs

**Paid off here, from earlier episodes**
- **The envelope, pocketed unopened (6), and Maggie's "Thursday fortnight" (6):** opened in the car; the ferry, at Nobody low, "just you".
- **Kieran's 02:04 ticket (4, 5):** laid before Maggie — "Fifty-nine. That's my Joe" — and returned; taped home under a clean square.
- **Two bodies in the log at 3:12 (1, 3), and Ray's notebook "2ND BODY · 59.0" (5):** Joe, three fathoms off, timing Rosie with a stopwatch. The spinner is dead — and Tess tells the table, so the whole family knows it, and Rosie's confession answers it.
- **Maggie's tip (4: "Did the police sit on their basement?" / "Good"):** confessed on the ferry and passed to the whole family at the table, Kieran included from the stair.
- **"Something took hold of me" (3):** Rosie's retraction, in full, with the shame named.
- **Terry's eleven two-second hops (1, 2, 6):** plotted as eleven ticks and no cross and no cure — "he thought it was him" — and "I could've been eating at this table five year since" at the burning.
- **The drift table (6):** the graph gives it a shape; "ten seconds mends seven in ten"; "We can cure them here."
- **Danny's Cheapside boast (2) — "'24... the floor underneath", "Two floors to get through, not one. That's a long time to be nowhere" — the Marlow vans at Aoife's bank (4), and Aoife's nine seconds (4):** the second half of the second major reversal. The flash is the March yard as it happened, and Maggie places the bank: Mynors, two doors down from the one that flooded. Nobody spun anybody; a Marlow floor, poured by day, legally, turned Aoife; Con died in a war about concrete.
- **"She can't hear you" / "I know" (1) and "So someone's counting" (3):** Danny to Peg at the wheel, and Peg counting Shaun home with Tess's voice.
- **"Near enough isn't" (1, 3, 5):** inverted by Peg at the board — "Call it three kilos... Near enough" — with Terry's spoon stopping. Her guessed box is the margin nobody checks, while the one check that costs nothing, the meter, is the one she refuses.
- **Pinnock's "twenty-five years of audit rolls in your loft, by law" and his certificate (1):** the loft is read for the first time; the certificate is what stops the rolls burning.
- **The stove (1, 3, 4):** it eats the timetable and the binder.
- **The tide book's pencil in the spine (4):** the pencil that draws the first graph. Tess's own pencil comes down at last — and goes back behind her ear, unfired, where it waits for Kieran (10).
- **Con's pills, and Tess knowing (4, 5):** "they're ash in Grays"; "the day I rung" / "the day you rung".
- **Shaun's leg and his seconds (5):** his first job back, eight seconds through a room fifty feet deep, straight, on the stick between crossings — the first of his two eight-second legs (9).
- **The Laundry's trebled bill and the month up front (6):** the Silvertown job pays it; the money sits squared on the counter the morning the van stops.
- **"We don't go to them" (3):** the sentence Tess is put out of the house on, now "five and twenty year".

**Planted here, for later**
- **The box of rolls, saved by the certificate (10):** the night of writing, the vote, and the drift made public all hang on Peg putting that box on the dresser instead of in the fire.
- **The loft's names (10):** Gary in Leeds, Maureen in Southend, Vic, Lee's curve on the roll — the family's dead stories, spoken aloud once, ready to be written.
- **The box marked 02 (10):** opened a hand's width and closed again on Terry's certainty — "the one thing in this family everybody's sure of".
- **Maggie's drawer (10):** "My half's in here. Whenever." Her tide books, her steelyard, Joe.
- **The radio's forty-one regular events, "like a metronome" (8):** the spacing without the meaning; what the events are writing is left for June's front pages.
- **Nine days of tubs and no van (8):** the last white tub, split three ways; Ray's suspension; the depot raid.
- **Rosie's "when it's been counted proper, and checked against somebody else's, I'm going first" (8, 9):** the ask itself — ten seconds — waits for the empty pantry in 8.
- **Peg's "No Marlow crosses till I say" (8, 9):** three rigs locked in the safe over the painting — Terry's, Tess's old one with the cracked strap, Danny's — which Peg hangs back on the rack in 9, the crew taking all but the cracked one, which Rosie takes.
- **The meter refused (9):** Peg's "He said straight", and Terry's hand not reaching for the first-aid box — in 9 Peg herself meters Shaun in front of everyone, and it reads LO.
- **Tess's watch beat (8, 10):** she knew Shaun's odds, started to say them, and went to the ferry anyway; Danny names it at the stove. Alone, trusting her own sums again, she hands Rosie to the ward in 8; in 10 she writes each turned person's odds on the roll where they can read them.
- **Danny's guilt (8, epilogue):** "Best job we ever did... four fingers cheaper than the last one"; the road to the Register.
- **Kieran's page spare, and the ticket taped home (9, 10):** the boy who keeps the book while the house falls; "I've a whole page spare if you find anything out."
- **Terry's five years (9, 10):** the crab, the die, the sea.
- **Maggie's change (epilogue):** "Nobody of mine crosses till somebody's counted it" — against Declan, who never does.

**Knowledge at the end.** The family knows: nobody spun anybody; the odds by seconds; Rosie's lie and its reason; Danny's floor at Mynors; Maggie's tip; Tess's clinic book. Maggie knows: Rosie turned at 9.4; Danny turned; the shape of the odds and 7-in-10; that the pills burned. Only the audience knows: Kieran's 38.6, and that the box marked 02 was opened and shut again. Nobody knows why Liam ran out.
