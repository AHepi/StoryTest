## 1. Title and logline

**SECONDS**

**Logline.** An Essex family of thieves who can step through walls has one proud boast: Marlows always come back straight, never mirror-turned. Then their best crosser starts starving at the family table while they cheer every kilo she loses. The woman who keeps the family's numbers in her head must find out what is turning them. The answer is the seconds she has squeezed out for them. The only proof is the thing the family swore never to keep, paper, and someone else's paper to check it against. And ever since crossers could come home from anywhere, the one place a turned Marlow could eat has been one world over, under their feet.

The title means the seconds a crosser gets, second helpings at Peg's table, and a second go, the family's cure. Nobody ever says so.

## 2. Genre

**Lead: Crime.** The criminals are the heroes, inside a family firm. The pursuer looks like less than he is.

- **Promise, from the Crime profile.**
  - *Crimes worth watching:* the Mayfair dip, the Marrable Street diamond room, the hand through the Ahern cold store, the Ahern tugs set adrift with every knot undone, the Obverse records room, the raid on the Register depot, and a last job into the Marlows' own stolen field. Each shows a particular Marlow mind at work.
  - *A plain-looking master pursuer:* DS Ray Ambrose, thin, vodka at his desk, taken for a drunk. He reads pour contracts, stakes out a basement, takes a rig at a traffic stop, and adds seconds to every job he leans on.
  - *A long fight between equals:* the Marlows against Ray, and against the Aherns across the river.
  - *Deception on both sides:* Rosie hides her turn; Ray hides that he feeds the family; Maggie hides what Joe was doing; Kieran hides his hunger; Peg hides, even from herself, what Frank weighed.
  - *The line crossed:* Ray feeds thieves on dead people's rations; Tess goes to "them"; Danny reaches for a boy's heart and chooses something slower, and it costs him the hand.
  - *A reckoning paid in kind:* the family that kept nothing on paper is undone, and saved, by paper. At the end, "nothing on paper" works for once: without the rolls nobody can be charged. They hand the rolls over anyway.
- **The guarantee broken: the family table is safe.** It breaks early and from inside. In episode 3 the table where Rosie is applauded turns out to be where she is starving. In episode 5 the audience watches Peg pile Liam's plate after he has been weighed, knowing since episode 1 that everything in you counts. After that every meal carries a threat, and the audience feels it at the time.
- **The genre's question:** what, if anything, would exempt someone from the rules everyone else keeps? The Marlows' unspoken answer is "People like us look after our own": no police, no clinics, no letters, no paper. The ending shows that the exemption was their cage.

**Guests.**
- **Gangster**, all season: the firm, the code at the table (Peg serves breakfast while Danny plans a war), a rival family, turncoats (Tess, then Ray), a successor (Kieran, with a pencil).
- **Detective:** Ray hunts a "spinner" who does not exist; Tess hunts the thing that turns her family. The clues are fair. The answer to "who is to blame?" is no one person, only habits.
- **Horror**, in single scenes: running out inside concrete, the flat stump, Danny's hand coming back without its fingers, the black water, the boy weighed a tenth of a second short.
- **Science Fiction:** one hinge technology, the rig, and its traffic jams, followed all the way out, including one world over.

## 3. The world

### 3.1 The normal

Grays, on the Thames estuary, in 2026. Spring into summer. Container cranes at Tilbury, the QEII bridge in the haze, lorries, gravel, gulls, the Nobody Tide slapping at pontoons.

Thirty-one years ago, in 1995, something arrived in a field on Mucking Marsh. It left no crater and no trail, and it was already flickering in and out of the world. The field was the Marlows' tenancy. The Ministry fenced the field, took the object, and paid Peg and Frank Marlow enough to buy a gravel pit and a public weighbridge in Grays. Peg keeps the Ministry's receipt framed on the office wall, as a grievance: "One object, found wet, sand adhering." From the object came the **rig**: a padded belt, a collar with a small lamp, and a wrist unit. It lifts you off the world for a few seconds. Crossing is now a trade like plumbing: surgeons, rescue crews, couriers, and thieves.

Marlow Aggregates is a gravel pit, a concrete plant and a public, certified weighbridge. The weighbridge office is Peg's kitchen. It has two certified scales, and both print onto the same audit roll: the big bridge outside, which weighs lorries in twenty-kilo steps, and the platform scale in the office, which the family calls **the little bridge**. It weighs sacks, loose loads and people, to ten grams. By day the family sells concrete to the City. By night they "dip": they cross into vaults and take things out. A big dip comes four or five times a year; concrete pays the bills.

### 3.2 What changed, and what that changed (departures and ripples)

**Departure 1: walls mean nothing, so weight means everything.**
- Gold sits under the City as dust in concrete. The concrete is often **Marlow Mix**, poured by Danny's day crew. The City buys it because thieves designed it: Danny sells by day what the family knows by night ("I know what slows a crosser down"). So the longest jobs in London are the ones the Marlows built.
- Locksmiths became weightmen. Vaults have **weigh-walls**, which feel a body in the dark within four metres. So that licensed couriers and surgeons passing next door don't set off every vault in the City, a wall floods the goods with tungsten beads only when a body stays within its four metres for more than three seconds. Short visits slip under; one long visit always trips it.
- Jobs that took two seconds in Frank's day now take eight or nine.
- Police don't bother with cuffs on a crosser who has a rig. They take the rig.
- A thief's craft includes the knot and the seal: a crosser leaves a banded, knotted, lead-sealed crate exactly as it was, and empty, and can undo any knot without touching either end. Crossers wear slip-ons and buckles, never laces: "Knots don't hold in the black."

**Departure 2: people come back turned.**
- About forty thousand Britons have come back mirror-turned. Most turned in the unlicensed years before 2014, when anybody with a rig crossed for as long as they liked and nobody wrote down for how long. Turned, a body gets nothing from ordinary food or medicine, and no ordinary germ can touch it. It starves in about six months unless fed mirror food.
- **Licensed crossers**, such as surgeons who lift tumours out of children without a cut, and rescue crews, have been held to three seconds since 2014, when a surgeon ran out mid-operation with a child's tumour in her hand. The limit was set as a margin against running out. Nobody counted what else it did: licensed crossers hardly ever turn. The trade thinks them soft.
- **The Register** (the Handedness Register) is a health service people are glad of, run like a diabetes clinic. It feeds the turned free mirror rations and asks no questions, least of all how long anyone was in the black, since for most claimants the answer would confess a crime. A turned body's own gut germs are mirror life. One person's germs can barely feed on anything here and are a slow, rare risk to open wounds, newborns and the very ill, so the Register's rules are modest ones: claimants use sealed "Register loos" and don't handle food for sale. Since the Register Privacy Order of 2019, the police can no longer read the list. The Marlows don't know this. The letter is in Peg's drawer, unopened.
- **Passports** read HAND: C (common) or HAND: T (turned). Many countries refuse HAND: T travellers, for fear of mirror life. Airports test every traveller with a sugar strip at the gate, because a photograph can't show handedness and a strip can.
- **Obverse plc** began in 2009 as the first mirror kitchen, a Knightsbridge restaurant at £800 a meal. Now it makes the Register's rations under contract, and the Army's rations for the **Hand Company**, turned volunteers recruited from the Register who walk unmasked through plague wards. Its share price tripled on the Army contract. It sells a private cure, **Restoration**: £180,000, seventy per cent success, method confidential. Its face is Dr Helen Osei, a clinician who was cured by Restoration herself.
- **Handshakes.** Two turned people who hold each other in the black will bring one of them back straight, but the one who comes back straight leaves the hand that held on behind in the black. It is done in back rooms. Most turned people would rather be fed free than lose a hand on a coin toss.
- **Background law, never named on screen.** A Convention of 2012 bans stepping out onto another leaf and carrying seed or living cultures through the black. It followed an affair in 2011 when an army carried a rival's seed bank through the black to turn it, and the seed came back turned, and so did the soldiers. A law of 2016 makes looking inside a body a search, after ten years of courts over police lookers who saw a ring in a man's stomach and could not see why he had swallowed it. On screen these are only anecdotes.

**Departure 3: the neighbours, felt only as weight.**
- Everyone knows there is a world a hair's breadth away, because it can be weighed. Kieran's school poster says dark matter is "the neighbours' galaxies" and that SETI spent sixty years pointing the wrong way.
- The BBC reports "next-door events": a black-hole merger with nothing in our sky, a gravity blip over the North Sea.
- Thames watermen shrug at the **Nobody Tide**, a slosh on no moon's timetable. The Aherns, who run tugs, keep tables for it. It is the neighbours' moon.
- Crossers map the next world by its pull, in the dark: **the Hill**, a mountain felt over Mucking Marsh, and **the Deep**, a soft, wide pull under Grays. The saying is "The Hill takes you; the Deep keeps you." It dates from the years before spares: whoever followed the Hill stepped out inside rock, and whoever followed the Deep was never seen again.

**The main departure carries the theme.** A world where your safety depends on one number you cannot feel (your seconds, your hand, your weight) is a world that punishes anyone who will not write that number down and check it against someone else's.

### 3.3 The rules of crossing, as the audience learns them (shown, never lectured)

- **The black.** Between worlds it is black. The only light is the lamp your field carries. Within arm's reach you see the inside of everything: the inside of a wall, a guard's ribs, a painting in its crate, a beating heart. Beyond that, nothing. Radio can't cross, so you are alone with your count.
- **The budget.** On a Marlow rig, every kilo under a hundred buys a fifth of a second. Everything inside your field counts: your cargo, the air in your lungs, the breakfast in your stomach. Your fingertips tingle as the time runs out. Stay past it and you don't come back: you become a weight in an empty room, slowly spreading.
- **The wall.** A weigh-wall floods after three seconds.
- **The coin.** Any crossing can bring you back turned, and you can't feel it happen. What the odds are, and what changes them, nobody outside Obverse knows.
- **Facing home.** The only thing that tells you which way is home is the tug of home. Follow a different tug and you step out one world over.
- **The spare** (taught in episode 5). Since 2016 every rig carries one. Once you have stepped out onto a world, any world, it can fire once more for getting back. It is no use in the black.

### 3.4 Everyday surplus (taken for granted, never explained)

Phones have a "turned mode". The ticket printer chatters like a till. Peg's chalk board of everyone's weight hangs beside Frank's one photo, a big man, and the framed Ministry receipt. Old newspaper cuttings, framed: police forensics on Marlow jobs, "clean translation, no rotation". Peg is diabetic and pricks her finger every morning; her glucose meter lives in the cutlery drawer. Peg's old brass shop balance, from the farm, weighs her baking. Terry cooks in yellow washing-up gloves, a family habit nobody explains. Crossers' slip-ons by the door. The dog, Sixpence, an old lurcher, has always growled at Terry. Dilys's launderette van comes on Tuesdays. The Register's white tubs have batch codes printed on the lids. Lorry drivers eat bacon rolls in Peg's kitchen while they wait for tickets. Estuary mud, fog, the Tilbury ferry. Posters on the tube: "IMMUNE. FED. NEEDED. Join the Hand Company." A minute's silence is held beside a lost crosser's weigh-wall reading, as the lost are mourned now.

## 5. The show's own new guesses

The Fourth Direction's own stipulations are all accepted (many leaves carrying worlds; the field holds atoms for seconds, longer for less mass; gravity felt at human scale; the neighbours' life is the other hand). On top of those, the show makes **six** guesses of its own. They are declared here once. No writer may add a seventh, on screen or off.

1. **Drift grows with time.** The chance of coming back turned rises with every second in the black. The table: 2 s, none ever recorded; 3 s, 1 in 5,000; 4 s, 1 in 12; 5 s, 1 in 6; 6 s, 1 in 4; 7 s, 1 in 3; 8 s, 1 in 2; 9 s, 3 in 5; 10 s, 7 in 10. Between two rows, take the nearer one, or say "about". Nobody on screen ever gives a reason for the shape; only counting reveals it, which is the book's point and the season's. (For writers only, if a reason is ever needed: a slow, steady roll in the dark, like a boat swinging on its mooring, so almost nothing turns until the roll nears a quarter turn, and the odds pass a half as it nears half a turn.)
2. **Budget.** On a Marlow rig, every kilo under a hundred buys a fifth of a second. Everything inside the field counts. Clinic rigs buy more; nobody outside Obverse knows how much.
3. **The cargo.** Whatever rides inside one field (a painting, a crab, a case of rations, a breakfast) turns or slides with its carrier, always. So nobody can turn a thing without turning with it.
4. **Next door, here.** Under Grays the neighbours have a sea whose surface stands about eight metres above our ground. Over Mucking Marsh a mountain rises out of that sea, an island: the Hill. Where the Hill's shore meets our ground level, at the low water of the neighbours' tide (which runs to the same moon as the Nobody Tide), is the Marlows' old field. On 4 April the neighbours take the top off the Hill, a mountain's worth of rock moved elsewhere. Its pull on Grays changes, and Grays scales read 1.1% light from then on (the source's own dial, applied here). Its shore, at sea level, does not move.
5. **The spare.** Standard on every rig since 2016. Once a crosser has stepped out onto a leaf, any leaf, the rig can fire once more, within a minute, for half its seconds. The spare's field is a tight one that holds only the crosser and whatever touches them, so it can fire in water, and it is blind. It cannot fire from the black. (Revised from version 1, where the spare was an ordinary second field.)
6. **The grip.** When two crossers, each in their own field, hold each other in the black, the grip turns one of them relative to the other, and which one is a coin toss. The one who turns leaves behind in the black whatever of them was doing the holding or being held, cut off at a clean plane as in a partial return. Only flesh holding flesh does this: a brush of fingers slips, and a rope or pole between two fields is simply cut where they part. (New in version 2. Without a price, two turned people could cure one of themselves for nothing by holding hands, and the whole Register would empty in a few rounds.)

**Derived, with nothing new added.**
- Radio can't cross, so the crosser is alone.
- A mirrored canvas can't be a forgery.
- Glucose can't revive a turned body, and a straight glucose meter reads LO on turned blood, because its enzyme never crossed. A meter made for turned blood (the Register's T-meter) reads LO on ours.
- A field fired in water fills with water and can't lift you, because everything inside the bubble crosses. So a crosser who stepped out into the Deep before spares could never fire home ("the Deep keeps you"). Stepping out inside the neighbours' rock kills you ("the Hill takes you").
- A hand sent through a wall stays fixed to a body still on the leaf, so it has an up and never turns. It is also blind, because the eye has to be inside the bubble to see. Thieves send hands to lift by feel; surgeons must go in bodily.
- In the black a knot has a way out it never had, so knots slip, and a crosser can undo any knot without touching either end.
- A turn can't be felt. Afterwards the turned person sees the world reversed but can't prove which side flipped. A turned reader sees print the right way round only in a mirror. A turned person who has trained herself to write so that straight people can read it will write in mirror script the day she is cured.
- A weighbridge or platform scale measures weight, the pull on what stands on it, so a mountain changed next door makes it lie. A load-cell platform also feels a body standing in the black over it, roughly as its weight, the way a weigh-wall does. A rig counts mass, a beam balance or steelyard compares mass, and a weigh-wall feels a body's own pull, so none of those lies.
- Mirror germs can't be recognised or fought here, but they can barely feed here either: only on fat, alcohol and the few foods with no hand. One person's germs are a slow, rare risk to open wounds, newborns and the very ill. A whole mirror food web (sea water, weed that lives on light, a crab) is a different order of risk. To the neighbours, our life is the mirror life.
- Because cargo turns with its carrier, a thief can't turn an enemy's food without turning with it, and food could be turned only by carrying it on long crossings, which licensed rigs never make. So mirror food is built by chemistry, as far as anyone knows. What Obverse does is left open.
- The step onto the neighbours' shore is itself a crossing, so it is itself a toss, and its odds depend on how long it takes: a turned crosser who turns on the way arrives straight, starving there and a risk to them.
- Nobody can guide another crosser through the black by the hand, because the grip would turn one of them.
- The neighbours' sea rises and falls to the same moon that makes the Nobody Tide.

**On screen.** Guess 2 and the lore of the Hill and the Deep appear in episode 1. Guess 1 is glimpsed in episodes 1 and 2 (surgeons held to three seconds; Terry's eleven failed hops; the painting's seven) and stated in episode 6 (the protocol). Guess 3 appears in episode 1 (the painting turned with Rosie) and episode 4 (Tess on the seed bank), and pays off in episode 9 (Shaun's case). Guess 4's Hill and Deep are lore in episode 1; the Hill's change is episode 4; the sea is episode 9; the door is worked out on screen in episode 10 from records shown in episodes 1, 7 and 9. Guess 5 is taught in episode 5 and used in episodes 9 and 10. Guess 6 is planted in episode 2 (the clinic sign), stated in episode 4 (Tess's warning), paid in episode 6 (Danny's fingers) and used again in episode 10.

## 6. The culture that never questions: the Marlows

The Marlows are warm, funny, loyal and admired in Grays. Their code is spoken, never written, and every rule was right once.

| Rule | Why it was right | How it grows the doom |
|---|---|---|
| **Nothing on paper** | Paper is evidence; its fibres even show police whether a thief turned | No pattern across years can be seen, so each turning feels like one person's bad luck. And since only thieves cross for long, and thieves keep nothing, nobody in the country has a record of what long crossings do |
| **Never go back twice** | In Frank's day a second visit walked thieves into a police stake-out, so a Marlow takes the whole haul in one trip | A weigh-wall floods only when a body lingers past three seconds, so one long trip trips the wall that two short ones slip under. And since the odds of turning grow with time, one nine-second trip turns far more people than three short ones. Admired, and now it costs twice |
| **Make weight** | Lighter means more seconds; Peg made it law after Frank "ran out because he was fifteen stone" | Thinness is cheered, so a starving crosser looks like the most devoted one. It doesn't cause the turning; it hides it. And a crosser made to weight is sent with no margin (Frank, Liam) |
| **Lightest goes longest** | The lightest has seconds to spare | The best and youngest get the longest jobs |
| **Face home** | It keeps you on your own world; before spares, whoever followed another tug stepped out into rock or sea and never came back | Since the spare came in (2016), a crosser could have followed the Deep and fired home. Nobody tried, because the rule and the saying were made in the years without spares. The family also believes facing home keeps you straight, so a turn means you failed, and the turned hide it |
| **We feed our own** | It kept the turned off a list the police could read | Each turned mouth costs £300 a day: more jobs, longer jobs, more turned. And since 2019 the police can't read the list anyway (the letter is in the drawer) |
| **Never go to them** | Clinics and, once, the Register reported crossers; in 2001 even the Ministry's offer of food came with a police interview | It cuts them off from the one number that would save them, and from the letter that would have saved Liam |
| **Blood pays blood** | For twenty years it kept rivals away | A turning with an enemy nearby becomes a war |
| **Official letters go in the drawer** (Peg's own rule, never stated) | They were always bad news: the Ministry, the field, the bailiffs | One habit, three letters: in 2001 the offer that would have fed Frank; in 2019 the news that the police can't read the Register; in April 2026 the order to re-verify the scales |

**The doom, step by step.**
1. When jobs took two seconds (1998 to about 2010), almost nobody turned. Police forensic reports said "clean translation" and Peg framed the cuttings. "Marlows come back straight" became the family's pride. Frank turned on a four-second job in 2001, bad luck at one in twelve, and nobody knew what was wrong with him, Frank included: "off his food", "his sugar", the jam jar, the papers "printed funny". A Ministry letter offering him screening and a place on its small ration scheme for turned crossers came with a police interview, so it went into the drawer. He starved for six months. Peg weighed him at 10 st 2, pencilled "7.1" on his ticket, and sent him on a seven-second job with a tenth to spare. She remembered him as fifteen stone and made "make weight" law.
2. Families like theirs got so good that the City moved to weight. Danny sold what the family knew by night as Marlow Mix by day. Every year's pour added seconds to the family's own jobs.
3. The family answered with its rules, more carefully and more lovingly: never go back twice, so each trip got longer; make weight, so each crosser got lighter; lightest goes longest. Tess grew proud of squeezing out tenths.
4. Long jobs turn people. Each turning read as a failure to face home, so the turned hid it (Rosie) or were fed at home (Terry), which meant more jobs.
5. With no paper, nobody saw that every turned Marlow had been on a long job. The family's cure, "go again" with short hops, never worked, and nobody counted. Nor did anyone else: the lawful world crosses for three seconds, and the Register never asks how long.
6. One turned near a rival family (Aoife Ahern), and blood paid blood: Con, Shaun's leg, Liam.
7. Then the world changed again, one world over. The neighbours moved the top of a mountain; the family's own scale began to lie; a letter saying so went into the drawer; a boy was sent out with a tenth to spare.
8. And ever since the spare came in, ten years ago, the one place a turned Marlow could eat lay one world over, in the Deep every Marlow had felt on every crossing. "Face home", a rule made for the years without spares, made it unthinkable. It cost Terry five years.

**Why "always" is earned.** Frank shows the code was already killing its founder twenty-four years ago: the drawer chose hunger for him, "make weight" arithmetic sent him with a tenth to spare, and the family's memory turned his death into the lesson that killed Liam. The Aherns keep the same code with one difference: Maggie opens her post. She filled in a Register form for her turned uncle, and her father put it in the stove. One right person, overruled. The Aherns wrote down every job's seconds for twenty years, for the tug pickups, and never who turned; the Marlows remembered who turned and wrote nothing. Neither family could see it alone. In the last image a girl is cheered on a bathroom scale while the front page that would save her lies under it, unread. The world always changes eventually, here twice in one spring. A family that cannot change its mind was only waiting.

**One right person is not enough.** Tess works it out on the ferry in episode 7. Rosie stands with her at the table, and both are overruled. Alone, Tess makes things worse: she hands Rosie to the ward. Only when the whole family writes its crossings down together, in episode 10, does anything change, and the door is found by three adults laying their memories side by side.

**Asking is not punished.** The one time Rosie breaks "face home", she finds the food, and her landing gives the clue that explains Liam. Kieran's homework log saves the family. Tess is wrong often (Mayfair's one long trip, Liam's missing margin, the ward), so the heroine is plainly not always right.

## 7. Characters

Eight principals. The inner circle, where care must run deepest, is Tess, Rosie, Peg and Danny.

**TESS MARLOW, 38, the weigher.** She keeps every number in her head. The family's lightest crosser from fourteen to twenty-six, she never turned; jobs were short then. Now she plans every dip. Single mother of Kieran.
- *Want:* keep the family fed and whole; this season, keep Rosie working and get her well.
- *Wound:* the week her father died, Peg made the fourteen-year-old the family's crosser, and made her light. She has counted every mouthful since.
- *Flaw:* she answers every loss with a tighter number, and she decides things for other people. "Margins are for people who can't count."
- *Secret:* she planned Mayfair as one 9.4-second visit when two short ones would have done, because one trip is the rule and tenths are her pride.
- *Levers:* competence (the pleasure of watching her plan), interiority (we see her mouth move as she counts), love for Kieran and Rosie, and the pencil behind her ear that she never uses.
- *Arc:* she keeps the numbers in her head; she writes them down (episode 7, the back of a ferry timetable); she hands the pencil to Kieran and asks each person instead of deciding for them (episode 10).
- *Voice:* numbers instead of feelings. Short sentences, tenths, no swearing in front of Kieran. "How much?" "Near enough isn't." When frightened she gets more exact.

**ROSIE MARLOW, 22, Danny's daughter, the lightest adult crosser.**
- *Want:* to be the best ever. Secretly, to lift tumours out of children like the surgeons in the videos she watches. After Mayfair, to fix herself before anyone knows.
- *Wound:* she has been praised for being hungry since she was twelve.
- *Flaw:* pride that hides weakness, and passes the habit on.
- *Secret:* she has been turned since the cold open of episode 1. She saw it at once in the print on a crisp packet and has told herself since that the world is printed funny.
- *Levers:* competence, humour, courage; from episode 3, the audience re-reading every cheer; from episode 5, her guilt.
- *Arc:* she hides; she coaches Liam into her own habits and times his last runs; she tells the table the truth and stands with Tess (episode 7); she walks out of the ward to find food nobody can cut off; she chooses to stay turned.
- *Voice:* quick and jokey, turns everything into a boast. "Show-off, me." When she is lying she gets funnier.

**PEG MARLOW, 72, the founder.** Frank's widow. Diabetic. Runs the weighbridge and the kitchen.
- *Want:* a family that is together, proud and owes nobody. Underneath: her field back.
- *Wound:* the Ministry took her farm, and the black took Frank.
- *Flaw:* certainty. She puts official letters in the drawer unopened, and remembers what she needs to.
- *Secret, kept even from herself:* she weighed Frank the morning he died, wrote "7.1" on his ticket, and sent him with a tenth to spare; and the letter that would have fed him is in her drawer.
- *Levers:* warmth, humour, the fry-up, the chalk board, her daily finger-prick.
- *Arc:* the code in person becomes the first Marlow to write on paper ("Frank. 10 st 2. Turned."), says "turned" to her brother, stands on her field again, and carries the rolls to the police herself.
- *Voice:* Essex, sayings of her own making. "We made that floor, we can have what's under it." "Hills don't move." "Then you've had a good day."

**DANNY MARLOW, 41, the foreman.** Runs the concrete crews by day. At 88 kilos, as the family's scales have read since April (89 in truth), he is good for a hop of about two seconds, or for sending his hand through a wall.
- *Want:* keep his kids safe; then revenge.
- *Wound:* at seventeen he was too heavy to take his father's place, and his little sister was chosen.
- *Flaw:* every loss is an attack.
- *Secret:* he said yes to Liam.
- *Levers:* love for his kids, humour, grief, the physical craft of his hand.
- *Arc:* he gambles his hand to turn an enemy and loses both the toss and the fingers; carries his son's printout unread from episode 5 to episode 9; pushes his share of the last tub to Rosie; refuses to send Kieran; asks the question that explains Liam; says no to handing over the rolls, then yes; goes to the Register himself.

He shows his Register card without shame.

### Episode 1: "Straight"
*The Marlows pull off the cleanest dip of their lives and get back a painting nobody believes is real.*

**Cold open.** 3 a.m., Mayfair, early March. Rosie, 44 kilos, in slip-ons, steps off the world beside a private bank's art store. In the black we see only what her collar lamp lights: the inside of the wall, a guard's ribs rising and falling, the painting in a banded, knotted, lead-sealed crate. In the van, Tess counts aloud: "...two... three. There's the wall." Tungsten beads roar down. Rosie lifts the canvas out; the crate stays sealed and knotted; she lifts clear with half a second before the beads bury the shelf. At 3:12 something heavy passes in the dark. She flinches. Fingertips tingle; she faces home. In the van Danny asks, as the family always does, "Straight?" "Straight." She looks a beat too long at a crisp packet on the dashboard.

**Act one: the table.** Fry-up at the weighbridge office, under the chalk board of weights ("a fifth of a second a kilo under a hundred"), Frank's one photo (a big man) and the framed Ministry receipt. Peg, serving: "And everything in you counts, breakfast too." 9.4 seconds is the longest job a Marlow has ever done, and the family toasts Tess's tenths. The rules come out as family banter: "Never go back twice." "Lightest goes longest." "Face home." Dilys's laundry van drops off Terry's white tubs. Terry, cooking in yellow gloves, eats his tub, a nip of gin and a spoon of dripping: "Fat and spirits. It's all that sticks." Sixpence growls at Terry, as always, and at Rosie: "He's jealous, she's been away." Liam carries the canvas in from the van like a tray of glass. They burn its tissue wrapping in the office stove; on the wall, the framed cuttings show why ("clean translation, no rotation").

**Act two: the painting.** *The Assayer's Wife* lies on the table: a woman at a window with a balance. In the catalogue cutting Peg pins to the wall, the window is on the left. On the table it is on the right. Nobody looks; the camera lets the audience. Danny's day crew, Liam among them, pours Marlow Mix into a bank's vault floor. Peg to Kieran: "Your grandad was fifteen stone when the black took him. That's why we make weight." Kieran wants to cross; in the flooded gravel pit Tess times him blindfold: "Can't see up? Breathe out a bit. Follow your bubbles." Terry, cooking, tells him the black never gave him back: "Eleven times I went again." And what happens if you stay too long: "You come apart. There's nothing in the black to hold you together." Kieran: "What about the Hill?" Terry: "The Hill takes you. The Deep keeps you."

**Act three: paper.** Mr Pinnock of Trading Standards checks the scales: "Twenty-five years of audit rolls in your loft, Mrs Marlow, by law." "Bloody paper." On the office TV a licensed surgeon lifts a tumour out of a child in three seconds; Rosie watches every one of them. Danny: "Three seconds. Soft." On the radio, unremarked, the BBC reports a black-hole merger "with nothing in our sky". Peg's morning finger-prick; the meter goes back in the cutlery drawer.

**Act four: the birthday.** Kieran's birthday. Peg weighs the flour for his cake on her brass balance from the old farm shop. Everyone is weighed on the little bridge to cheers; Rosie reads 43.6 and gets the loudest cheer. Liam reads 72.0, and the cheer turns into a joke about the big lad. She turns a jam lid the wrong way ("our Rosie never could open a jar") and signs Kieran's card in flawless mirror script, next to the card on the fridge from when she was fourteen, also in mirror script. "Show-off," says Tess, as the family always does. Late that night Terry notices the level in his pot has dropped.

**Act five: the sale.** The insurer's expert examines the painting for the buy-back: the light falls from the wrong side. It's a copy. No money.

**Twist.** The perfect job has stolen a forgery.

**Hook.** Ray Ambrose lays two photos on Peg's counter. "Linen has a hand, Mrs Marlow. This canvas came back the wrong way round. It's the original." Peg: "Then the bank hung a copy." Leaving, Ray to Tess: "I met your dad once." "You never met my dad." "I met what was left of him."

**Gaps.** *Opens:* whether the painting is real (Peg's copy theory against Ray's); the weight at 3:12; what was left of Frank; Terry's pot; the Hill and the Deep (lore, a wonder). *Closes:* whether the dip worked.

### Episode 2: "The Assayer's Wife"
*To sell the painting by Friday, the Marlows must make it look like itself, and Rosie secretly uses every attempt as a cure.*

**Cold open.** 04:17. Every gravimeter on Earth twitches once. In a hundred City basements every weigh-wall floods its vault with beads at the same moment. The BBC: "a next-door event". In the Grays office Kieran, up late with his school project, watches the 20 kg test weight on the little bridge read 20.00, then 20.31, then 20.00. He writes all three down. Above his desk, his poster: "SETI pointed the wrong way for sixty years", and a map of dark matter labelled "the neighbours' galaxies". In the dark bathroom Rosie steps on the scale: 43.1. She smiles.

**Act one: Peg's plan.** A buyer will pay if the painting matches its catalogue, but he flies home on Friday. Peg's answer: hop it until it turns back. Rosie volunteers at once. Tess, uneasy at how grey she looks, allows only three-second hops. The morning after the Twitch, insurers demand thicker bead floors. Danny, to a bank's facilities man, with Liam holding the drawings: "I know what slows a crosser down." His order book triples. Peg: "Every job just got a second longer. Good for us both ways."

**Act two: seven hops.** In the office, seven three-second hops, Wednesday to Friday. After each the family peers at the painting, and Rosie's eyes go to the catalogue cutting on the wall. It stays wrong. The audience reads it as care for the painting; it is Rosie checking whether she has turned back with it. Terry, gently: "The black never gave me back." Rosie complains the new ticket roll is printed backwards; Tess checks it: fine. "You been at the vodka?" Friday evening the buyer's flight leaves.

**Act three: the flu.** Kieran brings a flu home from school and the whole house goes down with it, all but two. Rosie and Terry are the only ones on their feet. Terry brings her a tub on a tray and gives her a look she doesn't meet. Rosie nurses Kieran through the night, and is good at it. On the phone, Terry swaps gravy recipes with his friend "Con": "Con says a drop of gin in it." Rosie renews her passport at the Post Office; the form asks HAND: C or T and she ticks C; the clerk says they strip-test you at the gate now, and HAND: T doesn't fly. On her phone, a Lisbon clinic offers Restoration at half the price; she closes it.

**Act four: Harley Street.** Rosie walks into the Obverse clinic "to price a Restoration for Uncle Terry", she tells the receptionist, and we believe her. A sign: "Patients must never touch staff in transit." The receptionist's left hand is an artificial one; nobody mentions it. On the tube poster outside: "IMMUNE. FED. NEEDED. Join the Hand Company." Dr Helen Osei gives her the price. In the waiting room, eating lard on a cracker, a Register card on the table beside him, sits Ray Ambrose.

**Twist.** The detective is turned. His vodka and lard are dinner.

**Hook.** "A hundred and eighty thousand. Seventy per cent." Outside, Rosie looks at the jeweller's across the road. Ray, at the clinic door: "Tell your uncle it's seventy per cent. They don't tell you how."

**Gaps.** *Opens:* what Restoration's method is; what the Twitch was (a wonder); what Ray knows. *Closes:* whether hops can fix the painting (not these ones); what Ray eats.

### Episode 3: "Making Weight" (first major reversal)

Danny poured its floor in 2023; Liam, who barrowed the beads, says it's thickest at the back wall. Tess's plan: two short trips from the basement next door, under three seconds each, so the weigh-wall never floods. Peg: "Never go back twice." One trip, the whole tray. Tess loses.

**Act two: the pursuer.** Ray is at the concrete plant, reading Marlow Mix pour contracts, and he has a car outside the basement next door to Marrable Street. He finds Tess. He believes someone is spinning the Marlows: a murder that takes six months and looks like a diet. The law won't let him look at which side her niece's heart is on ("a man had a ring in his stomach, we could see it, couldn't see why he'd swallowed it; ten years of courts"). **Act four: the diamond room.** Ten seconds in the black under Marlow concrete: the lamp shows the beads inside the floor, the tray, a sleeping guard's heart. Tess, counting in the van: "Three. There's the wall." The beads come; Rosie has the tray. The tingle. Home. "Straight?" "Straight." On the little bridge afterwards, Rosie folds, and Liam carries her in.

**Act five: the meter.** Tess grabs Peg's meter: LO. "She's hypo." A glucose gel into Rosie's mouth. Nothing. Another. The meter still says LO. Terry comes with his pot. One spoonful, and Rosie's eyes open. Terry: "She's been at my pot since the party."

**Twist.** Rosie has been turned since Mayfair. The thinness they cheered was starvation. The audience re-reads the birthday card, the jar, the dog, the lard, the backwards roll, the flu that left her standing, the seven glances at the painting, and Harley Street.

**Hook.** At the table Rosie says, "Something took hold of me in the black." Two bodies in the log, and the Aherns work Mayfair. Danny is already reaching for his coat. Peg: "Blood pays blood."

**Gaps.** *Opens:* who took hold of her (the "spinner"); can she be cured, and how. *Closes:* what is wrong with Rosie; why the pot drops.

### Episode 4: "The Other Bank"
*The war begins, and across the river the enemy is living the same nightmare.*

**Cold open.** 4 April, 05:40. A quake with no fault shakes Thurrock; cups walk off shelves. The radio, the same BBC voice that reported the silent merger, calls it a next-door event. Danny, back from a practice hop: "The Hill's moved." Peg: "Hills don't move." In the office Kieran's test weight reads 19.78. He frowns and writes it down.

**Act one: blood pays blood.** Danny's first plan: take the Aherns' ration store through the black until it turns, so their food starves them. Tess: "Then you'd be eating it." Whatever you carry turns with you. She tells Kieran, in one line, about an army that once carried a rival's seed bank through the black to turn it: "Seed came back turned. So did the soldiers." Danny: "Then I'll turn one of them." Tess: "Grab someone in there and one of you turns. Coin says which. And whoever turns leaves the hand in there." So they will steal the store instead. Rosie is barred from the job; Peg won't risk a turned girl. So she coaches Liam, who wants in: "Skip the fry-up. Sleep in your coat." She is passing on her own wound, and she knows it.

**Act two: the hand.** Night, Gravesend. Danny sends his hand, which lasts a long time in the black, through the Ahern cold-store wall. The hand is blind. We stay on Danny's face, eyes shut, as he counts tubs by touch, and Tess, timing: "His hand's tied to him. It knows which way's up. It won't turn." The crew lifts the Aherns' mirror stores, tub by tub.

**Act three: across the river.** The Aherns: Maggie, Joe, Declan, and Con, turned, already thin, because the Ahern supply off the Rotterdam boats has been short since Aoife died. Maggie holds out a Register form she has filled in for him. Declan puts it in the stove: "We feed our own." Siobhan, 13, asks her grandad to chalk her weight on the kitchen board, and he does, proudly. On their fridge, Aoife's photo and a weigh-wall printout. At 3 a.m. Maggie makes up a bottle for Aoife's baby, Cian: "Her milk was wrong. He screamed three days before we understood." Con, whose food has just vanished, rings Terry, his friend by phone: "Your lot took my dinner, Tel." Terry's gravy friend is an Ahern.

**Act four: light.** At Grays, Mick the lorry driver says the big bridge has him two hundred kilos light. Peg: "Then you've had a good day." Kieran: "My weight's gone light too, Nan." "Stop wasting roll." A Trading Standards envelope goes into Peg's dresser drawer unopened, on top of years of others. At breakfast Liam slips his fry-up to Sixpence under the table.

**Act five: 02:04.** Morning. Sorting the night's tickets for his log, Kieran finds one nobody booked: 02:04, the little bridge, 58.4 kg. Nobody was in the office. He files it as a glitch and keeps it.

**Twist.** The Aherns lost Aoife last autumn. She turned on a nine-second job in a bank where a Marlow crew was working the floor below. They have been at war for months, and the Marlows never knew.

**Hook.** Maggie rings Tess: "Con's heart pills were in them tubs. Made to his hand. There's no more."

**Gaps.** *Opens:* the 02:04 ticket; why the scales and the test weight read light (planted, not pressed); why the neighbours moved a mountain (a wonder). *Closes:* why the Aherns hate them; who Con is.

### Episode 5: "Delivered"
*The war turns lethal, and the family's heaviest boy starves himself to join it.*

**Cold open.** Peg opens her own safe with its key, for the first time in years; the family always just hops into it. A bullet sits on the painting. The safe was locked. "It's been delivered."

**Act one: the radius.** Ray brings a coroner's report: Southend, 2019, a bullet in a skull with no entry wound. "Coroner wrote 'delivered'. That's what your safe's telling you. Whoever did it stood beside your safe." Peg: nobody stood there. Kieran shows his 02:04 ticket: 58.4. Peg moves Kieran's bed into the office: "Nobody stands near that boy without the scale knowing." On the A13 that afternoon Ray pulls Rosie's van over and takes her rig: "We don't cuff crossers, love." A rig costs more than a car. The family is short again.

**Act two: Con.** Con Ahern dies of his heart, thin, with no pill that fits him. Terry rings Con's phone and Siobhan answers it. "Con used to ring me about gravy." The Marlows are killers too.

**Act three: the knots.** Shaun's reprisal, at night on the Gravesend moorings: in the black beside the Ahern tugs he undoes every mooring knot without touching either end, and the tugs swing out on the ebb with their lines still lying round the bollards. He comes back a hair slow, his leg below the knee still in the black when his field closed. The stump is a clean flat plane, bloodless for a second, then not. Terry dresses it, in his gloves.

**Act four: no margin.** Liam, who has secretly lost nearly ten kilos, begs for the next dip: the Ahern salvage lock-up, cash for Rosie's cure. Danny says yes. Rosie has been timing his blindfold runs in the pit. Before breakfast Tess weighs him on the little bridge: 62.4, plus 1.5 kilos of notes to carry out, 63.9, so 7.2 seconds. The job needs 7.1. "Margins are for people who can't count." Tess checks the spare light on his rig. Liam: "Can I use it if I'm short?" Tess: "It's for if you step out wrong. In the black it's no use to you." She goes out to load the van. The camera stays at the table while Peg piles Liam's plate with the lucky fry-up, and he eats every bite.

**Act five: the tenth.** In the black, inside the lock-up's bead floor, Liam counts. The tingle comes early, while he is still inside the concrete, where stepping out means death. He pushes on. A tenth of a second from home, he runs out.

**Twist.** Liam runs out, though every number said he would make it. The Ahern weigh-wall goes on reading him.

**Hook.** Maggie lets the family stand one minute beside the reading, as the lost are mourned now, and gives Danny the printout: 65.1 kg. He folds it into his wallet without looking. Rosie cannot look at Danny. That night Kieran writes a second column under his test weight: his own weight, 39.6. At home Danny weighs himself: 88. "Two seconds is enough to grab someone."

**Gaps.** *Opens:* why Liam ran out (the audience saw the fry-up and suspects it; nobody in the family compares 63.9 with 65.1). *Closes:* who delivered the bullet (the Aherns, by implication).

### Episode 6: "Ten Seconds" (second major reversal, part one)
*Danny gambles his hand to turn an enemy, and Tess breaks into a clinic for a cure and finds a clock.*

**Cold open.** A week later. Danny sits in the dark office, rigged, watching the little bridge's readout, with Kieran asleep on his camp bed behind him. It is a stakeout, and Danny has done the sum: whoever came at 02:04 before will come at 02:04 again. At 02:04 the readout jumps to 58.4 with nobody on the plate. Danny steps into the black for a two-second hop. By his lamp he finds Joe Ahern mid-crossing beside the safe: Joe's ribs, his beating heart within reach of Danny's hand, an envelope inside Joe's jacket. Danny's hand hovers at the heart. Then he grabs Joe by the collar and wrenches him round. The tingle comes early. He's back. "Got him." He lifts his right hand. The four fingers are gone, cut clean across the knuckles, a flat plane, bloodless for a second.

**Act one: breakfast.** Peg's meter: LO. Sixpence growls at Danny. Terry dresses the hand, in his gloves, and slides his pot across without a word. Danny: "Tingle came early. That's the grab." Joe came back straight; in the safe, delivered after all, is an envelope addressed to Tess. Peg hands it over unopened: "From them." Tess pockets it. The Laundry's bill trebles. Kieran's second column: 39.1.

**Act two: the records room.** Tess decides to rob the cure. Rosie has no rig and Danny is maimed, so Tess crosses for the first time in twelve years: 61.0 kilos on the little bridge, a 2.5-second hop into Obverse's records room on Harley Street. The tingle comes back like an old song. By her lamp she reads the spine labels inside a locked cabinet and lifts one binder out.

**Act three: the protocol.** In the car she reads: "Transit 10.0 s. Expected restoration 0.70." Below it, a table: the odds of turning, second by second. None ever recorded at two seconds. One in five thousand at three. One in twelve at four. Seven in ten at ten.

**Act four: the sums.** Next morning Tess walks into Harley Street and puts the binder on Dr Osei's desk. "How long have you known?" "Since 2011. It cured me." Osei doesn't call the police. "That price pays for the Register's kitchens. That secret feeds forty thousand people. Put it back." Tess keeps it. At the office, silently, she runs the season through the table. The surgeons' three seconds, set after one of them ran out, and nobody counted what else the limit did. Terry's eleven two-second hops. The painting's seven three-second hops. Marrable Street's ten seconds, a seven-in-ten chance of curing Rosie that came up wrong. Mayfair's 9.4, which she could have split in two.

**Twist.** The cure is nothing but time, and so is the curse. That is why surgeons hardly ever turn, why the short hops never cured anyone, and why Rosie, sent in for 9.4, turned.

**Hook.** Tess pricks her own finger with Peg's meter: 5.4. Straight. She looks at Rosie. "I gave you nine."

**Gaps.** *Opens:* what's in Maggie's envelope; whether Tess will tell the family. *Closes:* Restoration's method; why Rosie turned; what the grab did.

### Episode 7: "Two Counts" (second major reversal, part two)
*Two enemy weighers meet on the ferry and lay their families' secrets side by side, while at home Peg sends a one-legged man into the black.*

**Cold open.** The Nobody Tide slaps the Tilbury pontoon under no moon. On the ferry at Nobody low water, Maggie sits with a waterman's tide book open at the Nobody columns. The envelope said: "Ferry. Nobody low water. Thursday. Just you."

**Act one: the loft.** Before she goes, Tess finds what nobody knew the family kept: twenty-five years of audit rolls, one ticket for every job-day weigh-in. The rolls give the dates and the weights; Tess's head gives the seconds of every job she planned; Terry's memory gives who turned. On the loft floor they name names, and the family's stories come apart: "Gary went to Spain" (he couldn't; HAND: T; he's on the Register in Leeds), "Uncle Vic's heart", "Maureen's nerves". And two that mended: cousin Lee's bad winter, "off his food till Easter, right as rain after the Canary Wharf job". Nobody ever asked why. Intercut, all episode: Peg, planning without Tess ("near enough"), sends Shaun on his first job since the leg, eight seconds, to pay the Laundry. Down in the office Kieran writes 38.7 in his second column, and nobody is there to see it.

**Act two: the Ahern side.** On neutral water Maggie brings the Ahern count: their tide books time every job to the second, for the tug pickup, and for twenty years nobody wrote down who came back turned. Joe felt a weight at 3:12 at Mayfair and flinched; Maggie had sent him to watch the Marlows work, and he never came within six metres of Rosie. Tess lays down Kieran's 02:04 ticket, 58.4 kg. Maggie: "He's fifty-nine, my Joe." Neither of them stops on it. Aoife turned on a nine-second job. Joe came back from Danny's grab straight, and still made his drop: this invitation.

**Act three: the graph.** On the back of a ferry timetable the women draw the first graph either family has ever made: seconds along the bottom, turned or not. The Ahern books hold the seconds; the Marlow memories hold who turned. Eleven Marlows and seven Ahern crossers turned in twenty-five years. Nobody under three seconds. Most over eight. And two who mended, both after a later long job. Maggie, folding the timetable: "Twenty years I've had half of this in a drawer." Intercut: as they pencil "most over eight", Shaun's wrist unit reads 8.0. He comes back. "Straight?" "Straight." He is.

**Twist.** Nobody spun anybody except Danny, and he spun himself. Con died in a war about nothing.

**Act four: the stove.** At the table Tess lays down the timetable and the Obverse binder: "Ten seconds. Seven in ten. We can cure them here." Rosie stands with her and tells the table the truth: "Nothing took hold of me. I thought I'd faced wrong, and I was ashamed." Danny, not Peg, turns on Tess: "And Liam? Did your sums do Liam too?" The count can't explain running out, and Tess has no answer. Danny: "You went to them." Peg feeds the timetable and the binder to the office stove. Then she carries a box of rolls toward it, and stops at Pinnock's certificate on the wall: without the rolls, no licence, no weighbridge. She puts the box back. She takes every rig into her safe: "No Marlow crosses till I say."

**Hook.** Tess is put out of the house. Next morning the Laundry van doesn't come.

**Gaps.** *Opens:* who is the Laundry, and why has it stopped; why Liam ran out (pressed hard now). *Closes:* who is turning the Marlows; the Ahern war's cause.

### Episode 8: "The Laundry"
*With their secret supply cut off, the Marlows find out who has really been feeding them.*

**Cold open.** The last white tub, split three ways at Peg's table with a teaspoon: Terry, Rosie, Danny. Danny pushes his share across to Rosie. "I'll sort it." It is all the forgiveness he will ever say.

**Act one: go again.** The pantry is empty; Terry, Rosie and Danny turn grey. Rosie asks Peg for ten seconds. Peg: "Ten seconds is what did it." Rosie to Danny, low: "We could hold on, in there." Danny lifts his ruined hand: "Coin says who keeps one. Not you." Peg's answer is the family cure: fourteen two-second hops for Rosie on the pit's pontoon, blindfolded, facing home, Peg with the rig key round her neck. Nothing. In the office bin, Kieran's school dinner, uneaten. His second column: 38.4.

**Act two: the batch codes.** Tess, living in her car, traces the van to Dilys's launderette, and the batch codes on the lids to seven claimant files that the Register closed to police in 2019.

**Act three: Ray's flat.** Tess knocks. Tubs, vodka, lard, and a folder of every Marlow's weight since 2002 in his own hand. Ray has been suspended: an audit has found seven dead claimants still drawing rations. "I watched your dad's weight spread for a month. I wasn't watching it again." The money the family paid is in an account in Terry's name; he never touched it. He never once asked them to change. Tess: "You fed us so you'd never have to tell us anything."

**Twist.** The Laundry is Ray. For five years the man hunting them has fed them on dead people's food.

**Act four: more.** Tess tells the family: "We never fed our own." Peg's pride breaks, and she answers with more: raid the Register's depot at Purfleet, long crossings, in a fortnight. The family votes with her. To keep Rosie off it, Tess rings the Register's night line. A crew in plain green takes Rosie to the Register ward in Whitechapel: no locks, only food. Rosie, as they take her, to the one person who stood where she stood: "You decided. Again." A nurse, handing her a T-meter: "Police don't get the list, love. Not since 2019." In the next bed a fireman, turned on a rescue in Leeds, is going home on Friday with his first ration box, and can't stop talking about his allotment.

**Hook.** Two weeks on, Rosie is fed and strong. A Hand Company recruiter sits on her bed and offers good pay and rations for life; she keeps his card. On the ward TV: the tremors since April have spelled out a molecule, a sugar of the other hand, read by the detectors that heard March's silent merger. A scientist: "Sixty years we pointed dishes at the sky." The Register's people say it may mean the neighbours' life is the other hand. Front pages: "COME ROUND FOR TEA." Her phone lights with a text from Kieran: "Nan's doing the depot tonight. They won't let me go." Rosie looks at the unlocked door, and walks out. The last shot is her empty bed.

**Gaps.** *Opens:* what's next door; will Peg's raid work; where Rosie is going. *Closes:* who fed them; why the van stopped.

### Episode 9: "The Deep" (third major reversal, part one)
*The family raids the state's pantry while Rosie breaks the family's oldest rule and finds what it hid.*

**Cold open.** Raid night. Peg does the numbers now, on the chalk board. Shaun asks for the longest leg himself: "My leg weighed five and a half. Eleven tenths, Nan. I'm worth more without it." Kieran begs to go: 38.0 kilos, 12.4 seconds, the lightest Marlow ever. The table cheers the number. Peg's chalk says yes. Danny looks at the ticket, then at the boy: he was 40 at his birthday. "You've been making weight." Liam did too. "Not him. Not after Liam." It is Danny's first no. Peg takes the rigs out of her safe and hangs them on the rack, and the crew takes all but one. They leave Kieran asleep in the office. On the last train, Rosie gets off at Grays.

**Act one: two crossings, one clock.** Intercut. At Purfleet the family lifts sealed Register cases out through bead floors. Shaun's leg is eight seconds with a 3-kilo case; the chalk said 8.9 seconds. At Grays, Rosie lets herself into the office. The one rig left on the rack is Tess's old one. She steps onto the little bridge and, for the first time in her life, does not face home. She follows the Deep's soft pull for two seconds, and steps out one world over into black water: cold, salt, no floor, her own held breath. Her main field is gone; a field can't lift you out of water. She breathes out a little, the way the pit taught her, feels which way her bubbles go, and swims after them, eight metres up, in the dark. She breaks the surface of a sea we never see, into air we never see, and takes one breath of it. Something brushes her face. With seconds of her minute left she fires her spare, a tight blind field, faces home, and lands on the little bridge drenched, with weed in her hair and a crab in her hood. The printer chatters. Kieran wakes. She gasps the breath out, and it is gone. "It's sea. Under us. All of it."

**Act two: the cases.** Dawn. The van comes back. Terry, on a two-second leg, came back turned, as everyone knew he would. Peg asks Shaun: "Straight?" "Straight." Peg's meter, in front of everyone: LO. The cases go on the table. Sixpence growls at every case but Shaun's. Kieran dabs Peg's meter on Shaun's porridge: a normal reading. Shaun's case turned with him: sealed, unopened, and now the wrong hand for every turned mouth in the house. Two weeks of food, and a case of porridge only the straight can eat. Peg: "Bad luck." Kieran writes it on the roll.

**Act three: the other hand.** Kieran wrings Rosie's jumper into a jar. A smear of the weed on Peg's meter: LO. The same smear on the T-meter the nurse gave Rosie: 4.8. Two meters, two answers, and both are right. Sixpence growls at the jar as he growls at Terry. Terry cooks the crab and eats it slowly, and holds out his plate for more. Nobody says what it tasted of. That night he tells Peg he is going. Kieran, for his log, weighs the jar of sea on the little bridge and on Peg's brass balance; the two numbers disagree by 1.1 per cent. Peg: "Get rid of it before they come." Danny lifts the drain grate for her, and the sea goes down the drain.

## 12. The ending

**Epilogue, December.** Four short scenes, no dialogue longer than two lines.
1. **The front page.** "THE TEN-SECOND CURE: A GRAVEL FIRM'S RECORDS". The drift table is public. Turned people are cured on licensed rigs for the price of an appointment. Obverse's shares have halved; Dr Osei, on the news, says only, "That price fed forty thousand people." The Register's cure clinics now offer crossing next door, at Mucking, as one choice among three. Over the headline: Rosie on an isolation ward at the Royal Free, HAND: T on her badge, gowned and gloved for her patients' sake, walking between fever beds that nobody else can walk between without a mask. She is training with the licensed crossing surgeons, and she lifts a tumour out of a feverish child in three seconds, inside the limit she once called soft. The Hand Company recruiter's card is still in her locker.
2. **Visiting day.** Tess in prison, having claimed every number as hers; on her cell wall, a letter from Kieran with a graph drawn on it. Across London, Danny on the Register's secure ward, where no door is locked except the pantry's; on his table, an appointment card for a licensed cure in March. He hasn't decided. Ray, charged with misconduct, visits them both, and signs both visitors' books in plain writing.
3. **Breakfast at the bridge.** Peg, on bail for the depot raid and for "release of other-hand matter", serves Kieran his fry-up, then weighs him after it ("Everything in you counts") and writes it on the roll. He writes each lorry's weight on the roll too, and adds in pencil, "checked against Maggie's". Beside Frank's photo hangs Terry's last ticket, 71.4, with "Went next door" in Peg's hand. Across the river Siobhan Ahern, 13, steps on a bathroom scale while Declan chalks the number and the family cheers. Under the scale lies December's front page, unread. Maggie, in the doorway, does not cheer.
4. **The hook.** On the office radio, next-door events: since November the tremors have spelled a second molecule. This time it is a sugar of *our* hand. A scientist: "It's ours. We don't know why." Kieran writes it on the roll. Peg looks at Terry's ticket. **Last shot:** the Grays outfall at Nobody low water, the estuary running out to sea.

**What it settles.**
- Why the Marlows turned: time in the black, lengthened by their own concrete and their own rules.
- Who fed them: Ray, on dead people's food.
- Why Liam died: the family's scale, the fry-up, the margin cut to a tenth, the letter in the drawer.
- What Frank weighed, why the legend said otherwise, and what killed him: a turn nobody understood, a letter in the drawer, and a tenth to spare.
- Where a turned person can go: back into the dice, next door, or on as they are, and the whole country now knows the odds of the first.
- The war, ended between the two women who compared numbers.
- The family's normal has changed: it still feeds its own, and now it writes things down and checks them against someone else's. The world's normal has changed too: the drift is public and a door is known.

**What it costs.** Liam, Con and (before the season) Aoife. Frank's legend. Tess's and Danny's freedom. Danny's fingers, and Danny turned for good unless he takes the dice. Shaun's leg, and Shaun turned until March. Terry, gone. Ray's career. Peg's pride. The firm's name. And the Aherns, unchanged under Declan, still cheering a child's hunger with the answer under the scale.

**Residue.** Something from next door went down a Grays drain, and something of ours went into their sea on Rosie's jumper. Somewhere one world over, an old man is either eating crab or starving among people for whom he is the mirror life. And every viewer has a bathroom scale.

**The hook for a second season.** The neighbours have answered, in the only honest channel there is, with a molecule of our hand. It can be read three ways, and the season has shown all three rules without saying so: they can feed us too; they have found our life in their sea (the lost who drowned in the Deep before spares, and one wet jumper); or they have met someone who arrived the wrong way round. Season two begins with the Register's first licensed volunteer at the Mucking fence, and a gravel firm that now keeps paper.

## 13. Rules the physics must never break

1. **Nothing crosses between leaves but gravity.** No light, sound, radio or smell from next door, except what a crosser physically carries back inside a field.
2. **Next door is never shown.** Not through Rosie's eyes, not in flashback, not in a dream. We see only what comes back: water, weed, a crab, sand, a breath.
3. **Sight in the black is lamp-lit and arm's length.** Inside that circle, every inside is visible; outside it, black. A sent hand sees nothing.
4. **The budget is a fifth of a second per kilo under a hundred, on a Marlow rig.** Everything inside the field counts: cargo, air, breakfast, and water if you fire in it. Nobody outlasts their budget and comes back.
5. **Running out is dispersal.** No body is ever found. What remains is a weight, read by instruments, spreading slowly.
6. **A turn cannot be felt.** Afterwards the turned person sees the world reversed but cannot prove which side flipped. Only chemistry against a sample that never crossed (Peg's meter, the T-meter, the catalogue photo, the airport strip) settles it for anyone else. Never a look in a mirror, and never a taste or a smell.
7. **The drift table in section 5 is law.** Every crossing's odds come from it, and outcomes must be believable at those odds: no long runs of failure at good odds, or of success at bad ones.
8. **Cargo turns with its carrier.** Nobody can turn a thing without turning with it.
9. **The grip turns one of two, a coin toss which, and the one who turns leaves the holding hand in the black.** Only flesh holding flesh. Nobody can guide anyone through the black by the hand.
10. **A turned body gets nothing from ordinary food or drugs and catches nothing ordinary.** It lives on water, spirits, fat and mirror food, and starves in about six months without mirror food. Ordinary drugs don't fit it.
11. **A turned person's own germs are mirror life.** One person's are a slow, rare risk to wounds, newborns and the very ill; a whole mirror food web is a different order. This shows only as law, borders, gloves, gowns and risk. It is never shown as a growth, a dish, a flask or a mould.
12. **No wall, lock, seal, knot or skin holds a crosser with a rig.** Take the rig. Knots slip in the black.
13. **Weigh-walls flood after three seconds within four metres.** Short visits slip under.
14. **Gravity from next door falls with the cube of distance.** The Hill's change makes Grays and Gravesend scales read 1.1 per cent light and London's not at all. Weighbridges, platform scales and bathroom scales lie; balances, steelyards, weigh-walls and rigs do not. A platform scale feels a body standing in the black over it.
15. **A field fired in water fills with water and cannot lift you.** Only the spare can fire there.
16. **The spare fires once, within a minute, for half the seconds, only after you have stepped out onto a leaf.** Its field is tight and blind. It has existed since 2016.
17. **Facing home keeps you on your own world. It does nothing about turning.**
18. **Stepping out next door is a crossing, and so a toss, at the odds for however long it takes.** A turned person who slides arrives where the food fits; one who turns arrives straight, starving there and a risk to them. Whoever goes goes stripped, washed and on empty lungs.
19. **The neighbours' life is the other hand.** Turned people can eat it. Straight people cannot.
20. **No new rules.** If a scene needs one, change the scene.

## 14. Notes for episode writers

**Tone.** Plain, vivid, concrete, funny until it isn't. Essex kitchen warmth set against the black. The world is ordinary and works: inspectors inspect, nurses nurse, the Register feeds the turned and a fireman goes home with his ration box, the police investigate crimes. Only the crossing families, across one river, never ask, and they are the only people who cross for long.

**Words that never reach the screen.** No character or caption may use, as ideas: conjecture, criticism (as a principle), error correction, refute, falsify, fallible, "good explanation", "hard to vary", growth of knowledge, static or dynamic society, tradition of criticism, authority (as a lecture topic), optimism (as doctrine), "nothing is settled", "question everything", "the problem of", infinity. Nobody makes a speech about asking questions. The words in this plan's scaffolding ("the culture", "the doom", "the code" as an abstraction) stay in this plan. The questions on screen are ordinary ones: "How much?" "Why's it a kilo out?" "What did my dad weigh?"

**Names that never reach the screen.** The Convention, the seed-bank affair, the Looking Act, the court case and the Privacy Order are background for writers. On screen each is only an object or a single anecdote: "Then you'd be eating it"; the ring in the stomach; "Police don't get the list, love."

**Never echo The Catch.** No mint, and no taste or smell test that settles anything: identity and next door are settled by meters, the catalogue, the strip, the dog, a balance. No backwards signs or print from a turned character's point of view; the camera stays on the straight side of every mirror. No dishes of germs, no flask, no mould, no growth shown. No hands meeting on glass. No episode ends on a meal that can't be shared. No instrument showing an unseen visitor live in an empty room; the 02:04 ticket is found in the morning, and the ambush in episode 6 is a stakeout. No kind figure from next door; no ship; no child's drawing; no toy with a letter; no lift, lift engineer or the word "lift" for a job (it's a "dip"). No oxygen cylinder or breathing gear, charge bar, gravity needle or ledge.

**Never echo Caraway.** No rite at a set age, no scripture, no heretics, no twins, no moving the machine, no dead fed to a machine. **The Marlows never send a message in mass;** only the neighbours do. Kieran never asks the question the adults need; he writes down what they remember.

**Never end the way the rejected pitches did.** Nobody on our side converses with next door by weight in a rhythm (no dances, no steps, no knocks). Nobody breathes out a neighbour's air for someone else, and no smell decides anything. Terry's going is a job on the count, not a farewell at the water; it is not the season's climax, and nobody watches a trace to see whether he lives.

**Crossing grammar.** Sound drops to heartbeat and breath. The lamp's circle is the whole frame. The wrist unit counts tenths. Close-up on fingertips when the tingle starts. Return with a gasp and the smell of the room. Every return gets "Straight?" "Straight." through episode 9, where Shaun says it and Peg's meter reads LO in front of everyone. In episode 10, when Ray comes back, nobody asks: Kieran holds out the meter instead.

Ray and Maggie get scenes of their own that have nothing to do with the Marlows. Liam is on screen in every episode from 1 to 5, wanting to stop being "the big lad", before he is weighed. ## 15. What changed from the pitch, and why

Written for the owner. Each entry says what changed from pitch A and why. Entries marked **(v2)** are new or rewritten in version 2; the revision log `51-revision-log-r1.md` gives the critique finding behind each one.

**The big changes, from making every law true**

1. **Next door is now food for the turned.** In the pitch, Rosie followed the Deep, found a sea and nothing else, and the book's ending was tested and failed. Now the neighbours' life really is the other hand. **(v2)** It is proved by instruments, not by taste: a smear of weed from the sea reads LO on Peg's meter and normal on the ward's T-meter, the dog growls at the jar as it growls at Terry, and Terry eats a crab from it and holds out his plate for more. *Why:* you asked for every law, including this one. Followed honestly, it sharpens the season's point: since spares came in ten years ago, the one place a turned Marlow could eat lay one world over, and a rule made in the years before spares made it unthinkable.
2. **Exile is a real cure, and someone takes it.** **(v2)** In episode 10 Peg, Maggie and Rosie lay three memories side by side (the framed Ministry receipt, the Nobody Tide tables, the sea under Grays) and find that the old field is where the neighbours' ground meets ours at Nobody low water. Rosie scouts it and keeps the one breath she brings back to herself. Terry, who decided the night he ate the crab, goes on the count like any job, through Peg's old gate, alone, because nobody can hold his hand in the black. *Why:* you asked for "exile is a cure" to be true. It isn't free (the step is itself a coin toss, at the odds for however long it takes), the heroine doesn't go, and the finale isn't about the going.
3. **Terry is not cured in the depot raid, and is now a principal.** In the pitch he came back straight and ate a bacon sandwich. *Why:* on a two-second leg a cure would be a fluke; and he is the right person to go next door: the family's pariah, who went again eleven times, cooks food he can't taste, and has never heard Peg say the word "turned". His phone friendship with Con gives the war's first death a face on the Marlow side.
4. **Mirror life is accounted for and does jobs.** **(v2)** One turned person's germs are a slow, rare risk; a whole mirror food web is a different order. So the Register's rules are modest (sealed loos, no food for sale), Terry has always cooked in gloves, HAND: T passports are refused in many countries (so Rosie can't run for a cheaper cure), Rosie works gowned for her patients' sake, and whoever steps next door goes stripped, washed and on empty lungs. Peg's jar of sea down the drain is the season's residue. It is never shown growing.
5. **A second message closes the season.** The neighbours spell a sugar of our hand. *Why:* the one honest channel, used again; every reading of it grows from rules already on screen.

**Changes to the show's own guesses**

6. **(v2) The drift table is much lower at short times:** none ever recorded at two seconds, one in five thousand at three. *Why:* at higher odds, surgeons crossing every day would turn and turn back often, the whole medical world would know that repeating short hops cures, and the second reversal would be old news.
7. **The cargo rule.** Whatever rides in one field turns or slides with its carrier. *Why:* the painting turned with Rosie; it also blocks cheap mirror food, makes the crab fit Terry, and, in version 2, turns Shaun's stolen case back into ordinary porridge.
8. **(v2) The spare is its own guess, and now has limits.** Standard since 2016; fires once, only after you've stepped out onto a world, for half the seconds; a tight, blind field that works in water. *Why:* without it nobody could come back from next door; with these limits it can't save Liam in the black, and it explains why the Deep kept everyone before 2016.
9. **(v2) Next door is mapped a little further, and the Hill loses its top rather than moving.** *Why:* exile needs a place to land; if the whole mountain had moved, the door found from 1995 would have moved with it.
10. **(v2) A sixth guess, the grip.** Two crossers holding each other turn one relative to the other, and the one who turns leaves the holding hand in the black. *Why:* without a price, any two turned people could cure one of themselves for nothing by holding hands. With it, Danny's revenge costs him four fingers, Obverse's sign makes sense, and Terry must go alone.

**Fixes the three pitch reviews asked for**

11. **(v2) The doom's engine is now the concrete, the weigh-wall and "never go back twice".** Marlow Mix sells the family's night knowledge by day; a weigh-wall floods only after three seconds, so the one-trip rule both trips the wall and turns people; Tess's secret is that she planned Mayfair as one long visit when two short ones would have done. "Make weight" stays, as what hides the starvation. *Why:* the originality review saw Caraway's admired fast in the pitch's chain of cause; version 1 still had Rosie's turn caused by weight loss.
12. **Rosie's tells are hidden in plain sight.** Mirror writing is her old party trick; she has always been bad with jars; she prices a cure "for Uncle Terry"; the lard is "for cold-water swimming". **(v2)** Ray no longer says "so did whoever carried it" in episode 1; he says it to Tess in episode 3, so the audience suspects along with her for two acts. *Why:* the first reversal must be one for the audience.
13. **Ray is revealed as turned in episode 2**, eating lard in the Obverse waiting room.
14. **Peg's glucose meter settles who has turned.** *Why:* sugar tastes sweet in either hand, and a meter keeps the show away from any taste test.
15. **"The Chemist" is now "the Laundry".** *Why:* the name pointed at Breaking Bad.
16. **A job is a "dip", not a "lift"; Hatton Garden is now Marrable Street.** *Why:* "lift" echoed The Catch; Hatton Garden was a real robbery.
17. **Kieran has a want and a hobby.** **(v2)** His nightly test weight debuts in episode 2, and the SETI poster is why he weighs instead of looks. From episode 5 his own weight falls in a second column the audience sees and the family doesn't. He never asks the adults' question for them; he writes down what they remember.
18. **Liam's answer moves to the end of episode 9.** **(v2)** Kieran weighs a jar of the sea on the little bridge and on Peg's brass balance, and they disagree. Liam's sum now includes the cash he carried.
