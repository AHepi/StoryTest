# SECONDS, round 6: an editorial review of the full season

*Reviewed: `glm-seconds/round-6/full-season.md`, all ten episodes and the epilogue, read start to finish against the brief (`00-brief-2.md`), the owner's second feedback (`05-owner-feedback-2.md`), the notes on The Catch (`the-catch-notes.md`) and the master plan, version 3 (`70-plan-v3.md`). Every claim below points at an episode and a scene and quotes the page. I read a script; nobody has watched a film of it, so anything I say about pace is a judgement from the page.*

A few words I cannot avoid, explained once: a **plant** is a detail put in early so that a later surprise is fair; a **reversal** is a moment that changes how you read everything that came before it; a **cold open** is the scene before the titles; a **beat** is one small moment inside a scene.

---

## 1. The verdict

SECONDS is a ten-episode crime drama about an Essex family of thieves, the Marlows, who step through walls for a living and whose one boast is that they always come back the right way round. Their best crosser, Rosie, comes back mirror-turned in the first minute of the season and hides it; her family cheers every kilo she loses, because thin means more seconds in the dark. The season follows that lie outward through a war with the family across the river, a scale that quietly starts lying, a detective who has been feeding them in secret, and a sea next door that nobody has ever looked at because the one rule that kept the family alive, "face home", also kept the starving from their food.

It achieves what the owner asked for. The message, that a culture which never questions itself dooms its own people, is never said. It is done to us, in applause. Every invented law of the source is on screen and doing plot work, and I found no place where one is broken. The three big reversals (Rosie's starvation in 3; time as both cure and curse in 6 and 7; the lying scale and the founder's turned memory in 9 and 10) are planted fairly, and each one makes the audience reread the season. Nothing from The Catch is echoed. No banned word reaches the screen. The wider world is ordinary and kind: an inspector who hangs a certificate with his own little hammer, a nurse who hands out a meter, a fireman who talks about leeks.

Is it finished? As a draft, yes: it is complete, consistent, and it lands. It is not yet locked. I found four small contradictions between scenes that a careful viewer would catch (all one-line fixes) and a handful of judgement calls that only an audience can settle. None of them touches the spine. This is a polish pass away from done.

## 2. How it keeps the owner's rules

### The message is shown, never stated

The doom lives in habits the family loves. The applause is the murder weapon, and the season never says so. Episode 1, scene 3: Peg serves the fry-up with "And everything in you counts, breakfast too," then Rosie eats fast and slides her plate to Liam, and "The table laughs." Episode 3's cold open: "PEG: Forty-one eight. / The room goes up." Then, at the meter, episode 3, scene 17: "PEG: You've sat at this table every morning. / ROSIE: Every morning. / PEG: And we clapped. / Nobody has anything to say to that." That is the whole thesis, in three lines, and it is a fact, not an idea.

The other habits are objects: the drawer that won't shut (1.9, 4.11, 9.11), the stove that eats every ticket (1.3, 3.10, 7.12), the chalk board that gets wiped (4.2, 9.1, 10.5), and the ritual "Straight?" / "Straight." that the season sets up in its first minute and kills in episode 9, scene 7: "PEG: You said straight. / SHAUN: I knew before we were out the marsh. (beat) I said it anyhow." When Ray comes back in episode 10, scene 12, "Nobody asks him anything. ... Kieran holds out Peg's meter."

The closest any line comes to the moral is ordinary talk about specific facts: Terry's "Nobody ever asked why" over Lee's weights (7.4), Maggie's "Nobody counted my girl" (7.7), Tess's "We never fed our own" in front of a full fridge (8.7). None of them is a speech. Nobody says "question", "authority", "tradition" or any of their cousins as an idea. I searched the whole file for the owner's list of forbidden words and the background-law names (the Convention, the Looking Act, the Privacy Order): none appears in screen text. (The word "knowledge" appears only in the writers' own "Plants and payoffs" notes, headed "Knowledge at the end", which are not screen text.)

The brief also warned against teaching the opposite by accident, that asking is dangerous or the heroine is always right. The season avoids both. Tess is wrong often and pays: she planned Mayfair as one trip (6.9, "Twice. Four and four"), she hands Rosie to the ward on her own sums (8.8), and Danny's "And Liam? Did your sums do Liam too?" gets "I don't know. ... I don't know, Danny." (7.12). Rosie breaks "face home" and is rewarded with a crab.

### Not The Catch

I searched for every device on the owner's list. No mint, no taste or smell settles anything ("Nobody says what it tasted of," 9.8; Danny's "What's it taste—" dies unfinished, 9.9). No backwards print from a turned character's eyes: when Rosie says the roll is printed backwards, "The camera sees the ticket as Mick folds it into his cab: ordinary purple print, the right way round" (2.10). No dishes, flasks or growths; the only "flask" is tea on a tailgate. No hands on glass, no ship, no kind figure, no child's drawing, no cylinder, no ledge, no gravity needle showing a live visitor: every reading of an unseen body is found on the roll afterwards (2.3, 4.13, 6.2). The word "lift" never names a job. The moral question is the season's own, whether you wrote it down and checked it, not The Catch's "may you decide for someone else".

### Not a dystopia

The world works and is largely decent. Pinnock hangs the certificate "with a little hammer from his own pocket" (1.9). The Register is a kitchen with an open door. Osei keeps her secret for the reason "That price pays for the Register's kitchens. Forty thousand people eat off it, every day." (6.8). Ray grows tomatoes he cannot eat because "They need the watering." (8.6). Den the fireman goes home with a ration box and a postcard of leeks. The only people who never check are the two crossing families across one river.

## 3. What works best

**The applause.** The season's cleverest move is that its murder weapon is love. "PEG: That's making weight. Four hundred grams since Monday." (1.13) is a line the audience cheers with, and by episode 3 cannot hear again without flinching. Rosie passing it on completes the circle: "ROSIE: They'll clap you. / LIAM: They never clap me. / ROSIE: They will." (4.4)

**"So someone's counting."** Tess counts aloud in the van though nobody in the black can hear her. Episode 3, scene 14: "LIAM: Then why d'you do it? / TESS: So someone's counting." That line becomes Peg's, in Tess's voice, at Silvertown (7.10, "PEG: I know. ... So someone's counting."), then Ray's alone in the black (10.12, "he counts anyway, the way he has counted everything for twenty-four years, in a book nobody read"). The whole season is people counting for each other, too late.

**The meter in the cutlery drawer.** Planted as a diabetic's morning habit (1.12), it becomes the only honest thing in the house. "TESS: It's not low. It can't read her." (3.16) is the first reversal in six words. Then, in 9.9, two meters on one bead of blood, and Rosie's "Two meters. Same blood. Both of them right." That is the source's "whoever holds the reference sample decides who counts as normal", done as a kitchen object.

**Peg at the stove.** Episode 7, scene 12: she feeds the ferry timetable and the clinic's book to the fire, picks up the box of rolls to follow them, and stops at the certificate on the wall: "If the rolls burn, there's no licence. If there's no licence, there's no weighbridge." She puts the box on the dresser. "PEG: Not them." A woman saved by her own paperwork without knowing it. It is the hinge of the ending and it is played in silence.

**"I went shopping."** Rosie, drenched, with a crab, to Kieran (9.6): "I didn't go to be turned back, Kiz. I went shopping." The season's biggest rule-break, made small and funny, and the discovery that follows is settled by an instrument and a man's blood, not by a taste: "TERRY: Five point six. (beat) That's a dinner, that." (9.9)

**Frank.** The cold open of episode 10 shows Frank "fifteen stone if he's an ounce" as Peg remembers him, and then scene 3 replays the kitchen "as it was": "the man at the table is thin ... His hands are steady around a jam jar, turning the lid the wrong way." The cut to "Rosie in the loft hatch, sitting on the top rung, wearing exactly that look" is the season's best single edit. Peg's pencil, "FRANK. 10 ST 2. TURNED." (10.5), pays "There's your number, love." from 9.12 with "There's your number, Frank."

**Terry's going.** The die: "Six, I stay hungry. Anything else, I get my tea." (10.8) He holds up his yellow gloves to the handshake: "I cook with these." At the gate, no goodbye, nobody reaches for anything, and he counts himself: "TERRY: One. ... TERRY: Two. / And steps off the world. / There was a man, and then there was the field." (10.12) Intercut with Ray's mirror signature and "Give us a fortnight", it is grief and a joke in the same minute.

**The last two images.** Siobhan on a bathroom scale, "Under the scale, folded, lies December's front page, unread. Maggie stands in the doorway ... and does not cheer." (epilogue, scene 18). The doom, still running, one river over. And the hook, a message "of our own hand, and fainter", with Kieran writing one word on the roll.

Smaller things worth protecting: the wiped column showing "an older name ... TERRY" under the chalk dust (4.2); Danny's "Best job we ever did. (beat) And it's four fingers cheaper than the last one." (7.12); the pencil behind Tess's ear that "has never been sharpened" and Maggie's "Mine's sharp." (7.9); Kieran's "FOOD TURNS TOO" written on the certified roll (9.7); Peg's "Well. You're fed now, anyhow." to a newly turned Shaun (9.7), which is the cruellest kind line in the season.

## 4. The problems, most serious first

None of these is large. I have marked each SUBSTANTIVE (a careful viewer would notice it and it weakens the season) or QUIBBLE (could go either way, or only an audience can settle it).

### SUBSTANTIVE

**S1. Terry gives away the vote before there is a vote.** Episode 10, scene 8 (Sunday, 2 a.m.): "TERRY: Six goes to the end of my count, at my age, with the rigs gone to the police Sunday night. Or one go, and I land the right way round." At that moment nobody has decided anything about the police. The choice between burning the rolls and giving them is not put until Sunday afternoon (scene 13, "DANNY: Burn them tonight. We lose the bridge. We stay free.") and is not settled until the vote in scene 14. The plan's own note for this episode says "the final choice is real". Terry's line says it is already made, and names the night. *Why it matters:* it lets the air out of the season's last decision fourteen scenes early, and it makes Danny's "Burn" look like a line nobody could mean. *Fix:* keep Terry's arithmetic and change what threatens it: "Six goes to the end of my count, at my age, and a warrant Monday. Or one go, and I land the right way round."

**S2. Frank's roll has his name and Peg's pencil on it, though the rolls have neither.** Episode 1, scene 13 establishes what a roll is: Liam's ticket is burned, and "Inside the printer the audit roll winds on, keeping its own copy." The roll is the printer's internal copy; the torn-off ticket is what people hold and pencil on. Episode 7, scene 4 shows the rolls exactly that way: "TESS: Tuesday, March the ninth, '09. Sixty-one kilos. / TERRY: Fenchurch Street. Maureen." The roll gives a date and a weight; Terry has to supply the name. Episode 9, scene 12, the same: "Her finger finds the ticket. 4:30 a.m., the twenty-ninth of April." Then episode 10, scene 2: "September: FRANK 78.0. Beside it, in pencil, in Peg's own hand from twenty-four years ago: 12 st 4." A name printed, and Peg's pencil beside it, on the roll. Peg could only have pencilled on a ticket, and Marlow tickets go in the stove. *Why it matters:* the paper is the season's proof; a viewer who has watched two roll scenes closely will see the third change the rules of the object. *Fix (and it is a better beat):* in the 02 box, under the roll, an envelope of Frank's actual tickets, torn off and kept, with Peg's pencil on them, the one lot of paper she could not burn. Terry: "She kept his. Every one. Never looked." The roll's own copy beside them then confirms the dates.

**S3. Aoife's bank was "six seconds ... all her life", but Danny poured its floor in 2024.** Episode 2, scene 6: "And Mynors had us back last autumn, for the floor under the one we did in '24. Two floors now." So there was a Marlow bead floor from 2024, and a second one under it from autumn 2025. Episode 7, scene 9: "MAGGIE: ... That vault was her bread and butter. She'd walked it since she was fifteen — six seconds, in and out, all her life, tide or no tide." Tess repeats it in 7.11 and 7.12 ("It was six seconds, Danny, all her life"). If the 2024 floor was a metre of Marlow Mix, her six seconds should already have grown in 2024; if it did not, the 2025 floor is doing work the 2024 one did not. *Why it matters:* "Why nine?" is the question the second reversal turns on; the answer should not wobble. *Fix:* one change in Maggie's line: "Four seconds, when she started. Six since the Marlows did the floor in twenty-four, and she never asked why that was. Then nine." That makes Danny's concrete lengthen her job twice, which is truer to the season anyway.

**S4. Where Aoife ran out is told two ways.** Episode 4, scene 9, Con: "A month, the wall read her. Less every day. We stood our minute on the pavement, with the buses going by." Episode 7, scene 7, Maggie: "She went in off our pontoon on her own, and the wall read her a month." The Ahern pontoon is a tug quay in an alley; the wall that prints in Maggie's kitchen is the lock-up wall on the river (5.16, "It printed all night ... He's on my wall"), and the episode 5 notes intend Liam to die on "the same wall". A pavement with buses is somewhere else. *Why it matters:* the resonance of Liam dying where Aoife died is one of the war's quiet symmetries, and Con's line places her death in a street instead. *Fix:* Con's line becomes "We stood our minute on our own quay, with the tide going out under us." (Keep "with the buses going by" for nothing; it is only colour.)

### QUIBBLE

**Q1. Rosie's turn may be too easy to see in episode 1.** The plan wants ordinary viewers level with the family until 3 and only sharp ones ahead. Episode 1 gives ten separate tells: the crisp packet, the plate given away, the dog, "Never turned" over the surgeon, the jar, the mirror card beside the old one, watching the tissue burn, first to wrap the canvas, Terry's pot dropping, and Terry then looking "up at the ceiling" (1.14), which points straight at Rosie's room. Episode 2 adds the dark-bathroom smile, seven glances and the roll joke; then Ray hands Tess the answer: "If the painting turned, so did whoever carried it." (3.5). I think most attentive viewers will arrive at the meter already knowing. The scene still works, because its power is "And we clapped", not surprise, and the plan accepts sharp viewers being ahead. Only an audience can settle how many that is. *If you want more doubt:* cut Terry's look at the ceiling (the pot dropping is enough), and let the dog's first growl at Rosie wait until episode 2.

**Q2. The budget counts cargo from the start of a crossing, and nobody says so.** From the first line, planners subtract cargo from the whole budget: "Eleven two, less the lady" (1.1); "Sixty-two four. One and a half of notes coming out. Sixty-three nine." (5.13). Liam only picks the bags up at 2.6 seconds (5.14) and carries them for the rest. The "two hundredths" sum in 9.12 (6.98 seconds at 65.1 kilos for a 7.0 job) treats the notes as carried for all seven. That is consistent with the plan's own sums, and the show teaches it by use in every crossing, but it is never stated, and a viewer who thinks "he only had the bags for the last four seconds" will feel the season's most exact number go soft. *Fix:* one line in the pit or the van, early, that gives the field its rule: Tess to Kieran (1.7), "It counts you at your heaviest. Pick a thing up at five and you were carrying it from nought." No new number, and the sums all stand.

**Q3. Danny's weight does not move though the season says it does.** Episode 1, scene 13: "Danny steps on. 89.0." Episode 9, scene 1: Danny "working a new hole through his belt — rough at the edges, the way his son's was — and pulls the strap in a notch tighter." That beat is meant to show a fortnight of hunger. Episode 10, scene 8, on Maggie's steelyard, which tells the truth: "DANNY: Eighty-nine dead." A man who has punched a new belt hole from starving weighs what he weighed in March. Numbers are this show's currency, so I would not leave one that argues with a picture. *Fix:* either lose the belt hole (the bandaged hand already carries his cost) or let the steelyard say "Eighty-seven two" and leave him at "nothing at two" all the same.

**Q4. Tess's call to the Register is short of its stated cause.** Episode 8, scene 7: at the vote Rosie's "hand never moves"; nobody puts her on the raid, and Peg has refused to let her cross since April ("You'll not put a finger in the black till you're right," 5.1). Yet Tess's reason in scene 8 is "You're not going in the black for a box of porridge." The call's real motive, that Rosie is starving and the family will not walk her in, is on the page and is enough; the line about the raid is the part that is not. *Fix:* either let Peg say at the vote "And when she's eaten, she goes in. She's lightest," so that Tess has heard it, or change Tess's line to "You're not starving at that table for a box of porridge."

**Q5. The plate reads Joe and not Danny, an arm's length apart.** Episode 5, scene 2: "A plate reads a man in the black as a pull upward. Minus fifty-nine's a man, standing over your scale." Episode 6, scene 1: Danny "stands in the narrow gap between the safe and the dresser, just off the plate's end, an arm's length from where a man would come," and the plate prints "Just the one on it" (6.2). Joe is at the safe, which stands "at the end of the little bridge's plate", so he is also beside it rather than on it. The rule as taught is "over", and the difference between over and beside is being asked to carry a whole plot point. Most viewers will let it go; the plan chose it on purpose so that no instrument shows a visitor live. *If you want it tighter:* let Danny say it himself in the cold open ("Off the plate. Plate only knows what's over it.") so the audience is told the rule the scene depends on.

**Q6. "Twenty years" is said twice and then quietly waived.** Tess to Liam: "And step out on purpose, it's twenty years." (5.13). Terry: "It's twenty year, stepping out." (9.10). Peg at the police station: "And my girl stepped out on purpose. Twice. Put it with the rest." (10.15). In December, Rosie is on a fever ward training with licensed surgeons, and the only line that explains it is the sister's "Your file lives in our kitchen now, love. You eat where you work." The plan's idea, that the pantry is the prison, is the right one and the picture shows it, but a viewer who remembers "twenty years" will want one more word. *Fix:* give the sister, or a caption on her badge, the shape of the sentence: "Twenty year, they said. Then they asked where she'd eat."

**Q7. Three counting scenes in a row.** Episode 7's loft (scene 4), ferry (scene 7) and graph (scenes 9 and 11) are each necessary and each excellent, but on screen they are perhaps twenty-five minutes of people reading columns aloud, and the loft's family names (Maureen, Gary, Vic, Lee) come round again on the ferry and a third time in the night of writing (10.7). The Silvertown intercut helps a great deal. Only an audience can say whether it sags. *If it does:* keep Lee's curve in the loft, which the ferry needs, and let Gary and Vic wait for the ferry, where the ticks and crosses give them a shape.

**Q8. "Then the birthday boy's turn" in June.** Episode 9, scene 1: "Then the birthday boy's turn, uninvited. Kieran is already on the plate." It is a callback to episode 1, but it is an action line, and Kieran's birthday was in March. If it reaches a shooting script as written it reads as a slip. *Fix:* "Then Kieran, uninvited, the way the birthday boy went last in March."

**Q9. Mick delivers the depot's date a little too neatly.** Episode 8, scene 5A: "MICK: Purfleet's shut to us Wednesday fortnight, Peg. Army's in. Whole shed." A gravel driver knowing the Register depot's Army consignment day is the one lucky meeting in the season that helps the family. It is small and Mick has been useful all season. *If you want it earned:* let Tess bring it from the gatehouse ("A lorry every ten minutes, and a sign up: closed to deliveries Wednesday fortnight, Army consignment") so the family's plan comes off Tess's watching, not a driver's chat.

**Q10. One line says the moral in the plan's own words.** Rosie, 7.12: "And when it's been counted proper, and checked against somebody else's, I'm going first." It is an ordinary condition and it is about her own crossing, so it passes the brief. But "checked against somebody else's" is the plan's exact phrase for the season's question, and it is the only place a character speaks it. The epilogue's "checked against Maggie's" in Kieran's pencil does the same work as an object. *If you want it out of a mouth:* "And when it's been counted proper, on paper, I'm going first."

**Q11. Tess does not vote.** Episode 10, scene 14 lists every voice: Danny ("Burn ... No. Give."), Kieran, Rosie, Shaun, Maggie's books, Joe's hand, Peg.
