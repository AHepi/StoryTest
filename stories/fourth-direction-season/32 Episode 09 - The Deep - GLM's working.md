# Episode 9: The Deep

*The family raids the state's pantry while Rosie breaks the family's oldest rule and finds what it hid.*

---

## Cold open

### Scene 1. The weighbridge office, Grays, Wednesday, ten o'clock at night.

Job night, and the ritual holds the room together the way it always has. The lorries are parked in a row outside, the blinds are down, the yard lights lay their orange bars on the lino. Terry wraps bacon butties in foil in his yellow gloves. Peg, apron over her nightie, chalk in her hand. On the board, in Peg's handwriting, not Tess's: **PURFLEET — DEPOT.** Under it her sums. Kieran has been allowed up, in his pyjamas under his school coat, and sits on the camp bed by the safe with his exercise book on his knees, because no power on earth would have kept him upstairs.

SHAUN (26, on a stick since the leg) steps onto the little bridge in his socks. The printer chatters. Peg tears off the ticket and reads it under the lamp.

PEG: Fifty-one nine.

DANNY: Nine and a bit, less the case.

SHAUN: Eight for the far stack, Nan. *(He taps the stick on the steel.)* My leg weighed five and a half when it went. Eleven tenths. I'm worth more without it.

The table laughs, because you do. Peg chalks: SHAUN 51.9 + CASE 3.0 = 9.0.

Then the birthday boy's turn, uninvited. Kieran is already on the plate. The printer chatters.

PEG: Thirty-eight dead.

KIERAN: Twelve point four. I'm the lightest Marlow there's ever been.

The room goes up. Shaun whistles through his fingers. Terry sings a line of the washing-day song. Even Danny bangs the table once.

PEG: Lightest there's ever been. Fourteen—

She is writing it. Danny has taken the ticket off Peg, and he reads it, and then he looks at the boy on the plate, and something in his face closes like a shop shutter.

DANNY: Forty. At your birthday.

KIERAN: I'm the lightest—

DANNY: You've been making weight.

The room goes quiet in the middle of its cheer.

KIERAN: Everyone makes weight.

DANNY: Liam made weight.

Nobody says anything. Kieran looks round the table for one adult to say it isn't the same thing, and finds none, because it isn't a different thing, and they all know it.

DANNY: *(to Peg, not loud)* Not him. Not after Liam.

Peg's chalk hangs over the board. Then she turns, and with her thumb she wipes the 38.0 away, three strokes, the way she wiped Rosie's column in April.

PEG: Bed.

KIERAN: Nan—

PEG: *Bed.*

He goes, slowly, hugging the exercise book. On the stairs he stops.

KIERAN: The Army cases are at the far end. Auntie Tess said. Mind the beads, Shaun.

SHAUN: Aye. Night, shrimp.

Peg lifts the bootlace over her head and kneels at the safe — the printer prints her knees, and nobody tears it off — and the lock gives its clack. She lifts the three rigs out, laying them on top of *The Assayer's Wife*, and hangs them on the steel rack by the back door. Terry takes his own belt. Shaun takes Danny's and knots the strap short for a thinner man, and Danny, who cannot do a knot one-handed, watches him do it.

One rig stays on the rack: the oldest, its shoulder strap cracked along its length.

TERRY: That strap wants seeing to.

PEG: Everything wants seeing to.

They load the van. At the door Peg stops them, one hand each, the way you'd hold two dogs you loved.

PEG: Face home, the both of you.

TERRY and SHAUN: Face home.

The van rolls over the big bridge and the printer chatters it out of the yard. On the landing, at the dark window, Kieran watches its lights go down the lane.

### Scene 2. The last train out of Fenchurch Street, eleven thirty-five.

A warm June night, the carriage half asleep. ROSIE (22) sits awake in her coat with the estuary sliding past, refineries and cranes hanging their light on black water. In her fist, in her coat pocket, the T-meter from the ward.

The train slows. A recorded voice says *Grays*. She is the only one who gets off. The platform, the fog off the river, the one lit way out. She goes through it at a crosser's walk, counting nothing, knowing the way.

## Act one: Two crossings, one clock

### Scene 3. The marsh behind the Register depot, Purfleet, five past midnight.

Reeds, a rutty track, the palisade fence, and beyond it the long blind back of the depot shed, corrugated steel on a brick slab. Far off at the front, the gatehouse light, and a floodlight on a pole that swings its glare across the yard every six minutes, because Peg counted it, off Tess's numbers, two days ago.

The van sits in the reeds with its lights off. Danny stacks crates at the tailgate, one-handed, his bandaged hand held against his chest. Peg kneels in the load bay with her lips already going. Terry, belted, stands at the shed wall with his palm flat on the corrugated steel.

PEG: Two seconds. Out at two, whether you're holding a case or holding nothing.

TERRY: Two's all I've ever asked for, love.

He flexes his fingers once and steps off the world.

**The black.** His glow opens, an arm's length of grey-gold. The sheeting's inside rolls past him, then the slab, and then the floor of the shed: concrete with the beads hanging in it grey and far between, like currants in a bad cake. A pallet edge. Shrink-wrap, seen through like water. A sealed case, stencilled in green.

**The marsh.** He's back, gasping, the case in both arms.

DANNY: Straight?

TERRY: Straight.

Away off in the yard the depot dog starts barking, plain and level, at nothing anyone can see.

DANNY: Dog again.

PEG: He's been at it all night. Foxes.

Danny takes the case and stacks it. Terry squares his shoulders to the wall and goes again. Peg counts each one, low and even, and the crates fill, one case at a time, two seconds a go.

### Scene 4. The weighbridge office, twenty to one.

Rosie lets herself in at the back. The office is dark but for the printer's small green light. On the camp bed, face turned to the safe, Kieran sleeps like a boy who lost an argument.

She hangs her coat on the stairs. At the rack there is one rig, and she knows whose it is: Auntie Tess's, the strap cracked along its length. She buckles it on, takes the gaffer tape out of the toolbox, and binds the crack tight, the way you'd bind a split spade.

She sets the T-meter on the dresser, square by the safe, where it will stay dry. Then she stands a moment in the dark, talking to the sleeping room, in a whisper, the way you'd talk a job to yourself.

ROSIE: If it's rock, I'm dead where I stand. If it's nothing, I fall for ever. If it's water, this rig's no use — a field in water fills with water. *(She touches the brass disc at her throat.)* Then it's the minute. Stand a minute on the wrong side, and the tab brings me home. Half my seconds. Five point four.

She steps onto the little bridge. The printer chatters. Under the counter, in the dark, the roll winds on, keeping its copy.

She flexes her fingers once, the way every Marlow does. And then she does the thing no Marlow has ever done. She does not look for home.

**The black.** Sound drops to her heartbeat and her breath. The glow, the arm's length of everything. Her ears scream that she is falling, and there is nowhere to fall to, and she knows the voice.

And the pulls come, the way Terry taught a boy at a stove: home, strongest, like someone calling you in for your tea. Off east, hard, like leaning on a lorry — the Hill. Below her, soft and wide, a feather bed you'd sink in — the Deep. And behind it all, faint, like a kettle two doors down, one more: the Far.

All her life she has turned to the caller. She turns her back on it, and lets herself sink into the feather bed.

One. Two.

She steps out.

### Scene 5. The far stack at Purfleet, and the sea one world over, intercut. One clock.

**The black at Purfleet.** Shaun goes through the wall's beads and into the floor, and here the beads are packed grey through grey, frogspawn in jelly, the thickest pour he has ever pulled through. The wrist unit counts tenths. Pallets slide past in the glow. At the far end, stacked three high: cases stencilled ARMY.

**The marsh.** Peg, eyes shut:

PEG: …two… three…

**The water.** Rosie steps out and there is nothing to stand on.

The screen goes black.

Cold closes on her like a hand. Salt. No floor, no up, no down, and her ears telling her she is falling in every direction at once. In her chest: the office air, bacon fat and ink, going stale. Her thumb goes to the wrist unit, and stops, an inch off the button. A field in water fills with water. She takes her thumb away.

She lets a little breath go, the way the pit taught her, and the bubbles tickle past her face — up — and she follows them, kicking in her clothes, in the dark, eight metres by her own count.

**The black at Purfleet.** The tingle starts in Shaun's nails at six, a fizz like rain on a shed roof. He takes the second case off the stack and turns for home, small pulls at nothing, patient through the thickness of it.

**The marsh.**

PEG: …seven… eight.

He lands with a gasp, the case in his arms, rain of the reeds on his face.

DANNY: Straight?

SHAUN: Straight.

PEG: Once more, son. Then we're away.

He squares up and goes again.

**The sea.** Rosie's head breaks surface into air she cannot see. A wave slaps her face. She takes one breath of it — one — and holds it, and treads water, and counts.

Something long and soft slides across her cheek. Weed. She doesn't cry out, because her mouth is shut, and her breath is the only sample of that world anyone has ever held.

Forty. Forty-one. Fifty.

Something small walks into her hood and settles there, against her skull, cold and quick. She lets it stay.

Fifty-nine. Sixty.

Her teeth find the tab, and she bites.

**The bite.** No light. No glow, no gauge, nothing to see at all — only her own body, the cold going out of her clothes, and the pull of home through it all, steady as the tide. She lets herself sink, and counts the count she counted up, the way you drop a floor into a vault.

Eight.

The pull of home stands square on, like a door into it. She steps.

**The black at Purfleet.**

PEG: …seven… eight…

Shaun lands on the marsh with a gasp, the last case in his arms.

**The weighbridge office.** Rosie lands on the little bridge with a gasp, drenched to the skin, weed in her hair, and the smell of the room comes back to her: bacon fat, printer ink, dog.

The printer chatters.

On the camp bed, Kieran sits up like a cork.

For a moment nobody moves: a boy in his pyjamas, a girl dripping on a steel plate in the dark.

She reaches into her hood and pulls out a crab, a green-shelled crab the size of her fist, legs going, and holds it up in the green light, water running off her wrist onto the plate.

ROSIE: It's sea. Under us. All of it.

## Act two: The cases

### Scene 6. The weighbridge office, ten past one.

Rosie, wrapped in the good blankets, sits at the stove end with her teeth chattering, a mug of tea she can't drink going cold in her hands. The crab is in the washing-up bowl on the drainer, walking the rim. Sixpence has come out of his basket and stands in the middle of the floor, growling his low steady note, at her, and at the bowl, and at the puddle spreading off the plate.

ROSIE: You an' all.

Kieran tears the night's tickets off the printer and squares them. Then he does the only thing this family has ever done with a stranger: he lifts the crab out of the bowl, sets it in the middle of the little bridge, and waits. The printer chatters. He tears off the ticket, and writes the number in his exercise book, and under it, in pencil: *CRAB. FROM NEXT DOOR.*

KIERAN: Nobody's ever brought anything back.

ROSIE: I know.

KIERAN: What's it like?

ROSIE: Cold. *(beat)* Dark. Hungry work.

KIERAN: *(looking at the ticket in his hand)* If you'd turned on the way back, you'd have come home straight. You'd be at Nan's fry-up.

ROSIE: Five in six I stay as I am. I know. *(beat)* I didn't go to be turned back, Kiz. I went shopping.

He looks at her. The word lands somewhere behind his eyes and stays there.

KIERAN: The message. *Come round for tea.* That's what the papers—

ROSIE: Aye.

She shivers, and holds the mug anyway for the warmth of it. He goes to the airing cupboard and brings the second blanket, and puts it round her shoulders without a word, the way she used to put things round Liam.

### Scene 7. The weighbridge office, dawn.

The van rolls onto the big bridge and the printer prints it coming home. The family comes in with the cold on them: Peg, Danny, Terry, Shaun on his stick — and stops, all together, at the sight of the salt.

The puddle by the plate has dried to a white crust. Peg crouches and rubs it between finger and thumb, and looks at the powder on her skin.

PEG: There's salt drying on my bridge.

ROSIE: *(from the stove end, in the big jumper, dry now)* You went in the Deep.

PEG: You went in the Deep.

ROSIE: I went in a sea, Nan.

PEG: Same thing.

ROSIE: It weren't. *(quiet, so only the room hears)* Eight metres up. I counted it. Their sea's sitting over this lino.

Danny crouches at the plate and touches the crust, and looks up under the board at the chalk, at the painted rule that has ruled them all their lives.

Then the morning's business, because lorries don't know. Terry carries the cases in and stacks them on the table. Eleven cases, sealed, stencilled, batch-coded. Peg wipes her hands.

PEG: In. One at a time.

Terry first, out of habit. She pricks his finger with her meter, and waits for the beep. **LO.**

TERRY: I could've told you that for nothing.

PEG: You count as crossed. Shaun.

Shaun sets his stick against the table and holds out his hand.

PEG: Straight?

SHAUN: Straight.

She pricks him. The beep. **LO.**

The kitchen goes very still. Shaun looks at the little screen, then out of the window at the yard, and says it plainly, because lying has never been his way and there is no point now.

SHAUN: Clock in the van come up backwards, over the bridge. Wrote it off as tired.

Nobody says anything. Danny closes his eyes.

PEG: *(finally, and it is awful)* Well. You're fed now, anyhow.

DANNY: We went out to feed three.

He says it to the table. Terry, counting the cases with his eyes, the way everyone in this family counts everything now, finishes it.

TERRY: Two week, at three. *(beat)* Four now.

PEG: Then we go again. Fortnight Thursday.

Nobody argues. Shaun, white around the mouth, nods once, like a man taking a wage cut.

Then Sixpence works along the stack, case by case, growling his low note at each one — at the green-stencilled Army cases, at the Register's plain white ones — and stops at the last case of all, the second of Shaun's two, and puts his nose down and his tail loose, and lies flat, incurious, the way he lies at Peg's feet.

Kieran fetches the knife and cuts the case open. Inside: sealed bricks of porridge in sachets, printed, labelled, ordinary as a supermarket. He pinches one open, mixes a little with water in an eggcup, and dabs Peg's meter strip with it. The beep. A number. An ordinary number.

KIERAN: It's ours now. This lot. This case is food for us.

He looks at the meter, and the dog, and his uncle, and something enormous arrives in a twelve-year-old's face. He takes his pencil, leans over the printer, and writes on the audit roll itself, in the margin, in a schoolboy's hand: **FOOD TURNS TOO.**

Peg looks at the case, and at the dog, and at her chalk sums on the board, 51.9 + 3.0 = 9.0, the numbers she trusted.

PEG: Bad luck.

That is all she has. She says it, and goes to put the kettle on.

## Act three: The other hand

### Scene 8. The weighbridge office, twenty past eight in the morning.

The hatch is up; the day runs on; Mick has been and gone with his ticket and his bacon roll. In the scullery bucket by the door, Rosie's sea-soaked jumper and a fistful of weed sit in their own water. Sixpence lies in front of the bucket, growling at it, steady as a fridge.

ROSIE: Sit down, Uncle Terry.

TERRY: It's my kitchen, love.

ROSIE: Sit down.

He sits. She takes the T-meter out of her coat — it has waited all night on the dresser by the safe — and pricks his finger, and squeezes the bead onto the strip, and waits.

The screen: **2.8.**

ROSIE: Two point eight. That's a starving man. *(She puts the meter down in the middle of the table where everyone can see it.)* Nothing else for an hour. Not a tub. Not a spoon of dripping. Not a sweet.

TERRY: I couldn't eat another thing anyhow.

ROSIE: You're the worst liar in this family, and that's a cup with entries.

Terry gets up — nobody stops him — and does the thing he is: he fills the big pot, he salts the water with a teaspoon levelled off with a knife, and he cooks the crab, in his gloves, at his own stove. Then he lays a place. Plate, fork, napkin folded. Bread and butter for the look of it, that he cannot eat. The family stands round the kitchen in the middle of a Tuesday—no, a Thursday morning, between lorries, and watches a turned man eat a crab from another world.

He eats slowly. He is starving, and he eats slowly, because he is a cook.

Nobody says what it tasted of.

Kieran sits at the counter with his exercise book and writes: *9:22. Crab.* Under it he rules a space.

### Scene 9. The weighbridge office, twenty past ten.

Rosie pricks Terry's finger again. The bead comes up. She holds her hand out to her grandmother.

ROSIE: Meter, Nan.

Peg brings hers out of the cutlery drawer, from among the spoons. Two strips, one bead of blood, one each.

The T-meter beeps: **5.6.**

Peg's meter beeps: **LO.**

Rosie lays them side by side on the table, both face up, the way Ray laid two photographs on the counter in March. Five point six. LO. Kieran writes both numbers in the ruled space.

ROSIE: Two meters. Same blood. Both of them right.

Terry looks at his own finger for a long moment.

TERRY: Five point six. *(beat)* That's a dinner, that.

He pushes his empty plate out into the middle of the table, towards the middle of the family, the way a man asks for more.

There is no more. The pot is empty. The bowl is empty. The sea is eight metres up.

DANNY: What's it taste—

He stops. He looks round the table, at his daughter, at his mother, at his uncle's face, and nobody in the room wants the answer, and the question dies where it stands.

ROSIE: Two point eight to five point six, off one crab. *(quiet)* There's a sea of it, Kiz. Under us.

Terry takes his plate back, and wipes it, and puts it away, and stands at the sink with his gloves dripping, looking out at the pit.

### Scene 10. The yard and the office, that night.

The office is lamplit. The stove ticks. Terry and Peg, alone, the way they have been alone in kitchens for sixty years. He is drying the last pot; she is at the dresser with the day's takings.

TERRY: That crab's the first thing in five year that's done me any good, Peg.

PEG: You had one crab.

TERRY: There's a sea of them.

PEG: Terry.

TERRY: I'm going, Peg.

The clock ticks. Peg does not turn round. When she speaks, her voice doesn't rise at all, which is the worst thing in the room.

PEG: Get rid of that jumper before they come.

TERRY: It's twenty year, stepping out.

PEG: *(to the dresser, to the drawer, to thirty years of envelopes)* So's starving.

Out in the yard, Danny works the iron bar under the drain grate and lifts it, one-handed, his bandaged hand braced on the kerb. Kieran kneels with the bucket between his knees and wrings the sea out of Rosie's jumper, twist on twist, and the water runs dark down the hole, and goes on running when the jumper is dry, because the weed holds it.

The grate goes back. The yard is the yard again.

At the counter, later, alone, Kieran opens his exercise book to the page with the 04:17 ticket taped in, and beside it tapes one green frond of the weed, flattened flat as a flower.

## Act four: The step

### Scene 11. The weighbridge office, Friday, nine in the morning.

Tess's car comes over the big bridge and the printer prints her in. Kieran unbolts the door before anyone has decided anything. Tess stands on the lino with two days of sleeping in the car on her, and looks at the salt crust, which nobody has scrubbed, and the cases, and her family.

TESS: The ward rang me last night. I'm down as next of kin. *(to Rosie)* You walked out.

ROSIE: Door had no lock.

Peg opens her mouth. Tess doesn't wait for it.

TESS: I'm not here to plan. I'm here about a number.

KIERAN: *(before anyone else can speak)* Auntie. Before anything.

He takes her by the sleeve — the first time in his life he has led an adult anywhere — to the graph on the wall. The flat line. The step. The circled wobble. And beside it, in his book, open on the counter: the second column, the one headed *me*. 39.2. 38.9. 38.6. 38.3. 38.0.

Tess reads down it twice. Her lips move. For once there is a number and she doesn't say it.

TESS: Since Easter?

KIERAN: Since Easter.

TESS: *(to the room, quietly)* He's been making weight under your roof.

PEG: He eats his tea.

KIERAN: I eat some of it.

Nobody has anything. Danny looks at the boy the way he looked at the ticket last night. Rosie looks away, at the fridge, at the two birthday cards, and keeps looking at them.

Tess turns back to the graph, and is Tess again.

TESS: The step. April the fourth. *(to Kieran, testing him, the way she tests sums)* How do you know it's not the weight?

KIERAN: Nan says it's chipped. Dropped it that morning. *(beat)* Chip's the size of a fingernail.

TESS: Right.

She goes to the shelf and takes down Peg's five-kilo bag of flour, and sets the brass balance on the counter, and lays the kilo and the two twos in one pan, and spoons nothing, because the bag is a bag of five kilos by law. She sets the bag in the other pan.

The pointer settles dead centre. Five kilos. Exactly.

She lifts the bag onto the little bridge. The printer chatters. The ticket: **4.95.**

The kitchen is silent. The fridge hums.

TESS: It was never the chip.

She stands looking at the plate, and everyone in the room does the same sum at the same speed.

DANNY: Every job since April. *(He looks at his bandaged hand.)* The bridge has had me at two point four since April.

TESS: Two point two.

DANNY: *(quietly)* I was fizzing before I ever touched that lad.

Tess takes the last thing out of her inside pocket: the ticket Kieran lent her on the ferry and gave back. 02:04 · LITTLE BRIDGE · −59.0. She lays it flat on the counter.

TESS: Everything this bridge has printed since April is a lie. Except this. A body in the black prints true. *(beat)* The one straight number in two months belongs to a boy nobody could see.

Then she turns to the dresser, and hauls the drawer. It sticks, as it always sticks, and comes with a tearing noise, envelopes sagging out of it, years of them, brown and buff.

PEG: That's my drawer.

TESS: Aye.

Tess takes the topmost brown window envelope — WEIGHTS AND MEASURES, April — and opens it with one slit of her thumbnail, and reads it aloud, flat, like a ticket.

TESS: "Following a local gravity change of minus 1.1 per cent beneath the estuary, all weighing equipment in the affected area must be re-verified within 28 days." *(beat)* Dated the eleventh of April. It's the twelfth of June.

She digs. Low down, the corner of pale green with the printed hand on it. She reads that too.

TESS: "Since the fourth of June 2019, patient lists held by the Handedness Register are no longer available to the police, save on the order of a court." *(beat)* Twenty nineteen.

DANNY: Seven year.

ROSIE: *(from the stair, not loud)* Nurse told me that my first day in.

Peg sits down slowly on the bottom stair, next to her granddaughter, under the framed cuttings that say CLEAN TRANSLATION, NO ROTATION, and says nothing at all, and nobody in the room can look at her, and nobody can look away either.

## Act five: The hundredths

### Scene 12. The weighbridge office, that afternoon.

The hatch down. The family at the long table: Peg, Danny, Tess, Terry, Rosie, Shaun on his stick, Kieran at the end with his book. The box of audit rolls from the loft sits on the dresser where it has sat since the night of the ferry.

TESS: Danny. His number. Bring me his number.

Danny takes his wallet out. From behind the driving licence he draws the folded thermal printout, soft at its folds, carried two months, never once unfolded. He opens it flat on the table and holds the corners down with both hands, one bandaged, as if it might blow away.

The wall's last line: **65.1 KG.**

Tess has the loft roll unrolled across the table, pinned at its corners with Peg's brass weights. Her finger finds the ticket. 4:30 a.m., the twenty-ninth of April.

TESS: Sixty-two four. Plus one and a half of notes. Sixty-three nine.

DANNY: *(to the printout, to the room)* Why's it a kilo out?

Tess turns the April letter over and takes the pencil Kieran holds out to her. She writes on the back of it, talking the numbers as she goes, because someone's counting.

TESS: Sixty-three nine, chalk. The letter says one point one per cent light. *(She writes.)* Sixty-four six, true.

DANNY: Sixty-five one.

TESS: Half a kilo short still.

The kitchen waits. Nobody has it. And then Peg, at the head of her own table, in her own kitchen, says it the way she says everything, plainly, to the table.

PEG: I made him breakfast.

Nobody breathes.

PEG: After he was weighed. Lucky breakfast. Three rashers. Fried bread. He ate every bite, and I scraped his plate into the bin because there was nothing on it to scrape.

Tess writes the last line of the sum: 64.6 + 0.5 = 65.1. Then the seconds, underneath, small.

TESS: Six point nine eight. Job was seven. *(beat)* The lie on its own, he makes it. The breakfast on its own, he makes it. *(She puts the pencil down.)* Either one, he makes it. Both, he doesn't. By two hundredths.

The clock over the counter ticks. The printer's green light blinks. Rosie is looking at the chalk board, at LIAM 62.4 and the star beside it that nobody has wiped.

ROSIE: *(very quietly)* He was so pleased about the star.

Peg gets up. She takes the duster, and reaches up, and rubs out the 62.4 under the star, and then, slowly, in her sign-writer's hand, writes in its place: **65.1.**

PEG: There's your number, love.

She stands back and looks at it, the first true number the board has ever held, and puts the duster down, and squares her shoulders on thirty years of running this family off that bridge.

PEG: Your dad never had a second to spare in his life. Fifteen stone. It's why we make weight. It's the one thing in this family that's never been wrong.

Tess looks at her mother. Then up at Frank's photo, the big man in the donkey jacket, laughing at whoever held the camera. She looks at him a long time.

Then she takes her coat off the chair and goes out.

### Scene 13. The yard, dusk.

Tess sits in her car with the phone at her ear and the river running grey behind the fence. The office window glows yellow behind her; through it, the family sits on at the long table under the chalk star.

**Intercut: the flat above the bookmaker's, Tilbury.** Ray, in his shirtsleeves, awake at his table among the squared files, the window box behind him. The phone is at his ear. He has been awake since two.

TESS: Ambrose. It's Tess Marlow.

A silence on the line, the length of a held breath.

RAY: Aye.

TESS: My dad. What did he weigh?

The line hums. Out on the river a tug sounds its horn, twice, far off.

RAY: Sixty-four kilos.

Tess closes her eyes. In the office window, small behind glass, the big man goes on laughing at whoever held the camera.

RAY: You'll be wanting the rest of it.

TESS: Aye.

RAY: Tomorrow, then.

He rings off. Tess sits with the dead phone in her hand, and doesn't go in.

**Cut to black.**

**END OF EPISODE 9**

## Plants and payoffs

**Paid off here, from earlier episodes**
- **"Face home" (1, cold open tonight):** Rosie turns her back on the caller, and the rule made in the years before bites comes apart on screen. "The Deep keeps you" (1) breaks with her: it kept her sixty seconds, then the tab brought her home — the Marlows' own pit training, the one family that trains it, is what kept her alive (1, 9).
- **The bite (taught in 5: "Only once you've stood a minute on the wrong side"):** used at last, in their sea, half her seconds, 5.4, blind, no light and no gauge. The rig's uselessness in water is said by Rosie herself before she goes, so the minute is fair.

## Critique by GLM (a separate call that did not write the draft)

**Checks that came back clean:** no banned word or named idea reaches the screen; next door is never shown (the sea stays black, the breath is gasped out alone); no taste settles anything (Danny's question dies half-asked); no episode-10 material arrives early (Frank's weight, the door, Rosie's shore stay hidden); the canonical numbers — 38.0/12.4, 51.9 + 3.0 = 9.0, 2.8/5.6/LO, 5.000 against 4.95, −59.0, −1.1 per cent, 62.4/63.9/64.6/65.1, 6.98 and the two hundredths, 2.4 against 2.2, the bite at 5.4 — are used correctly except where flagged below.

## Findings, most serious first

**1. Scene 7 — Shaun's "Clock in the van come up backwards… Wrote it off as tired."**
*Problem:* a turned character notices reversed print and explains it away.
*Why it matters:* the plan's hard rule (section 14) is that the turned know at once and lie; "printed funny" rationalising is exactly the Catch pattern the rule exists to ban, and it's the reason Frank's "never knew" was changed in version 3. It also contradicts the draft's own line ("lying has never been his way"), and it dilutes the designed beat: the ritual dying under Peg's meter in front of everyone. The notes make it worse by logging "Shaun's mileometer", which isn't even the clock the scene describes.
*Fix:* cut the clock. Let the meter be the only reveal, and give Shaun a plain line that carries the law instead: "I never felt a thing. You never do. That's the trouble with the black."

**2. Scene 3 — "DANNY: Straight? TERRY: Straight."**
*Problem:* Terry has been openly turned since episode 1; the whole family knows, and this episode's own scene 7 meters him LO. The question is meaningless and his answer is false on its face — a two-second hop cannot flip him back, which his eleven hops already taught everyone.
*Why it matters:* it breaks the ritual's established use (asked only of crossers whose state is unknown), confuses the audience about who knows what, and spends the episode's one "Straight"-that-lies eight scenes early, on the wrong man.
*Fix:* nobody asks Terry. Danny takes the case; keep "Straight?" for Shaun's legs, where it still means something and where its failure is the beat.

**3. Scene 4 — Rosie crosses from the plate, and the plate's behaviour is ignored.**
*Problem:* the finished episodes established that a body unpinned over the little bridge prints as a minus (episode 2: she stands beside it, not on it; episode 6: Danny keeps "the steel stays empty" so it prints only Joe). Rosie steps onto the plate to cross, so a minus ticket in the forties should print — and the draft even says "the roll winds on, keeping its copy" — yet no such ticket exists.
*Why it matters:* the roll is about to become the season's engine; whether her crime is on it matters for episode 10, and Kieran sorts the night's tickets in scene 6 and would find it.
*Fix:* keep her on the plate (the return needs the printer to wake Kieran) and own it: Kieran finds the minus ticket in scene 6 — paper proof of her crossing that makes the family believe the sea story, and a second rhyme with Joe's −59.0 for Tess to lay beside it in scene 11.

**4. Scene 5 — "The tingle starts in Shaun's nails at six."**
*Problem:* the tingle comes about two seconds before the true budget ends (9.0 on 11.2 at Mayfair; 4.9 on 6.98 for Liam). Shaun's true budget is 8.9 seconds (52.5 true + 3 kg case, per the canonical table), so the fizz should start at about 6.9 — not 6.0.
*Why it matters:* the plan says the numbers are exact. At 6.0 an attentive audience computes that Shaun has no margin and expects to lose him; the show has never allowed that error except when the lying scale explained it (Danny, episode 6), and Shaun's chalk/true gap runs the other way.
*Fix:* "at seven" or "nearly seven."

**5. Scenes 1 and 3 — Tess's recon is over-credited.**
*Problem:* Kieran: "The Army cases are at the far end. Auntie Tess said" — Tess's surveillance (episode 8) was from a layby: gatehouse, two cameras, a lorry every ten minutes. She could not see inside, could not know about Army cases or a far stack, and has had no scene in which to tell Kieran. Likewise the floodlight "every six minutes, because Peg counted it, off Tess's numbers, two days ago": her numbers contain no floodlight, and Peg wasn't there to count anything off them.
*Why it matters:* it breaks episode 8's continuity and hands the family knowledge the plan keeps from them — and it wastes the raid's own discovery, which the draft already stages beautifully (Shaun finding "cases stencilled ARMY" in the black).
*Fix:* cut Kieran's line to "Mind the beads, Shaun"; reword the floodlight to Peg's own watching: "Peg sat in the reeds on Monday and counted it."

**6. Scenes 9 and 10 — "the first thing in five year that's done me any good" / "There is no more."**
*Problem:* the Laundry's tubs kept Terry alive for five years, and the raid has just put ten cases of Register rations in the house — mirror food that feeds all four turned mouths ("Two week, at three… Four now"). So the crab is not the first thing to do him good, and there *is* more — just not more crab.
*Why it matters:* it blurs the act-three revelation (the meters prove next door is the other hand — a new kind of plenty, not the only food left) and undercuts Terry's real motive from episode 1: not starvation but the want to eat something that is actually food. That distinction is what makes his going a choice.
*Fix:* "Five year of them tubs, Peg. They kept me breathing. That's the first number that's ever moved." Attach "the pot is empty" to the crab only.

**7. Scene 11 — the plan's Maggie cross-check on the −59.0 ticket is dropped.**
*Problem:* the plan has Tess lay the ticket "beside Maggie's 'fifty-nine'" — the steelyard reading from episode 2 that makes the number stand up.
*Why it matters:* without it, "a body in the black prints true" is Tess's assertion; with it, two families' instruments agree — the season's whole method, and the direct seed of episode 10's "checked against Maggie's" and the tide books on the table.
*Fix:* one line as she lays it down: "Their steelyard had this boy at fifty-nine, and a steelyard don't lie. My mother's bridge can't read a bag of flour. It read him true."

**8. Scenes 5 and 7 — the raid trips the depot's weigh-wall and nobody registers the cost.**
*Problem:* Peg counts "…two… three. There's the wall," beads flood the room, and the family knows walls log every body's mass and time (Ray's two bodies at 3:12 started the spinner theory). They walk out of a state pantry leaving logged bodies and a beaded floor, then Peg chalks "Fortnight Thursday" as if the depot were reusable.
*Why it matters:* the plan wants exactly this recklessness — episode 10's investigators arrive on these logs — but the audience should see the family choosing not to look, or the noon visit in episode 10 plays as an ambush rather than a consequence.
*Fix:* one exchange loading the last case. Danny: "Wall'll have read us all." Peg: "Then they can read it on a full stomach." The code answering consequence with comfort, once more.

**9. Scene 3 — the depot dog.**
*Problem:* the dog barks "at nothing anyone can see" during the crossings, and Peg answers "Foxes." As staged it reads as a dog detecting crossings, which is not a law of this world (walls weigh; dogs smell the turned).
*Why it matters:* an unexplained detector at the season's biggest raid either muddies the physics or dangles as a loose threat. The fair version — the dog telling them something and the family not checking, again — is available and is the episode's own theme.
*Fix:* have the dog set up the moment Terry first walks to the wall, before anyone crosses, and keep it up all night; "Foxes" then does the not-checking. Or cut the dog.

**10. Cold open — Peg's "Lightest there's ever been. Fourteen—".**
*Problem:* a dangling number with no referent: not his age (twelve), not his seconds (12.4), not his weight (38.0). It reads as a typo and sits in the middle of the season's most important refusal.
*Why it matters:* the beat depends on the audience seeing Peg's chalk say yes before Danny stops it; noise in her line blurs the yes.
*Fix:* cut to "Lightest there's ever been" with the chalk already moving, and let Danny take the ticket out of her hand.

**11. Scene 11 — "The ward rang me last night."**
*Problem:* Rosie walked out on Wednesday night (episode 8's closing scene, the same night as this raid); a ward with a duty nurse reaches the listed next of kin within hours. On Friday morning, "last night" makes the ward a day late for no reason and leaves Tess's Thursday unaccounted for.
*Why it matters:* the season has kept its clock exact, and Tess is the one person whose movements the audience tracks.
*Fix:* "The ward's been on since Wednesday night. I waited till the vans were home before I knocked."

**12. Scene 4 — Rosie's whispered brief states the one rule never planted.**
*Problem:* "a field in water fills with water" is new lore delivered thirty seconds before it governs her survival. Everything else in the whisper was planted long ago (the minute and the tariff from Tess in 5; "the Deep keeps you" from Terry in 1; the tingle from 1).
*Why it matters:* the show's method is rules on screen before the moment that depends on them; a rule spoken *in* the moment reads as insurance for the writer, and it deflates the stopped thumb, which dramatises the same rule better.
*Fix:* trim the whisper to the choice — rock, nothing, water — and let her thumb stop an inch off the button carry the water.

**13. Scene 8 — "in the middle of a Tuesday—no, a Thursday morning".**
*Problem:* an uncorrected authorial correction left in the prose. It is Thursday (raid dawn Thursday, crab eaten 9:22, metered 10:22).
*Why it matters:* it's on the page; any downstream reader inherits the confusion the writer was in.
*Fix:* "on a Thursday morning, between lorries."

**14. Scenes 4 and 8 — the T-meter's whereabouts contradict themselves.**
*Problem:* scene 4, Rosie "sets the T-meter on the dresser, square by the safe"; scene 8, she "takes the T-meter out of her coat — it has waited all night on the dresser." Both cannot be the blocking.
*Why it matters:* small in itself, but this is the episode's decisive instrument and episode 10 and the epilogue keep using it; its journey should be clean.
*Fix:* she sets it down at 00:40 and picks it up off the dresser at dawn — cut "out of her coat."

**15. Scene 6 — "Nobody's ever brought anything back."**
*Problem:* as written it's false — the Marlows bring things back on every job (the painting, the tray, the notes). Kieran means from next door.
*Why it matters:* a flat contradiction in the mouth of the boy who keeps the records, in the scene that establishes the crab's uniqueness.
*Fix:* "Nobody's ever brought anything back from there."

## Act one: Two crossings, one clock

### Scene 7. The weighbridge office, dawn.

The van rolls onto the big bridge and the printer prints it coming home. The family comes in with the cold on them: Peg, Danny, Terry, Shaun on his stick — and stops, all together, at the sight of the salt.

The puddle by the plate has dried to a white crust. Peg crouches and rubs it between finger and thumb, and looks at the powder on her skin.

PEG: There's salt drying on my bridge.

ROSIE: *(from the stove end, in the big jumper, dry now)* You went in the Deep.

PEG: You went in the Deep.

ROSIE: I went in a sea, Nan.

PEG: Same thing.

ROSIE: It weren't. *(quiet, so only the room hears)* Eight metres up. I counted it. Their sea's sitting over this lino.

Danny crouches at the plate and touches the crust, and looks up under the board at the chalk, at the painted rule that has ruled them all their lives.

Then the morning's business, because lorries don't know. Terry carries the cases in and stacks them on the table. Eleven cases, sealed, stencilled, batch-coded. Peg wipes her hands.

PEG: Everybody that crossed, over here. One at a time.

Terry first, out of habit. She pricks his finger with her meter, and waits for the beep. **LO.**

TERRY: I could've told you that for nothing.

Shaun sets his stick against the table and holds out his hand.

PEG: Straight?

SHAUN: Straight.

She pricks him. The beep. **LO.**

The kitchen goes very still. Shaun looks at the little screen a long moment, then out of the window at the yard.

SHAUN: I never felt a thing. You never do. That's the trouble with the black.

PEG: You said straight.

SHAUN: I knew before we were out the marsh. *(beat)* I said it anyhow.

Nobody says anything. Danny closes his eyes.

PEG: *(finally, and it is awful)* Well. You're fed now, anyhow.

DANNY: We went out to feed three.

He says it to the table. Terry, counting the cases with his eyes, the way everyone in this family counts everything now, finishes it.

TERRY: Two week, at three. *(beat)* Four now.

PEG: Then we go again. Fortnight Thursday.

Nobody argues. Shaun, white around the mouth, nods once, like a man taking a wage cut.

Then Sixpence works along the stack, case by case, growling his low note at each one — at the green-stencilled Army cases, at the Register's plain white ones — and stops at the last case of all, the second of Shaun's two, and puts his nose down and his tail loose, and lies flat, incurious, the way he lies at Peg's feet.

Kieran fetches the knife and cuts the case open. Inside: sealed bricks of porridge in sachets, printed, labelled, ordinary as a supermarket. He pinches one open, mixes a little with water in an eggcup, and dabs Peg's meter strip with it. The beep. A number. An ordinary number.

KIERAN: It's ours now. This lot. This case is food for us.

He looks at the meter, and the dog, and his uncle, and something enormous arrives in a twelve-year-old's face. He takes his pencil, leans over the printer, and writes on the audit roll itself, in the margin, in a schoolboy's hand: **FOOD TURNS TOO.**

Peg looks at the case, and at the dog, and at her chalk sums on the board, 51.9 + 3.0 = 9.0, the numbers she trusted.

PEG: Bad luck.

That is all she has. She says it, and goes to put the kettle on.

## Act three: The other hand

### Scene 8. The weighbridge office, twenty past eight in the morning.

The hatch is up; the day runs on; Mick has been and gone with his ticket and his bacon roll. In the scullery bucket by the door, Rosie's sea-soaked jumper and a fistful of weed sit in their own water. Sixpence lies in front of the bucket, growling at it, steady as a fridge.

ROSIE: Sit down, Uncle Terry.

TERRY: It's my kitchen, love.

ROSIE: Sit down.

He sits. She takes the T-meter off the dresser, where it has waited by the safe since one in the morning, and pricks his finger, and squeezes the bead onto the strip, and waits.

The screen: **2.8.**

ROSIE: Two point eight. That's a starving man. *(She puts the meter down in the middle of the table where everyone can see it.)* Nothing else for an hour. Not a tub. Not a spoon of dripping. Not a sweet.

TERRY: I couldn't eat another thing anyhow.

ROSIE: You're the worst liar in this family, and that's a cup with entries.

Terry gets up — nobody stops him — and does the thing he is: he fills the big pot, he salts the water with a teaspoon levelled off with a knife, and he cooks the crab, in his gloves, at his own stove. Then he lays a place. Plate, fork, napkin folded. Bread and butter for the look of it, that he cannot eat. The family stands round the kitchen on a Thursday morning, between lorries, and watches a turned man eat a crab from another world.

He eats slowly. He is starving, and he eats slowly, because he is a cook.

Nobody says what it tasted of.

Kieran sits at the counter with his exercise book and writes: *9:22. Crab.* Under it he rules a space.

### Scene 9. The weighbridge office, twenty past ten.

Rosie pricks Terry's finger again. The bead comes up. She holds her hand out to her grandmother.

ROSIE: Meter, Nan.

Peg brings hers out of the cutlery drawer, from among the spoons. Two strips, one bead of blood, one each.

The T-meter beeps: **5.6.**

Peg's meter beeps: **LO.**

Rosie lays them side by side on the table, both face up, the way Ray laid two photographs on the counter in March. Five point six. LO. Kieran writes both numbers in the ruled space.

ROSIE: Two meters. Same blood. Both of them right.

Terry looks at his own finger for a long moment.

TERRY: Five point six. *(beat)* That's a dinner, that.

He pushes his empty plate out into the middle of the table, towards the middle of the family, the way a man asks for more.

There is no more crab. The pot is empty. The bowl it lived in is empty. The sea is eight metres up.

DANNY: What's it taste—

He stops. He looks round the table, at his daughter, at his mother, at his uncle's face, and nobody in the room wants the answer, and the question dies where it stands.

ROSIE: Two point eight to five point six, off one crab. *(quiet)* There's a sea of it, Kiz. Under us.

Terry takes his plate back, and wipes it, and puts it away, and stands at the sink with his gloves dripping, looking out at the pit.

### Scene 10. The yard and the office, that night.

The office is lamplit. The stove ticks. Terry and Peg, alone, the way they have been alone in kitchens for sixty years. He is drying the last pot; she is at the dresser with the day's takings.

TERRY: Five year of them tubs, Peg. They kept me breathing. *(beat)* That crab's the first thing that's ever moved my number.

PEG: You had one crab.

TERRY: There's a sea of them.

PEG: Terry.

TERRY: I'm going, Peg.

The clock ticks. Peg does not turn round. When she speaks, her voice doesn't rise at all, which is the worst thing in the room.

PEG: Get rid of that jumper before they come.

TERRY: It's twenty year, stepping out.

PEG: *(to the dresser, to the drawer, to thirty years of envelopes)* So's starving.

Out in the yard, Danny works the iron bar under the drain grate and lifts it, one-handed, his bandaged hand braced on the kerb. Kieran kneels with the bucket between his knees and wrings the sea out of Rosie's jumper, twist on twist, and the water runs dark down the hole, and goes on running when the jumper is dry, because the weed holds it.

The grate goes back. The yard is the yard again.

At the counter, later, alone, Kieran opens his exercise book to the page with the 04:17 ticket taped in, and beside it tapes one green frond of the weed, flattened flat as a flower.

## Act four: The step

### Scene 11. The weighbridge office, Friday, nine in the morning.

Tess's car comes over the big bridge and the printer prints her in. Kieran unbolts the door before anyone has decided anything. Tess stands on the lino with two days of sleeping in the car on her, and looks at the salt crust, which nobody has scrubbed, and the cases, and her family.

TESS: The ward rang me Wednesday night, the minute you walked. I'm down as next of kin. *(beat)* I gave you your raid. Then I came. *(to Rosie)* You walked out.

ROSIE: Door had no lock.

Peg opens her mouth. Tess doesn't wait for it.

TESS: I'm not here to plan.

KIERAN: *(before anyone else can speak)* Auntie. Before anything.

He takes her by the sleeve — the first time in his life he has led an adult anywhere — to the graph on the wall. The flat line. The step. The circled wobble. And beside it, in his book, open on the counter: the second column, the one headed *me*. 39.2. 38.9. 38.6. 38.3. 38.0.

Tess reads down it twice. Her lips move. For once there is a number and she doesn't say it.

TESS: Since Easter?

KIERAN: Since Easter.

TESS: *(to the room, quietly)* He's been making weight under your roof.

PEG: He eats his tea.

KIERAN: I eat some of it.

TERRY: *(at the stove, not turning round)* I found two rounds of butties in the bin last week. Dried them off and put them back in his bag. *(beat)* Thought he was fussy.

Nobody has anything. Danny looks at the boy the way he looked at the ticket on Wednesday night. Rosie looks away, at the fridge, at the two birthday cards, and keeps looking at them.

Tess turns back to the graph, and is Tess again.

TESS: The step. April the fourth. *(to Kieran, testing him, the way she tests sums)* How do you know it's not the weight?

KIERAN: Nan says it's chipped. Dropped it that morning. *(beat)* Chip's the size of a fingernail.

TESS: Right.

She goes to the shelf and takes down Peg's five-kilo bag of flour, and sets the brass balance on the counter, and lays the kilo and the two twos in one pan, and spoons nothing, because the bag is a bag of five kilos by law. She sets the bag in the other pan.

The pointer settles dead centre. Five kilos. Exactly.

She lifts the bag onto the little bridge. The printer chatters. The ticket: **4.95.**

The kitchen is silent. The fridge hums.

TESS: It was never the chip.

She stands looking at the plate, and everyone in the room does the same sum at the same speed.

DANNY: Every job since April. *(He looks at his bandaged hand.)* The bridge has had me at two point four since April.

TESS: Two point two.

DANNY: *(quietly)* I was fizzing before I ever touched that lad.

Tess takes the last things out of her inside pocket and lays them side by side on the counter. The ticket Kieran lent her on the ferry: 02:04 · LITTLE BRIDGE · −59.0. And, next to it, Wednesday's: −46.2.

TESS: Maggie's steelyard had her boy at fifty-nine, and a steelyard don't lie. My mother's bridge can't read a bag of flour. *(beat)* Those two are the only true numbers it's printed since April, and they're both of people nobody could see.

Then she turns to the dresser, and hauls the drawer. It sticks, as it always sticks, and comes with a tearing noise, envelopes sagging out of it, years of them, brown and buff.

PEG: That's my drawer.

TESS: Aye.

Tess takes the topmost brown window envelope — WEIGHTS AND MEASURES, April — and opens it with one slit of her thumbnail, and reads it aloud, flat, like a ticket.

TESS: "Following a local gravity change of minus 1.1 per cent beneath the estuary, all weighing equipment in the affected area must be re-verified within 28 days." *(beat)* Dated the eleventh of April. It's the twelfth of June.

She digs. Low down, the corner of pale green with the printed hand on it. She reads that too.

TESS: "Since the fourth of June 2019, patient lists held by the Handedness Register are no longer available to the police, save on the order of a court." *(beat)* Twenty nineteen.

DANNY: Seven year.

ROSIE: *(from the stair, not loud)* Nurse told me that my first day in.

Peg sits down slowly on the bottom stair, next to her granddaughter, under the framed cuttings that say CLEAN TRANSLATION, NO ROTATION, and says nothing at all, and nobody in the room can look at her, and nobody can look away either.

## Act five: The hundredths

### Scene 12. The weighbridge office, that afternoon.

The hatch down. The family at the long table: Peg, Danny, Tess, Terry, Rosie, Shaun on his stick, Kieran at the end with his book. The box of audit rolls from the loft sits on the dresser where it has sat since the night of the ferry.

TESS: Danny. His number. Bring me his number.

Danny takes his wallet out. From behind the driving licence he draws the folded thermal printout, soft at its folds, carried two months, never once unfolded. He opens it flat on the table and holds the corners down with both hands, one bandaged, as if it might blow away.

The wall's last line: **65.1 KG.**

Tess has the loft roll unrolled across the table, pinned at its corners with Peg's brass weights. Her finger finds the ticket. 4:30 a.m., the twenty-ninth of April.

TESS: Sixty-two four. Plus one and a half of notes. Sixty-three nine.

DANNY: *(to the printout, to the room)* Why's it a kilo out?

Tess turns the April letter over and takes the pencil Kieran holds out to her. She writes on the back of it, talking the numbers as she goes, because someone's counting.

TESS: Sixty-three nine, chalk. The letter says one point one per cent light. *(She writes.)* Sixty-four six, true.

DANNY: Sixty-five one.

TESS: Half a kilo short still.

The kitchen waits. Nobody has it. And then Peg, at the head of her own table, in her own kitchen, says it the way she says everything, plainly, to the table.

PEG: I made him breakfast.

Nobody breathes.

PEG: After he was weighed. Lucky breakfast. Three rashers. Fried bread. He ate every bite, and I scraped his plate into the bin because there was nothing on it to scrape.

Tess writes the last line of the sum: 64.6 + 0.5 = 65.1. Then the seconds, underneath, small.

TESS: Six point nine eight. Job was seven. *(beat)* The lie on its own, he makes it. The breakfast on its own, he makes it. *(She puts the pencil down.)* Either one, he makes it. Both, he doesn't. By two hundredths.

The clock over the counter ticks. The printer's green light blinks. Rosie is looking at the chalk board, at LIAM 62.4 and the star beside it that nobody has wiped.

ROSIE: *(very quietly)* He was so pleased about the star.

Peg gets up. She takes the duster, and reaches up, and rubs out the 62.4 under the star, and then, slowly, in her sign-writer's hand, writes in its place: **65.1.**

PEG: There's your number, love.

She stands back and looks at it, the first true number the board has ever held, and puts the duster down, and squares her shoulders on thirty years of running this family off that bridge.

PEG: Your dad never had a second to spare in his life. Fifteen stone. It's why we make weight. It's the one thing in this family that's never been wrong.

Tess looks at her mother. Then up at Frank's photo, the big man in the donkey jacket, laughing at whoever held the camera. She looks at him a long time.

Then she takes her coat off the chair and goes out.

### Scene 13. The yard, dusk.

Tess sits in her car with the phone at her ear and the river running grey behind the fence. The office window glows yellow behind her; through it, the family sits on at the long table under the chalk star.

**Intercut: the flat above the bookmaker's, Tilbury.** Ray, in his shirtsleeves, awake at his table among the squared files, the window box behind him. The phone is at his ear. He has been awake since two.

TESS: Ambrose. It's Tess Marlow.

A silence on the line, the length of a held breath.

RAY: Aye.

TESS: My dad. What did he weigh?

The line hums. Out on the river a tug sounds its horn, twice, far off.

RAY: Sixty-four kilos.

Tess closes her eyes. In the office window, small behind glass, the big man goes on laughing at whoever held the camera.

RAY: You'll be wanting the rest of it.

TESS: Aye.

RAY: Tomorrow, then.

He rings off. Tess sits with the dead phone in her hand, and doesn't go in.

**Cut to black.**

**END OF EPISODE 9**

## Plants and payoffs

**Paid off here, from earlier episodes**
- **"Face home" (1, said at the door tonight):** Rosie turns her back on the caller, and the rule made before bites breaks on screen. "The Deep keeps you" (1) breaks with her: it kept her sixty seconds, then the tab brought her home — the Marlows' own pit training, the one family that has it, is what kept her alive. Her thumb stopping an inch off the wrist unit is the moment she learns *why* the Deep keeps you; Terry's saying from episode 1 is the only plant it needs.
- **The bite (taught in 5: "Only once you've stood a minute on the wrong side"):** used at last, in their sea, half her seconds, 5.4, blind, no light and no gauge. The minute and the tariff were planted; nothing else is said.
- **The plate prints a body in the black as a minus (2, 6):** Rosie crosses standing on the plate on purpose — "Wanted it written" — and Kieran finds the ticket: −46.2 at a quarter to one, to the gram against the ward's 46.2. The girl who hid everything now leaves her number where it can't lie, and the roll holds her crossing.
- **The T-meter from the ward (8):** it settles what next door is, with Terry's blood — 2.8 to 5.6 on one crab — while Peg's meter reads LO on the same drop. The proof is an instrument and a man's blood, never a taste: Danny's question dies half-asked, and nobody says what it tasted of.
- **The message, "COME ROUND FOR TEA", first stroke 4 April (8):** "I went." Rosie's crossing is the family's answer to the invitation, made alone, at night, by its best and hungriest crosser.
- **The drift table (6, 7):** Shaun's two eight-second legs come up one each way, and the family rolls him with the odds already known. Peg's answer is the doom itself: "Then we go again. Fortnight Thursday."
- **The grip and the hand (6, 8):** Rosie's ward offer to Danny is dead ("Coin says which of us comes back with one hand"), and the crab shows her the other way to feed them.
- **Liam's two hundredths (5):** 62.4 + 1.5 = 63.9 chalk; the April letter's −1.1 per cent gives 64.6 true; Peg confesses the lucky breakfast; 65.1; 6.98 against a 7.0 job. Either error alone, he lives. The audience's own theory from episode 5 was only half the answer, and the missing half is the neighbours'.
- **Kieran's log (2, 4, 6, 8):** the step on 4 April, the wobble, the chip, the *me* column — "Since Easter." The boy's homework is the proof, and the flour on the brass balance ("Never told me a lie", 1) kills the chip story: 5.000 against 4.95.
- **Terry's rewrapped lunch (8):** he found the boy's unopened sandwiches and put them back, and thought he was fussy. Even the one who feeds him in secret didn't ask.
- **The drawer (1, 4, 5):** hauled open at last. The April letter read aloud; the 2019 Register letter read aloud, and the family learns what Rosie learned from a nurse in episode 8: the police haven't been able to read the list for seven years. "We don't go to them" ran out in 2019 and nobody looked.
- **Danny's early fizz (6):** two point four was the chalk; two point two is the truth; he was fizzing before he ever touched Joe.
- **The −59.0 ticket (4, 5, 7) and Maggie's "fifty-nine" off the steelyard (2, 7):** laid beside Rosie's −46.2, with the steelyard named — two instruments agreeing while the little bridge can't read a bag of flour.
- **The dog law (1–8):** the depot dog sets up at Terry's approach, before anyone crosses — dogs smell the turned — and Peg answers "Foxes." Sixpence then picks Shaun's turned case out of the stack without being told.
- **Rosie's "cold-water swimming" (3):** true at last, dripping.
- **Ray's 2002 file (8, shut):** the hook call. "Sixty-four kilos" against a legend of fifteen stone, and "Tomorrow, then" books his kitchen-door visit.

**Planted here, for later**
- **The sea's geometry:** eight metres up, counted twice, up and down. The door of episode 10 is a depth and a tide, and the baking tray waits.
- **Terry's decision:** made the night of the crab, against Peg's "Get rid of that jumper before they come." His five-in-six odds, said by Kieran tonight, are his die in episode 10.
- **The jumper and the weed down the drain (a wonder):** the neighbours' sea in a Grays drain, and our life in their sea on Rosie's jumper — the epilogue's charge and its residue. One frond stays taped in the book, beside the 04:17 ticket.
- **Kieran's crab weighed, the first thing ever brought back from there:** the only honest channel, used by a boy. His log now holds the step, the wobble, the *me* column, two minus tickets squared together, the crab, and FOOD TURNS TOO written on the roll itself — the first writing on the roll before the night of writing.
- **The roll keeps its own copy of Rosie's crossing**, and of every lie on the board since April: the raw material of episode 10's vote — and of whatever the depot's wall has logged, which Danny has now said aloud and Peg has answered with "a full stomach."
- **"I went shopping":** the pantry next door, priced in one crab and two meter readings.
- **Peg's re-chalk, 65.1:** the board's first true number, and her hand that writes it.
- **Fortnight Thursday:** another depot run promised, unfilmed, the ratchet still turning until the rolls stop it.

**Knowledge at the end.** The family knows: next door is a sea and its food fits the turned; Rosie crossed on purpose, on the plate, and it is on the roll; Shaun is turned; the little bridge has lied since 4 April; Liam died by two hundredths, of the scale and the breakfast together; the police cannot read the Register; Kieran is starving himself. Tess alone knows what Frank weighed, and that Ray has "the rest of it". Nobody but Rosie knows what the sea was like beyond cold and dark, and nobody asks twice.

**Numbers used.** Canon only: 38.0 (12.4) and 40.0 at his birthday; 51.9 + 3.0 = 9.0 chalk, legs of 8.0; Terry's 2-second legs; the bite at 5.4; eight metres; 2.8 and 5.6 and LO; 5.000 against 4.95; −59.0; −1.1 per cent, 28 days; 62.4, 1.5, 63.9, 64.6, 65.1, 6.98 and 7.0, two hundredths; 2.4 and 2.2; sixty-four kilos; "eleven tenths"; "twenty year"; 46.2 come home from the ward. Derived, nothing resting on them: eleven cases; four turned mouths.

**Gaps.** *Closes:* what the Deep is; whether next door is food; why Liam ran out; what made the scales lie. *Kept open, as wonders:* what went down the drain; what the neighbours are; what Terry will find; what Frank's sixty-four kilos means, which opens episode 10.
