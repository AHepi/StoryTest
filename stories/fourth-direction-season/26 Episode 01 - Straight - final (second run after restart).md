# Episode 1: Straight

*The Marlows pull off the cleanest dip of their lives and get back a painting nobody believes is real.*

---

## Cold open

### Scene 1. A mews behind a private bank, Mayfair, 3:11 a.m., early March.

Rain on cobbles. A plain white van with no name on it is parked hard against the back wall of a bank that looks like a gentlemen's club. The mews is asleep. Inside the van, in the dark of the load bay, four Marlows.

DANNY MARLOW (41, big hands, a foreman's donkey jacket) sits at the wheel with a stopwatch open on his phone, his thumb over it. LIAM (19, his son, broad as a door) sits in the passenger seat eating salt and vinegar crisps too loudly. Danny takes the packet off him without looking and drops it on the dashboard.

In the back, TESS MARLOW (38, a pencil behind her ear that she never uses) kneels facing her niece. ROSIE MARLOW (22) is rigged: a padded belt, a collar with a brass disc at the throat, a wrist unit reading zero. She is small, bright-eyed and can't keep still.

TESS: Forty-four dead at midnight. Eleven two, less the lady. Job's nine four.

ROSIE: Show-off, me.

TESS: Wall's at three. Don't be pretty about it.

The dashboard clock turns from 03:11 to 03:12. Tess touches Rosie's wrist unit. Rosie flexes her fingers once, the way every Marlow does, and steps off the world.

She's gone. There's no flash and no sound. There was a girl, and now there's the van floor.

**The black.** Sound drops away to her own heartbeat and breath. A dim, grey-gold glow reaches about an arm's length around her, and it's the only light there is. Past its edge there's nothing at all, not even darkness. Her inner ear screams that she's falling. She isn't. There's no down to fall to.

She moves the way the family taught her in the pit: small pulls at nothing, no wasted effort. The glow slides over the bank's back wall and shows her its inside instead of its face: brick and mortar, a dead wasps' nest in the cavity, steel mesh, a bundle of cables like veins. The wall slides away behind her into the black. Into the glow comes the leg of a stool, then a man's knee. A GUARD sleeps on the stool, and the glow shows her the inside of him too: his ribs rising and falling, the slow fist of his heart.

Her wrist unit counts tenths. 1.8. 1.9.

She goes on past him into the black, the way Tess walked her through it.

**The van.** Tess counts aloud, her lips barely moving, her eyes on nothing.

TESS: ...two... three. There's the wall.

Through the bank's back wall comes a roar like a tipper shedding gravel. Liam jumps. Danny keeps his eyes on the stopwatch.

DANNY: She can't hear you, Tess.

TESS: I know.

**The black.** Out of the dark above her and into her glow, tungsten beads come pouring down in silence, a black rain. She keeps going through it. The edge of a shelf comes into the glow, and on it one crate, bound with steel bands, tied with cord, the knot sealed in lead. The beads are already climbing its sides. She can see through the pine as if it were water. Inside lies a canvas wrapped in tissue paper. Rosie reaches through the side of the sealed crate and draws the canvas out in its tissue, not hurrying, while the beads climb. The crate stays banded, knotted and sealed. Half a second after the canvas leaves the shelf, the beads bury the spot where it lay.

4.9. 5.0.

She swings round with the canvas flat against her chest, and something heavy passes in the dark.

She doesn't see it. She feels it: a pull in her gut, the way a lorry passing too close on the A13 tugs at a car. Nothing shows in the glow. Beyond it there's only black. Rosie flinches and the canvas swings. Then the pull is gone.

She goes back the way she came, the canvas held flat: the guard's slow heart, the cables, the mortar, sliding past.

8.9. 9.0.

Close on her fingertips as the tingle starts, a fizz under the nails. She finds the one pull in the black that means home, faces it, and steps out.

**The van.** Rosie lands on the floor of the load bay with a gasp, the canvas flat against her. The van smells of diesel and vinegar.

TESS: ...nine... four.

Danny's thumb stops the watch. It reads 9.4. He looks at Tess, his little sister, the way you'd look at a card trick.

Then comes the question this family asks before it asks whether you're hurt.

DANNY: Straight?

ROSIE: Straight.

She sits up. Her eyes go to the dashboard, to the crisp packet. It's an ordinary packet of salt and vinegar. She looks at it a beat too long.

LIAM: Want one?

ROSIE: *(grinning)* Go on, then.

She takes one. Danny pulls out into the rain. Tess leans back against the side of the van with her eyes shut and her lips still moving, still counting something.

**MAIN TITLE: SECONDS**

## Act one: The table

### Scene 2. Marlow Aggregates, Grays, dawn, Tuesday.

The Thames estuary at first light. Container cranes at Tilbury. The QEII bridge standing in the haze. Gulls working the gravel heaps. A painted sign reads MARLOW AGGREGATES · READY-MIX CONCRETE · PUBLIC WEIGHBRIDGE (CERTIFIED). There's a flooded gravel pit as flat as pewter, a concrete plant with its drum turning, and across the yard the big bridge, a steel deck for lorries set flush with the ground.

The van rolls onto the big bridge and off again. Inside the office a printer chatters like a till and pushes out a ticket nobody asked for. The scales print by themselves whenever a load settles on them.

Liam gets out holding the canvas flat in front of him in its tissue, like a tray of glass, and carries it across the yard as if the ground might tip.

### Scene 3. The weighbridge office, continuous.

It's a public office and it's Peg's kitchen, and nobody has ever minded. There's a hatch and a counter for the lorry drivers, the ticket printer, a table long enough for twelve, a stove, a Welsh dresser, and a telly and a radio on the counter. At one end of the room a steel plate the size of a hearthrug is set flush into the lino: **the little bridge**, the platform scale that weighs sacks and people to ten grams. At the end of its plate, against the wall, stands Peg's safe, old and green.

On the wall hangs a chalk board of names and weights. Painted across its top, in sign-writer's letters: A FIFTH OF A SECOND A KILO UNDER A HUNDRED. Next to it is Frank Marlow's only photo, a big man in a donkey jacket laughing at whoever held the camera. Next to that, framed, is a yellowed Ministry receipt. Round them hang newspaper cuttings in cheap frames.

The hatch is down and the public door is bolted. Outside, two lorries idle in the yard with their lights on, waiting for Peg to open. PEG MARLOW (72, apron, reading glasses on a chain) is at the stove with the fry-up on. SIXPENCE, an old grey lurcher, lies by the stove.

PEG: Mind her. On the dresser. Not by the kettle.

Liam lays the canvas on the dresser as if it might wake.

The whole firm is at the table. Danny. Tess. Rosie, damp-haired, hands round a mug. Liam. SHAUN (26, a cousin, wiry) eats standing up because he's due on a pour. KIERAN (12, Tess's son, school jumper, cereal) is thrilled to be up this early with the grown-ups. And at the stove end, TERRY MARLOW (66, Peg's younger brother, soft and heavy) turns the bacon in a pair of yellow washing-up gloves, half-singing an old washing-day song, "Dashing Away with the Smoothing Iron", under his breath.

Peg serves. Eggs, bacon, black pudding, fried bread.

PEG: And everything in you counts, breakfast too.

She puts two rashers on Rosie's plate. Rosie eats fast, then slides the plate along to Liam.

ROSIE: Big lad needs it.

The table laughs. Liam takes it. Peg taps the chalk board with her spatula, where ROSIE 44.0 sits at the top.

PEG: That's making weight.

Danny bangs his mug on the table.

DANNY: Nine point four.

The table bangs back.

PEG: Longest job a Marlow's ever done. In your dad's day two seconds did most jobs. Now it's eight, nine. And we still come home with it.

DANNY: To Tess's tenths.

They raise their mugs. Tess doesn't smile, but her ears go pink.

LIAM: Could've gone in twice. Quick in, quick out. Wall never floods.

PEG, DANNY and ROSIE *(together, a chorus they've done a thousand times)*: Never go back twice.

KIERAN: Lightest goes longest!

ROSIE: Face home.

PEG: And Marlows come back straight.

Terry turns the bacon. He doesn't raise his tea, and nobody looks at him.

TESS: *(to Liam, quietly pleased)* One trip. Nine four.

A horn sounds at the back. Terry sings out one line.

TERRY: *(singing)* 'Twas on a Tuesday morning, when I beheld my darling...

DILYS (60s, a launderette tabard under her coat) bangs in at the back door with a crate of white plastic tubs. Batch codes are printed on the lids.

DILYS: Your whites, Terry.

TERRY: There she is. Bless you, Dilys.

She's gone in thirty seconds. Everyone calls the supplier "the Laundry", because the tubs come in Dilys's launderette van.

Terry peels a lid. Peg looks over his shoulder at the tub, then at the parcel on the dresser.

PEG: That's Terry's year at the Laundry, that painting. Three hundred a day.

TESS: Hundred and nine thousand five hundred.

PEG: Insurer's man comes down Saturday. Cash, no questions, and our Terry's fed till next March.

TERRY: Hark at her. Kept man, me.

Terry eats from the tub at the stove: something pale and smooth that he spoons without much interest. Then he tips a nip of gin into his tea and takes a spoonful of beef dripping straight from the bowl.

TERRY: Fat and spirits. It's all that sticks.

KIERAN: That's disgusting.

TERRY: That's breakfast.

Sixpence gets up stiffly and growls at Terry, low and steady, the way he has for five years.

TERRY: Morning to you too.

Rosie gets up to fill the kettle. Sixpence turns his head and growls at her.

ROSIE: Oi.

PEG: He's jealous. She's been away.

ROSIE: Four hours, you soft article.

She crouches with her hand out. He backs off, still growling. She laughs and leaves him be.

Peg looks at the clock, wipes her hands, and slides the hatch up. The talk at the table changes gear without a pause.

DANNY: ...a metre thick, and now they want it thicker at the back.

SHAUN: They'll want it thicker at the front next.

Passing the dresser, Tess drops a tea towel over the tissue parcel without looking at it.

MICK (50s, lorry driver, hi-vis) leans in at the hatch for his ticket.

MICK: River's at Nobody low, Peg. Ferry's sat on the mud and the tables say high water.

PEG: Frank used to say the tide went out and forgot to tell the moon.

MICK: Frank said a lot.

PEG: He was right a lot.

She tears his ticket off and hands it through with a bacon roll. The hatch comes down.

On the radio: "...a single painting taken overnight from a private bank in Mayfair. Police say the crate was found still banded, knotted and under its lead seal, and empty."

A cheer goes up round the table. Shaun drums the table.

DANNY: *(to Rosie)* Didn't even touch the knot.

ROSIE: Tidy, me.

Tess takes the tea towel off the parcel, unwraps the canvas on the dresser, turning it face down onto a clean towel, and balls up the tissue. She opens the stove door. Kieran watches her.

KIERAN: Why d'you always burn it?

Peg points her fork at the framed cuttings. Kieran reads the nearest headline aloud.

KIERAN: "Strongroom stripped through solid wall. Police forensics: wrapping shows 'clean translation, no rotation'." *(beat)* What's no rotation?

DANNY: Means we came back straight. Every time.

PEG: Police said so. In print. *(to Tess)* Paper talks. Burn it.

The tissue goes into the stove and flares up. Tess shuts the door on it. Rosie watches it burn through the little glass window until there's nothing left.

TESS: Bus.

Kieran grabs his bag and runs. Peg unbolts the public door, and the drivers come in out of the cold for their bacon rolls. The office is a weighbridge again.

## Act two: The painting

### Scene 4. The weighbridge office, mid-morning.

The drivers have gone. The table has been cleared and the painting lies face up on the tea towel. *The Assayer's Wife*: a woman in a fur-trimmed jacket stands at a window, holding a small brass balance up to the light and weighing coins. It is painted so tenderly that the light seems to be in the room.

Peg, glasses on, has the news up on the office computer at the end of the counter. She prints the page. Under the headline, STOLEN FROM MAYFAIR BANK, is the auction catalogue's own photograph of the painting. She trims it square with the kitchen scissors, headline and all, and pins it to the wall next to the framed cuttings.

The camera holds both in one frame. In the cutting on the wall, the window is on the woman's left. On the canvas on the table, it's on her right.

Nobody looks. Peg is admiring the woman.

PEG: Look at her. Weighing up.

DANNY: She's got your face, Mum. Like she's caught someone fiddling the scales.

PEG: She has.

Tess, who never looks at pictures, is looking at the balance.

TESS: Pans are level.

They are. It's a sort of joke, and Peg laughs at it.

Rosie isn't there. She has gone up to bed. We hear her door shut overhead.

PEG: In the safe till Saturday.

Danny rolls up his right sleeve. He puts his rig's belt on over his jacket, thumbs the wrist unit, and picks up the canvas by its stretcher. He holds it flat against the safe door and pushes. His hand goes into the steel, and the painting goes with it, like a letter going through a letterbox. His arm stops at the wrist in a clean edge, as if the door had swallowed the rest. He feels about in there with his eyes shut and lets go. He draws his hand back out, empty.

LIAM: Straight?

DANNY: Hand's always straight, you plum. Rest of me's still out here hanging on to it.

PEG: Nobody's had that door open since Blair.

### Scene 5. A new bank basement, Moorgate, the City, day.

It's a hole ten metres deep under a glass tower. Danny's day crew in hard hats pour concrete into a vault floor a metre thick. Liam barrows tungsten beads, grey and heavy as lead shot, and tips them into the mix. The mixer drum turns, MARLOW MIX painted round it in red. On the ramp, a second van: HARGREAVES & SON, WEIGHTMEN. Underneath, LOCKSMITHS has been painted over and still shows through.

A FACILITIES MAN in a hard hat that has never been rained on walks the edge with Danny.

FACILITIES MAN: The bullion goes in as dust next month. Mixed through the east wall.

DANNY: Then nobody's walking out with it. They'd have to carry the wall.

FACILITIES MAN: Insurers want the floor thicker at the back.

DANNY: Thicker everywhere. Every inch is a tenth of a second they haven't got.

LIAM: *(tipping beads, grinning)* Us included.

DANNY: Then we'll get lighter.

He pats his own belly. The facilities man laughs as if it's a builder's joke.

**Later.** Tea from a flask on the tailgate of the van. Liam has concrete to the elbows.

LIAM: If I got under seventy...

DANNY: You'd still be built like your grandad. Big lads pour, son. Concrete pays the bills.

LIAM: Grandad crossed.

DANNY: And look where it got him.

Liam looks at his boots.

DANNY: I was your size at seventeen. They picked your auntie instead of me. *(he drinks)* Best day of my life.

Liam waits for the joke to finish. It doesn't. Danny screws the lid back on the flask.

### Scene 6. The weighbridge office, 4 p.m.

Kieran is home from school. At the end of the counter lies the printer's pile of tickets since last night: lorries that weighed after hours, their numbers in purple ink, and one of his own. He finds it: 21:00, the little bridge, 20.00. He copies it into a school exercise book under a whole column of 20.00s. Under the counter by his feet sits the thing it weighs, a cast-iron 20-kilo test weight, grey paint, a stamped handle. Above the table, taped to the wall, is his graph: a dead flat line.

PEG: *(at the till, counting the day's cash)* You and your homework.

KIERAN: Miss says find the neighbours without looking. If they're heavy enough to feel, a good enough scale'll feel them.

PEG: The neighbours can pay for their own ticket roll.

Kieran shuts the book and looks up at Frank's photo.

KIERAN: When can I cross?

PEG: When your mother says. And you'll make weight first. *(She comes round the counter and stands beside him under the photo.)* Your grandad was fifteen stone when the black took him. That's why we make weight.

She taps the painted rule at the top of the board with her wedding ring: A FIFTH OF A SECOND A KILO UNDER A HUNDRED. For a moment the camera holds the big laughing man and the rule in one frame.

KIERAN: I'm forty.

PEG: Then you're lighter than your cousin Rosie. Don't tell her. *(calling out)* Somebody take this boy to the pit before he gets ideas.

### Scene 7. The flooded gravel pit, dusk.

The water has gone cold blue and the conveyor gantry stands black against the sky. A floating pontoon. Kieran stands at its edge in a wetsuit, wearing swimming goggles painted black with enamel. Tess is on the pontoon in her coat.

TESS: In. Roll. Find up.

He drops in.

**Under the water** it's black. Bubbles roar. His body tumbles and he loses every direction. He kicks hard the wrong way, down, into colder water.

**On the pontoon** Tess's lips move. At fourteen she's on her knees at the edge. At sixteen he breaks the surface, gasping, and tears the goggles off.

KIERAN: Couldn't... which way's...

TESS: Can't see up? Breathe out a bit. Follow your bubbles. Bubbles always know. *(beat)* Took your Uncle Terry a month to find his.

KIERAN: Is the black like that?

TESS: Worse. Your ears'll swear you're falling. You're not. There's nowhere to fall.

KIERAN: When can I go in?

TESS: When you find up blind. First time, every time.

KIERAN: Lightest goes longest.

TESS: Lightest goes when it can find up. Again.

He pulls the goggles down, and stops.

KIERAN: How old were you?

TESS: Fourteen.

KIERAN: That's only two years.

TESS: It was a different week.

She doesn't say which week. He drops in. She counts.

### Scene 8. The weighbridge office, evening.

Steam and the smell of shepherd's pie. Terry cooks in his yellow gloves. He measures the salt with a teaspoon and levels it off with a knife, because he can't taste it properly any more. Kieran, wrapped in a towel with blue lips, stands at the stove for the heat. At the table Peg counts cash into bags.

KIERAN: Mum says it took you a month.

TERRY: To find my bubbles. I was never a swimmer. I was a cook who could count.

KIERAN: I found mine. Second go.

TERRY: Did you now.

KIERAN: So you could tell her. That I'm ready.

TERRY: She doesn't listen to me, love. I'm the cook.

KIERAN: She does. Go on.

Terry stirs. He takes his time.

TERRY: You know I went again? After.

KIERAN: Everyone knows.

TERRY: The black never gave me back, love. Eleven times I went again. Two seconds a go. Hungry every time after.

KIERAN: I'd face home.

TERRY: Everyone faces home. *(He taps the pie dish with the spoon.)* Stay in too long and you come apart. There's nothing in the black to hold you together. Like a crust with no dish.

KIERAN: And then what?

TERRY: Then the wall goes on reading you. What you weighed, going soft at the edges, for a month. They let the family stand a minute by the reading. That's your funeral now.

Kieran swallows. He doesn't give up.

KIERAN: Rosie says you feel things. In there.

TERRY: *(stirring)* Pulls. Home's the strongest, like someone calling you in for your tea. Then out Mucking way there's the Hill, big and hard, like leaning on a lorry. And under us, right under this lino, there's the Deep. Soft and wide. Like a feather bed you'd sink in.

KIERAN: What if you followed one?

TERRY: The Hill takes you. The Deep keeps you.

KIERAN: And the Far?

Terry stops stirring. The boy has been listening to the men.

TERRY: Way off the other side. Faint. Like a kettle two doors down. *(beat)* Nobody's daft enough to find out.

KIERAN: So will you tell her?

TERRY: I'll tell her you found your bubbles.

Kieran takes that as a yes and grins. Terry doesn't.

PEG: *(not looking up from the cash)* Face home. That's all a Marlow needs to know.

## Act three: Paper

### Scene 9. The weighbridge office, Wednesday, 9 a.m.

MR PINNOCK (50s, Trading Standards, a lanyard, a cardigan under his anorak) sets his own cast-iron weights on the little bridge one at a time, each stamped with a crown. The printer chatters. He checks each ticket against a sheet on his clipboard. Outside, his flatbed full of more weights sits on the big bridge.

PINNOCK: Little bridge, true to ten grams. Big bridge, true to twenty kilos. Both on the one roll?

PEG: Both on the one roll.

PINNOCK: And the old rolls?

PEG: Loft.

PINNOCK: All of them?

PEG: All of them.

PINNOCK: Twenty-five years of audit rolls in your loft, Mrs Marlow, by law.

PEG: Bloody paper.

He hangs the new certificate on the wall with a little hammer from his own pocket. Then his eye falls on the framed receipt next to it, and he reads it aloud.

PINNOCK: "One object, found wet, sand adhering." *(beat)* That's never...

PEG: It is.

PINNOCK: The one?

PEG: Our field. Mucking Marsh. Nineteen ninety-five. Frank found it one morning in the wet grass, going in and out like a bad bulb. No hole, no scorch, no track, nothing. Like it had walked there. Then the Ministry came and put a fence round my farm and gave me a gravel pit for my trouble.

PINNOCK: They made the rigs off that.

PEG: They made the rigs off my field. I got a receipt.

PINNOCK: *(looking round the busy room)* It's not been a bad trade, Mrs Marlow.

PEG: It was a farm.

Pinnock notices Kieran's graph on the wall, the flat line of 20.00s.

PINNOCK: Somebody keeps better books than you.

PEG: My grandson. Homework. Twenty kilos every night and a straight line to show for it.

PINNOCK: Straight's what I like to see. *(packing his case)* If anything changes, you'll hear from us.

PEG: I'll look forward to it.

He goes. A little later the post comes through the door: a gas bill, a seed catalogue, and a brown window envelope from Thurrock Council. Peg opens the gas bill. She drops the council envelope, unopened, into the dresser drawer. The drawer is thick with them, brown and buff, years deep, and it won't shut. She flattens the pile with her palm, and it shifts. For a second, low down between the newer ones, the corner of an old envelope shows, gone the colour of tea, typed: MR F. MARLOW. Then it's gone under again. She shuts the drawer with her hip.

### Scene 10. The weighbridge office, Wednesday, 1 p.m.

The office telly plays with the sound low, for the drivers. It shows a hospital theatre. A LICENSED SURGEON, masked and gowned, a rig's collar over her gown, stands over a sleeping child. A caption reads: CROSSING SURGERY: HER 4,000TH. She steps off the world. Three seconds later she's back, with something small and dark in her gloved palm, and she drops it into a steel dish a nurse holds out. The child's skin is unbroken.

Rosie sits at the table in front of it with her chin on her fists, rapt. Her phone is propped against the sugar bowl, playing another surgeon and another child. She's been watching them all morning.

Danny comes in for his dinner, wiping concrete off his hands.

DANNY: Three seconds. Soft.

ROSIE: She's done four thousand. Never turned.

DANNY: 'Cause she faces home.

Rosie doesn't answer. On the screen the child's mother is crying.

On the radio over the counter, and nobody pays it any attention, the BBC: "...detectors picked up the ripple of two black holes merging, with nothing in our sky where they ought to be. Astronomers are calling it a next-door event. Now the weather: fog on the estuary by morning..."

At the stove Terry warms his dinner in a chipped blue enamel pot, his own pot, which nobody else touches. He has the paper folded back to an advert: a clean white page with one line on it. RESTORATION. SEVENTY PER CENT. OBVERSE, HARLEY STREET.

TERRY: One day.

ROSIE: *(without missing a beat)* I'll price it for your birthday.

TERRY: Price it? Buy me two. One for Christmas.

Rosie laughs, and so does Terry. Danny doesn't. He's watching the surgeon's hands.

### Scene 11. The Met Crossing Unit, an office block in Victoria, Wednesday, 1 p.m.

Thirty seconds. Grey open-plan, grey light. DS RAY AMBROSE (54, thin as a rake, a good suit hanging off him) sits at his desk. On it are a mug of vodka, with no pretence that it's water, a packet of cream crackers, and a block of lard with a butter knife standing in it. He spreads lard on a cracker and eats it.

In front of him is a photograph of the Mayfair art store: shelves drowned in tungsten beads, and in the foreground the crate, banded, knotted and sealed.

He opens a notebook and writes a time: 03:12. He underlines it, shuts the notebook and puts it in his inside pocket.

COLLEAGUE: *(passing, coat on)* Lunch, Ray? Or is that it?

RAY: This is it.

The colleague goes, shaking his head. Ray takes another cracker.

## Act four: The birthday

### Scene 12. The weighbridge office, Thursday, 6 a.m.

Peg in her dressing gown at the counter. She takes her glucose meter out of the cutlery drawer, from among the spoons. She pricks her finger and touches the blood to the strip. The meter beeps and shows a number, an ordinary morning number. She nods at it the way she nods at a lorry driver she knows, and drops it back in with the forks.

Then she ties her apron over the dressing gown. It's a big day.

Out of the dresser comes a brass shop balance, the kind with two pans and a pointer, and after it a wooden box of brass weights, from a pair of big two-kilos down to a ten-gram no bigger than a shirt button. Both came from the old farm shop. Peg sets the kilo weight in one pan and spoons flour into the other until the pointer settles dead centre.

Kieran comes down in his pyjamas with his hair on end, twelve today. He sits on a stool and watches.

KIERAN: Why don't you use the little bridge?

PEG: Little bridge is for sacks and Marlows. *(She taps the brass.)* These were my mother's. Never told me a lie.

She tips the flour into the bowl and hands him the sugar spoon to lick.

### Scene 13. The weighbridge office, Thursday evening.

Bunting hangs over the chalk board and a HAPPY 12TH banner is taped across the dresser. The lorries are parked up for the night. The whole family is there: Peg, Terry, Tess, Kieran, Danny, Rosie, Liam, Shaun.

For this family a birthday means a weigh-in, the way other families do candles. One by one everyone who crosses, and the birthday boy, steps onto the little bridge. The displays are dark after hours, so the printer does the telling: it chatters, Tess reads the ticket out, Peg chalks it and the table cheers.

Shaun steps on. 58.0.

TESS: Eight four.

Cheers.

Danny steps on. 89.0.

TESS: Two two.

DANNY: *(wiggling his fingers)* Enough to post a painting.

Laughter.

Rosie steps on. 43.6. Tess's lips move.

TESS: Eleven two eight.

It's the loudest cheer of the night. Peg chalks it and draws a little star beside it. Rosie bows.

PEG: That's making weight. Four hundred grams since Monday.

Liam steps on. The ticket reads 72.0. There's a beat.

SHAUN: Mind the plate!

DANNY: Big lad!

The cheer turns into a joke about putting him on the lorry bridge. Liam laughs along, because you do. Peg chalks it without a star.

Then the birthday boy. 40.0.

TESS: Twelve dead.

KIERAN: Twelve seconds! I'm longer than Rosie!

ROSIE: Oi!

KIERAN: Lightest goes longest!

The table roars. Tess doesn't.

TESS: Not yet.

KIERAN: When?

TESS: You know when.

KIERAN: *(rolling his eyes; the whole table has heard it before)* First time, every time.

Terry doesn't step on the plate, and nobody asks him to.

**At the printer**, Liam tears off his own ticket and speaks to Tess, low.

LIAM: What would I need?

TESS: For what?

LIAM: A proper job.

TESS: Seventy-two is five point six. I've nothing that needs five point six.

LIAM: What needs seven?

TESS: Most things, now.

LIAM: I'd get near it.

TESS: Near enough isn't.

He folds his ticket small. Tess holds out her hand for it, because a Marlow's ticket never leaves this room, and he gives it to her. She drops it in the stove. It burns. Inside the printer the audit roll winds on, keeping its own copy, as it has every night for twenty-five years.

**The cake.** Peg assembles the sponge and hands Rosie the jam.

PEG: Open that, love.

Rosie twists hard, then harder. Her knuckles go white. It won't give.

PEG: *(laughing)* Our Rosie never could open a jar.

Peg takes it and turns it once the other way, and the lid pops. She laughs as she spreads the jam. Rosie watches her laugh and says nothing.

Kieran blows out twelve candles. Peg cuts the cake onto saucers, one each, all round the table. Terry sits at the stove end with his tub. There's no saucer for Terry. There never is.

Kieran pushes his own slice along the table to him.

KIERAN: Go on, Uncle Terry.

TERRY: Goes through me like water, love. Since I turned.

Peg's knife stops in the cake.

For a second the only sound in the room is the fridge humming. Nobody looks at Peg. Peg looks at the cake.

ROSIE: I'll have it. *(She reaches for the slice.)* Birthday rules.

TESS: You'll have nothing. You're forty-three six.

The table laughs. Rosie clutches her heart as if she's been shot. The knife goes on through the cake.

PEG: Who wants the end bit?

**The card.** Rosie signs Kieran's card at the table, fast, with a flourish. It's flawless mirror script, every letter running right to left. She sticks it on the fridge next to an old card gone yellow at the corners, which says HAPPY 64TH NAN in a fourteen-year-old's mirror script. The old one is careful and a little wobbly. The new one flows.

Kieran takes the new card to the shaving mirror by the back door and reads it out.

KIERAN: "Happy birthday, shrimp. Face home. R."

TESS: Show-off.

ROSIE: Show-off, me.

**Nine o'clock.** The clock over the counter says so, and Kieran slides off his chair, birthday or not. He hauls the test weight out from under the counter with both hands and a lot of breath, and sets it in the middle of the little bridge. The printer chatters. 20.00. He writes it in his book. Peg opens her mouth to say "homework" and, because it's his birthday, doesn't.

### Scene 14. The weighbridge office, after midnight.

It's dark. Both scales' displays are dark at night. Only the printer's green light blinks.

Terry comes down in his slippers. He opens the fridge and takes out his blue enamel pot. He takes off the lid.

The level has dropped. It's two fingers below where he left it.

He looks at it for a long time. Then he looks up at the ceiling.

He takes a white tub off the stack, peels the lid, and tops the pot back up to the line where it was. He puts it back and shuts the fridge. In his basket, Sixpence curls his lip at him, as always.

TERRY: I know. I know.

He sits down at the dark table and hums the washing-day song, so quietly it's hardly humming.

## Act five: The sale

### Scene 15. The weighbridge office, Saturday, 10 a.m.

Danny puts his hand into the safe door up to the wrist and draws the painting back out, like pulling a letter back through a letterbox. Peg folds a clean tea towel round it. Rosie is already in her coat with the keys to her own van.

PEG: Tess comes. I come. You drive.

ROSIE: I carried her out. I'll carry her in.

Peg doesn't argue. Terry is at the stove.

TERRY: Get a good price.

PEG: I'll get your year.

### Scene 16. The upstairs room at the Ferry House, Tilbury, Saturday, noon.

A function room with a dartboard, stacked chairs and a folding table under a white cloth. Through the window, the Tilbury ferry is coming in across the mud. MR ASKE (60, loss adjuster, cashmere scarf) sits with a holdall at his feet. His expert, DR FARRANT (40s, conservator, cotton gloves, a loupe on a cord, a small camera with a macro lens), stands at the table.

Rosie unwraps the canvas from its tea towel and lays it on the cloth. Peg sits down opposite Aske as if it's parents' evening. Tess stands by the door, counting the holdall with her eyes.

ASKE: And you came by this, Mrs...?

PEG: Found it in a skip.

ASKE: Lucky skip.

Farrant gets to work: loupe, then an ultraviolet torch. The old varnish glows the dull green it should. She tilts the canvas and photographs its tacking edge up close, where the bare linen wraps round the stretcher, then the stretcher itself.

FARRANT: Paint's right. Cracking's right. Varnish is right.

Under the table, Aske's shoe nudges the holdall an inch towards Peg. Peg's hand nearly moves.

Farrant opens the auction catalogue at a full-page plate and holds it up beside the canvas. She frowns and looks from one to the other.

FARRANT: The window's on the wrong side.

Silence.

FARRANT: Verlaet lit from the left. Every picture he ever painted, the window's on the left. *(She draws the fall of light in the air above the canvas, not touching the paint.)* This light comes from the right.

ASKE: Meaning?

FARRANT: Meaning someone copied it off a slide with the slide in the projector the wrong way round. Beautifully. Old canvas, old ground, they've even aged the varnish. *(She peels off her gloves.)* It's a copy, Mr Aske.

Aske's shoe draws the holdall back.

ASKE: Then my client's lost nothing worth buying back.

PEG: That came off the wall of that bank.

ASKE: Then that bank's been had, Mrs... *(He stands and picks up the holdall.)* I don't believe I got your name.

PEG: Good.

Rosie is already wrapping the canvas back in its tea towel, quick and neat, before anyone else has moved. Peg hasn't moved at all. The camera finds Tess at the window with her lips moving.

TESS: *(barely audible)* Hundred and nine thousand five hundred.

### Scene 17. Rosie's van, the ferry road, Tilbury, Saturday afternoon.

Rosie drives. Peg sits beside her with the painting face down on her knees. Tess is in the back.

PEG: Bank's hung a copy. Kept the real one in Switzerland and let the insurance pay for the frame. They're worse than us.

TESS: So who's paying for Terry?

Nobody answers. Nine point four seconds, the cleanest dip a Marlow ever did, and they've stolen a copy.

PEG: We'll find it.

At the ferry landing the passengers come up the ramp off the mud. Among them is a man in a donkey jacket with a newspaper clamped under his arm. His left sleeve is folded and pinned where the hand should be.

PEG: Shook on it, him.

TESS: Mum.

PEG: What? He did.

The lights change, and Rosie pulls away.

### Scene 18. The weighbridge office, Saturday, dusk.

The painting lies on the dresser, face up and unwanted. Terry is at the stove with his blue pot. Rosie stands at the sink with her back to the room and the tap running, rinsing mugs. Kieran does homework at the table. Peg is at the counter with the till roll. Tess is at the chalk board with the duster in her hand, rubbing out nothing.

A car rolls onto the big bridge and the printer chatters out its weight. Nobody's due. Without a word Tess crosses to the dresser, drops a tea towel over the canvas, and goes back to her board.

Ray Ambrose comes in at the public door like any customer and waits at the counter until Peg looks up.

RAY: Mrs Marlow.

PEG: We're shut.

RAY: Your door's open. *(He shows his warrant card.)* DS Ambrose. Crossing Unit.

He takes two photographs out of an envelope and lays them side by side on the counter. Both show linen so close up that each thread looks like a twisted rope. In the left-hand photo the threads twist one way. In the right-hand photo they twist the other, like a left glove laid beside a right.

RAY: Their expert looked at the paint. I look at the cloth. *(He taps the left-hand photo.)* The auction house, before the sale. The tacking edge, under a glass. *(He taps the right.)* Same edge, photographed at noon today. The loss adjuster sent it over with a note. "Offered to my client by persons unknown."

PEG: I don't know any persons.

RAY: Linen has a hand, Mrs Marlow. This canvas came back the wrong way round. It's the original.

Nobody moves. At the table Kieran's pen has stopped. The tap runs.

PEG: Then the bank hung a copy.

Ray looks at her. Then he looks past her, along the wall: the chalk board with its names and its stars, the framed cuttings, and Frank Marlow's photo, the big man laughing in his donkey jacket. His eyes stop there. He looks at the photo for a long moment.

RAY: Then the bank hung a copy.

He leaves the photographs on the counter and goes. Tess puts down the duster and follows him out, to see him off the property.

**The yard.** It's dusk, with the lights of Tilbury across the water and the Nobody Tide slapping at a pontoon somewhere in the dark. Ray stands at his car with his keys in his hand.

RAY: I met your dad once.

TESS: You never met my dad.

RAY: I met what was left of him.

He gets in and drives off across the big bridge. Behind Tess, in the office, the printer chatters and prints the weight of his car.

Tess stands alone in the yard. Her lips are moving. She's counting something, and she can't make it come out.

**END OF EPISODE 1**

## Plants and payoffs

**Payoffs from earlier episodes.** None. This is the first episode. Inside it, one gap closes: the dip worked. It also sets up and breaks its own want, the Saturday sale that was meant to buy Terry's year.

**Gaps left open for later writers:** whether the painting is real (Peg's copy theory against Ray's linen); how to pay for Terry's year; the weight at 3:12; what was left of Frank; Terry's pot; the Hill, the Deep and the Far (lore, a wonder).

**Plants, with where each pays off.**
- **Rosie turned (pays off in 3).** Kept to the plan's quiet tells, so that ordinary viewers stay level with the family and only sharp ones suspect. She looks too long at the crisp packet (camera on the straight side). She eats fast and gives her plate away, and is cheered for it. Sixpence growls at her ("He's jealous, she's been away"). She watches the tissue burn. She says "Never turned" over the surgeon. She fails with the jar the wrong way. Her card is flawless mirror script beside the careful old one. At the Ferry House she is first to wrap the canvas away. Terry's pot drops overnight. The camera never singles her out at "It's the original".
- **The painting turned with its carrier (2, 3).** The catalogue cutting pinned on the wall shows the window on the left; the canvas shows it on the right; nobody looks. (The cutting is the news page Peg prints and trims that morning, headline and all; no morning paper could carry a theft done at 3 a.m.) Ray's two linen photos, the threads twisting opposite ways. The painting is left on the dresser under a tea towel at the end of the episode; episode 2's hops start from there.
- **The weight at 3:12 (3, 7).** Rosie's flinch at 5.0; Ray's notebook, "03:12", underlined.
- **The tingle (5, 6).** It comes at 9.0, late in Rosie's crossing, as she is nearly home, so the audience learns it means the seconds are almost gone. That lets Liam's tingle (5) and Danny's (6) come "early". Her route, for anyone who replays it: past the guard at 1.9, the wall floods at 3, the canvas out just ahead of the beads, the pull at 5.0, and back the same way, so going in and coming back take about as long. Nothing turns on these tenths.
- **Tess's secret (3, 6).** Liam: "Could've gone in twice." The chorus "Never go back twice." Tess: "One trip. Nine four." Mayfair's 9.4 feeds "I gave you nine" in 6.
- **Tess's flaw (5, 7).** "Near enough isn't", said to Liam; she keeps every number in her head and gives him no answer on paper. "First time, every time": her test for Kieran is a number she can check. In 5 she sends Liam with two tenths; in 7 Peg plans "near enough".
- **"Everything in you counts, breakfast too" (5, 9).** Said by Peg at the fry-up.
- **The double life (Gangster guest).** The door stays bolted and the hatch down until the tissue is burned; when the hatch opens for Mick, the table switches to the Moorgate pour and Tess drops a tea towel over the parcel; she does the same when Ray's car sets off the printer.
- **The doom's concrete (2, 3, 7).** MARLOW MIX on the drum at Moorgate. "Every inch is a tenth of a second they haven't got." "Us included." "Then we'll get lighter": the family answers thicker floors with thinner crossers, and Liam starts at once ("If I got under seventy..."). Peg: in Frank's day two seconds did most jobs; now it's eight or nine.
- **The safe (4, 5, 6).** It stands at the end of the little bridge's plate. The family only ever gets into it by sending a hand through the door; "Nobody's had that door open since Blair" sets up Peg using her key in 5.
- **The sent hand (4, 6).** Danny posts the painting into the safe; his wrist ends in a clean edge. "Hand's always straight... Rest of me's still out here hanging on to it" (a sent hand never turns). "Enough to post a painting."
- **The scales (2, 4, 5, 6, 9).** Both print by themselves when a load settles, with displays dark at night (the 02:04 ticket, the 6 stakeout). Kieran weighs his 20 kg test weight at nine every night, birthday or not, and sorts the tickets after school, which is how he will find the 04:17 ticket in 2 and the 02:04 ticket in 4. It reads 20.00 and his graph is a flat line. Pinnock says "Straight's what I like to see" and "If anything changes, you'll hear from us" (the April letter in 4).
- **Paper (7, 10).** Pinnock: twenty-five years of audit rolls in the loft by law, and the new certificate hung on the wall (Peg stops at it in 7). Tickets are burned but the audit roll keeps its own copy.
- **The drawer (4, 9, 10).** Peg drops an unopened council envelope into a drawer years deep; it won't shut, and as the pile shifts under her palm, the corner of an old envelope typed MR F. MARLOW shows for a second, low down (the 2001 Ministry letter, opened in 10). Nobody can read anything into it yet.
- **The door (10).** The framed receipt, "found wet, sand adhering". Peg: Mucking Marsh, 1995, "going in and out like a bad bulb", "like it had walked there". Frank's "the tide went out and forgot to tell the moon", said to Mick at Nobody low.
- **Frank (9, 10).** "Fifteen stone when the black took him" is said directly under the painted rule, a fifth of a second a kilo under a hundred (95 kg buys one second). Danny tells Liam "built like your grandad... look where it got him". The jar: Peg laughs as she opens it, and Rosie watches her laugh and says nothing, which is the look Frank gives her in 10's replay. Ray's long look at the photo of the big man, and "I met what was left of him" (he knows the wall read 64.3).
- **Terry (2, 3, 6, 9, 10).** Gloves; gin and dripping, "Fat and spirits. It's all that sticks". His eleven two-second hops, "hungry every time after". "Everyone faces home" (facing home did nothing for his turn; he wants Peg to stop blaming him for it). "Since I turned", and Peg's knife stops. He is never weighed. He keeps his blue enamel pot and tops it up in secret. "Took your Uncle Terry a month to find his" bubbles (his step in 10 will be slow). He sings a line of an old washing-day song, "Dashing Away with the Smoothing Iron", when Dilys comes on Tuesdays, and hums it alone at night (texture only; its words say nothing about home or going).
- **Ray turned (2).** Vodka in a mug and lard on crackers at his desk; "This is it".
- **The Laundry (8).** Dilys's van on Tuesdays; white tubs with batch codes on the lids; the family's name for the supplier.
- **Surgery and the drift (6, epilogue).** The TV surgeon at three seconds, her 4,000th, "Never turned", and Rosie's want. The surgeon wears the rig; the child never leaves the table.
- **Obverse (2).** The advert, "Restoration. Seventy per cent", with no price shown, seen on Wednesday. Rosie: "I'll price it for your birthday."
- **The message (8).** The BBC's merger "with nothing in our sky".
- **Lore (5, 9, 10, epilogue).** Terry, trying to put Kieran off: "You come apart"; the minute by the reading. The tugs: home is "like someone calling you in for your tea", the Hill "like leaning on a lorry", the Deep "right under this lino... a feather bed", the Far "a kettle two doors down". "The Hill takes you. The Deep keeps you." "Nobody's daft enough to find out."
- **The pit (9).** Blindfold goggles; "Breathe out a bit. Follow your bubbles"; "There's nowhere to fall".
- **Liam (5).** He wants to cross: "If I got under seventy", "What needs seven?" / "Most things, now", "I'd get near it" / "Near enough isn't". His 72.0 gets no star.
- **Kieran (5-9).** 40.0, "Twelve seconds!" He is "lighter than your cousin Rosie". "First time, every time". Tess's "It was a different week" (her wound).
- **Peg's meter (3).** She pricks her finger at six and the meter shows an ordinary number; in 3 it will read LO on Rosie.
- **The brass balance (9).** "These were my mother's. Never told me a lie." Its box of brass weights runs from a pair of two-kilos and a kilo down to ten grams, so in 9 it can weigh a five-kilo bag of flour.
- **Numbers used.** Rosie 44.0 at midnight before Mayfair (11.2 seconds) and 43.6 at the birthday (11.28). Danny 89.0 (2.2). Shaun 58.0 (8.4). Liam 72.0 (5.6). Kieran 40.0 (12.0). Test weight 20.00. £300 a day, £109,500 a year. The object in 1995. The surgeon's 4,000th. No new canonical numbers.
- **The grip (2, 4, 6).** The one-handed man on the ferry; "Shook on it, him". Nothing is explained.
- **Names introduced here** for later writers to keep: the painter Verlaet (always lit from the left); Mr Aske, the loss adjuster, and Dr Farrant, his expert; the Ferry House, Tilbury; the Moorgate bank floor; Hargreaves & Son, weightmen; Terry's blue enamel pot; Terry's washing-day song; the card HAPPY 64TH NAN on the fridge.
