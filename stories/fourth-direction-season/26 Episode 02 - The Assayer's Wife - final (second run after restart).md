# Episode 2: The Assayer's Wife

*To sell the painting by Friday, the Marlows must make it look like itself, and Rosie secretly uses every attempt as a cure.*

---

## Cold open

### Scene 1. Under Cheapside, the City of London, Tuesday, 04:16.

Deep under a bank. A corridor of painted breeze-block outside a strongroom door: steel, a wheel, a brass plate. A NIGHT GUARD in his sixties sits on a plastic chair with a flask of tea and a crossword folded to a square. Nobody has been through that door since six last night.

04:17. He fills in a clue.

A roar comes through the steel, like a tipper shedding its load, and it doesn't stop. The floor hums under his boots. His tea is in his lap.

His radio crackles, then fills: a dozen voices at once, other buildings, other basements, all talking over each other.

He's up and running for the stairs.

**Cheapside.** He bursts out of a side door into the empty street: wet tarmac, sodium light, a bank's brass lamps. The roar is out here too. It comes up out of the pavement, through the gratings and the glass pavement lights and the brass vents at the foot of every building. All down the street, in doorway after doorway, other guards have come out with radios: a woman in a tabard outside a jeweller's, two men at a bank's revolving door, a lad with a torch outside a gold dealer's. They look at each other along the street. Every one of them is talking. A night bus slows in the middle of the road, its driver leaning over the wheel to look.

Then the roar stops. Everywhere, in the same second.

In the quiet, from under the pavement, comes a ticking like rain on a shed roof.

**Below.** Torchlight on grey dust. Tungsten beads, grey as river gravel and heavier than lead shot, have buried a wall of steel drawers to their handles. In an art store, crates stand in a grey tide. In a safe-deposit hall the brass numbers have gone under, row by row. The beads are still settling, ticking. A hundred basements across the Square Mile, flooded in the same second.

### Scene 2. The Marlow house, the bathroom, Grays, a few minutes later.

Dark. The frosted window is the grey of the estuary before dawn. A tap drips.

ROSIE MARLOW, 22, in an old jumper of Danny's that reaches her knees, pads in barefoot and hooks the bathroom scale out from under the basin with her toe. She steps on. The display lights blue under her chin.

43.1.

She smiles, alone in the dark, the way a girl smiles at a good exam result. Then she steps off and slides the scale back with her toe, slow, so it doesn't scrape, so nobody wakes.

### Scene 3. The weighbridge office, Grays, Tuesday, 06:30.

Peg's kitchen is also the office of a public weighbridge, and it smells of bacon, diesel and toast. Before seven it's family only: the hatch onto the yard is down and the public door is bolted. The ticket printer. The little bridge, a steel plate set into the lino at one end, with Peg's old green safe standing at the end of its plate. Frank's one photo, a big man in a donkey jacket laughing at whoever held the camera; the framed Ministry receipt ("One object, found wet, sand adhering"); the framed forensic cuttings; the chalk board of everyone's weight. Outside the window, fog on the river, the Tilbury cranes coming out of it one by one.

On the radio, the same calm BBC voice that reported the silent merger.

RADIO: ...a ripple lasting several seconds, recorded by gravity meters around the world at seventeen minutes past four. Scientists are calling it a next-door event. In the City of London, every weigh-wall tripped at the same moment, and several banks say their vaults will be closed for days while...

KIERAN MARLOW, 12, in school uniform, sits at his end of the counter with a bowl of cereal. At "next-door event" his spoon stops. The printer's pile of tickets since last night is a job he saves for after school; he pulls it towards him now and goes through it like a bank clerk. Lorries that weighed after hours, their numbers in purple ink. His own ticket from nine last night, the cast-iron test weight on the little bridge: 20.00. He copies it into his log.

Then one more. He holds it up to the window.

KIERAN: Nan. Who weighed something at seventeen minutes past four?

PEG MARLOW, 72, at the stove, a fish slice in one hand, doesn't turn round.

PEG: Nobody weighs anything at seventeen minutes past four.

KIERAN: The little bridge did. Nought point three one. Nothing on it.

He looks at the plate. Nothing on it now, either. Just the steel, and the safe at the end.

TERRY MARLOW, 66, Peg's brother, cracking eggs into a pan in yellow washing-up gloves, nods at the radio.

TERRY: Somebody next door's dropped the good china.

Kieran tapes the ticket into his log. On the wall above him, over his graph with its dead flat line, hangs a poster his school printed for the class: a map of the sky in purple smudges and threads. THE NEIGHBOURS' GALAXIES: FOUND BY WEIGHING, NEVER BY LOOKING. Along the bottom: SIXTY YEARS WE POINTED DISHES THE WRONG WAY.

PEG: That's my roll you're sticking in your book.

KIERAN: It's them, Nan. Miss says you can't look at them, you can only weigh them. If I weigh the same thing every night for a year, I'll see their tide.

PEG: And have you?

KIERAN: *(honest)* Not yet.

Peg turns the bacon. Kieran smooths the ticket flat with his thumb: 04:17. 0.31 kg.

**MAIN TITLE: SECONDS**

## Act one: Peg's plan

### Scene 4. The weighbridge office, Tuesday, 06:45.

Breakfast, which at the Marlows is also business. Out in the yard two tippers idle in the fog with their lights on, waiting for seven. The family is at the long table. DANNY MARLOW, 41, foreman, in his concrete-grey work jacket. His son LIAM, 19, big-boned, quiet. Cousin SHAUN, 26, wiry. TESS MARLOW, 38, pencil behind her ear, which she never uses. Terry at the stove. Kieran at his end of the counter, his log open beside his cereal. Sixpence, the old lurcher, under the table, waiting on Liam, who is a soft touch.

At the dresser Peg pricks her finger, touches the drop to her glucose meter, reads it, and drops the meter back in the cutlery drawer among the fish knives.

Rosie comes down last, in the big jumper. She's grey under the eyes and bright everywhere else.

ROSIE: Forty-three one.

PEG: On what?

ROSIE: Bathroom.

PEG: Bathroom scale's a liar. On the bridge. Before you eat.

Rosie steps onto the little bridge in her socks. Its display is dark till opening; the printer chatters and pushes out a ticket. Peg tears it off and holds it up like a winning raffle number: 43.10. Danny bangs the table. Shaun whistles.

PEG: Lightest crosser since your Auntie Tess.

Peg chalks 43.1 beside ROSIE on the board, under 43.6, and draws another star. Tess watches her do it. Something crosses her face and goes.

A horn at the back. DILYS, 60s, launderette tabard under her coat, bangs in at the back door with a crate of white plastic tubs, batch codes printed on their lids, and sets it on the side.

DILYS: Your whites, Terry.

TERRY: Bless you, Dilys.

Peg counts notes out of the float tin into an envelope. It's a thin envelope.

PEG: Tell him the rest next Tuesday.

DILYS: He'll want it, Peg. He always does.

PEG: He'll get it.

Dilys goes. Terry stacks his tubs in the fridge, one shelf of his own, gloves on.

PEG: *(to the table)* That was the last of the float. So.

She nods at the safe. Danny buckles on his rig's belt, thumbs the wrist unit, and stands beside the plate, not on it. He puts his hand into the safe door up to the wrist and feels about inside, eyes on the ceiling. He draws out the painting through the steel, and lays it on the table.

Peg takes the catalogue cutting off the wall, where she pinned it last week, and lays it beside the canvas. *The Assayer's Wife*: a woman at a window in a fur-trimmed jacket, a little brass balance in her hand, light coming in over her shoulder. In the cutting the window is on the left. On the table it is on the right.

PEG: I've got a buyer. Private. Collector, over for the spring sales. He's seen the photo. Copy, real, he don't care which. If it matches the book, it's the real one as far as he's concerned, and he'll pay what the insurers would have.

DANNY: It don't match the book.

PEG: He flies home Friday.

TESS: So it doesn't match by Friday.

PEG: So we make it match.

DANNY: How do you turn a picture round?

PEG: Bank hung a copy the wrong way round. My man wants it the right way round. So we hop it till it turns.

TESS: Could take a hundred goes.

PEG: Then it takes a hundred. You face home when you cross, don't you? A picture doesn't. It's got nothing to face with. You hold it loose, you let it roll about, and one of these goes it comes back the right way.

DANNY: She's got a face, Mum. She's looking out the window.

PEG: Wrong window.

Laughter round the table. At the stove Terry laughs along with them and turns the bacon.

ROSIE: I'll do it.

She says it before Peg has finished looking round the table.

SHAUN: I'll do it. I've got eight seconds.

TESS: *(not looking up)* Eight point four.

ROSIE: It's my picture. I carried her out, I'll carry her round.

LIAM: I could do it.

PEG: Not with that breakfast in you, love.

The table laughs again, at the big lad. Liam laughs too, a beat late. Then he picks his fork back up and eats every bite on his plate, slowly, looking at nobody. Under the table Sixpence waits on him and gets nothing.

Tess is looking at Rosie. Properly looking: the grey under her eyes, her wrists in the big sleeves.

TESS: Three seconds.

PEG: Three?

DANNY: That's a surgeon's hop.

TESS: Three. In, count, face home, out. Not a tenth more. And a meal between every one. Look at her.

ROSIE: Three's lovely. I'll be a surgeon.

TESS: You're grey.

ROSIE: It's March. Everyone's grey.

PEG: Not on my bridge, mind. I'm not having forty tickets of a picture on my roll. Do it by the stove.

She pins the catalogue cutting on the wall beside the stove, at eye height, and smooths it flat with her palm. Then she looks at the clock. Two minutes to seven.

PEG: Put her back.

Danny posts the painting into the safe the way it came, and his hand comes out empty. Peg unbolts the door and slides the hatch up. MICK, 50s, lorry driver, hi-vis, leans in for his ticket.

MICK: Every vault in the City went off in the night, Peg. Beads to the ceiling. Radio says.

PEG: Then they'll be wanting floors.

She hands his ticket through with a bacon roll. Behind her, on the table, Danny's phone starts to ring.

### Scene 5. A bank on Cheapside, Tuesday, 11:00.

Underground. The strongroom from the cold open, its door open now. Grey beads lie knee-deep across the floor; men in masks work industrial hoovers that scream and cough. Two insurers in good coats stand in the corridor on their phones, both saying the same words: "by the summer".

A FACILITIES MANAGER in a lanyard and a suit that has had a bad morning shakes Danny's hand. Behind Danny, Liam holds a tube of drawings in both arms like something that might break.

FACILITIES MANAGER: Every wall in the City went off at once. With nobody in the vault. Insurers say if the walls can be tripped from — wherever that was —

DANNY: Next door.

FACILITIES MANAGER: — then a wall isn't enough. They want the floor done. Thicker. Before they'll renew.

DANNY: A wall's a tripwire. Tripwires go off. A floor doesn't go off. A floor's just there, being thick.

He crouches, takes a handful of beads and lets them run through his fingers.

DANNY: I know what slows a crosser down. Show him, Li.

Liam, surprised to be asked, unrolls a drawing on top of a filing cabinet: a section through a vault floor, concrete in layers with grey dots thickening towards one edge.

LIAM: Beads in the pour. Not even, though. Thickest at the back. That's where they'd come in, off the lane. Every bit of that a crosser's got to be in the black for. And you can't see the beads till you're in it.

FACILITIES MANAGER: You sound like you've done it.

DANNY: We pour it. Same thing.

The manager laughs. Danny doesn't.

FACILITIES MANAGER: Who else have you done?

DANNY: Half this street. Did a vault further down in '24, they had us back last autumn for the floor underneath. Two floors to get through, not one. That's a long time to be nowhere.

The manager signs. Liam rolls the drawing up again. His ears are pink. He has been praised for his head, not his size, and he doesn't know where to put it.

### Scene 6. The weighbridge office, Tuesday, evening.

Danny's phone buzzes on the table between the ketchup and the teapot and doesn't stop. He answers it, writes on the back of his hand, rings off; it goes again. Peg has the order book open. Every page this week is full, and she's started on next month.

DANNY: Banks. Banks I've never heard of. One wants Marlow Mix in a basement that hasn't got a vault in it yet.

PEG: Three times what we had on Monday.

She shuts the order book with satisfaction.

PEG: Every job just got a second longer. Good for us both ways. We sell them the floor. And who's light enough to get through it?

She looks at the chalk board. So does everyone. Rosie's name at the top.

TESS: A second's a lot.

PEG: Then find it.

TESS: I'll find it.

She says it the way she says everything about numbers: as if it's already done.

Later. The family has gone up. Tess is last, turning off lights, and she stops at Frank's photo: the big man in his donkey jacket, laughing at whoever held the camera.

TESS: That copper said he met Dad.

Peg, at the sink.

PEG: Your dad never met a copper in his life. Too quick for them.

TESS: He said he met what was left of him.

PEG: There's nothing left when the black takes you. You know that. Don't you let that man in your head.

Peg turns off the tap. The kitchen is dark except for the small green light on the printer, and the safe stands at the end of the plate with the painting inside it.

## Act two: seven hops

### Scene 7. The weighbridge office, Wednesday, 05:30. Hop one.

Before the gate opens. Blinds down. The yard lights make orange bars on the lino.

Rosie is in her rig: the padded belt, the collar, the wrist unit on her left arm. She stands beside the little bridge, not on it, leans to the safe and puts her hand into its door up to the wrist, the way Danny does. Her eyes go to the ceiling while her fingers feel about inside, blind. She draws the painting out through the steel.

She takes it to the lino by the stove and holds it against her chest in both arms, loosely, the way Peg showed her: "like a tray of hot tea". Danny, in his work jacket, sits on the table's edge. Peg stands by the safe. Tess has her eyes on the wall clock. Terry is at the stove with the kettle, which is always on.

TESS: Three. Face home. Out.

ROSIE: Yes, miss.

She presses the wrist unit.

*The black.* Sound drops to breath and heartbeat. There is no floor. Her field glows round her, a soft circle the width of her arms, and in it, and only in it, everything is open. The painting is not a picture any more but a thing with an inside: the weave of the linen like a ploughed field; the paint lying on it in layers, like the faces of a gravel pit; and under the woman's hand on the balance, a second hand, an earlier one, painted out centuries ago. Rosie looks at it. Beyond her glow there is nothing at all.

The wrist unit counts tenths. 2.1. 2.2. She feels for the tug of home and faces it, the way you roll over in bed towards a warm back.

3.0. She steps.

*The office.* A gasp. The smell of bacon fat and the kettle. Rosie on the lino, the painting in her arms.

DANNY: Straight?

ROSIE: Straight.

Everyone leans in over the painting. The window is on the right.

Rosie's eyes go past them, to the catalogue cutting on the wall. She looks at it for exactly as long as it takes to read a line of print. Then she looks at the painting like everyone else.

PEG: Coin didn't come up.

DANNY: First go.

PEG: Again after dinner.

Under the table, Sixpence growls at Rosie, a low steady note.

DANNY: Still jealous, is he? She's only been gone three seconds.

Rosie posts the painting back into the safe, and her hand comes out empty.

### Scene 8. The weighbridge office, Wednesday noon to Thursday dawn. Hops two to four.

Each time, the painting comes out of the safe through the door, and goes back into it after.

**Hop two. Wednesday, 13:10.** The door bolted, the hatch down, a card in it: BACK AT 2. Tess puts a plate of dinner in front of Rosie and watches every forkful go down. Then Rosie by the stove, the painting loose in her arms, the wrist unit. Tess counts aloud for the room, "...two... three", though nobody in the black can hear a word of it. The gasp. Peg this time:

PEG: Straight?

ROSIE: Straight.

The window on the right. Rosie's eyes to the cutting, and back.

**Hop three. Wednesday, 22:00.** After tea. Danny back from a pour, grey dust on his eyebrows, eating a pie standing up.

DANNY: Straight?

ROSIE: Straight.

Right. The cutting. Back.

DANNY: Bloody thing's facing home better than we do.

**Late Wednesday, the bathroom.** The house asleep. Liam, in a T-shirt and shorts, hooks the bathroom scale out from under the basin with his toe, exactly as Rosie did. He steps on. The display lights blue under his chin. We don't see the number. He breathes in and holds his stomach in, and looks again. A board creaks on the landing. He steps off and slides the scale back with his foot, slow, so it won't scrape.

**Hop four. Thursday, 05:30.** Blinds down. Tess makes Rosie eat a slice of toast first, standing up. Rosie is slower into her rig; Tess does the collar clip for her.

TESS: Three.

ROSIE: I know.

She goes. The empty place by the stove.

TESS: ...one...

Headlights sweep across the blind. The printer chatters.

Every head in the room goes to the window. Nobody's due. Danny is at the blind in two strides, one finger on a slat. Peg's hand goes flat on the tea towel on the table, though there's nothing under it to hide. Tess's lips keep moving.

TESS: ...two...

The black, briefly, for Rosie alone: the woman's face in the glow, the earlier hand under the paint, her own breath. Home.

*The office.* The gasp. Rosie is back by the stove with the painting in her arms, and nobody is looking at her. They are all looking at Danny's back.

PEG: *(without turning round)* Straight?

ROSIE: Straight.

DANNY: Tipper. Early. Driver's asleep on his wheel.

The room breathes out.

PEG: If it's ever that copper, it's a picture of a lady off Rayleigh boot fair.

Then they look at the painting. The window on the right. Rosie's eyes go to the cutting, and back, and nobody sees her do it.

### Scene 9. The weighbridge office, continuous.

Nobody moves for a second. Four goes, and the woman is still looking out of the wrong window.

PEG: Coin's got to come up sometime.

Terry is drying a pan in his gloves. He says it to the pan.

TERRY: *(gently)* The black never gave me back.

Peg's hand stops on the tea caddy.

DANNY: It's a picture, Tel.

TERRY: I know, love.

He hangs the pan up. He opens his fridge, takes out a white tub, and eats standing, a spoon at a time, with a nip of gin beside it. Rosie posts the painting back into the safe.

Peg starts the fry-up. It isn't yet six, and the lorries come at seven.

### Scene 10. The weighbridge office, Thursday, 08:15.

Open for business. A queue of tippers on the big bridge outside, engines ticking. Danny's new orders have the yard full, and Peg has put Rosie on the hatch for the day. Peg loaded a new paper roll into the printer first thing, and the printer is going like a till.

Rosie is behind the counter, doing the tickets. She's quick and she's funny with the drivers, and she's greyer than she was on Tuesday. Mick's lorry settles on the big bridge; the printer chatters; Rosie tears off his ticket and holds it out through the hatch.

ROSIE: New roll's printed backwards, Nan.

Tess, at the table with the job diary she keeps in her head, looks up sharp and half rises.

TESS: Show me.

Rosie sees her face and laughs.

ROSIE: Joke.

TESS: Not funny. That roll's certified.

ROSIE: Bit funny.

MICK: *(taking his ticket)* You want your eyes testing, girl.

ROSIE: I want a lot of things, Mick.

The camera sees the ticket as Mick folds it into his cab: ordinary purple print, the right way round, MARLOW AGGREGATES along the top.

Nobody checks. Tess sits down and goes back to the numbers in her head. Peg tears off the next ticket herself.

## Act three: the other bank

### Scene 11. Kieran's bedroom, Thursday, 12:30.

Kieran is off school with a cold: red nose, a box of tissues, a hot-water bottle, his log open on the duvet.

Terry comes in with a bowl of soup on a tray, gloves on, the way he always handles food.

TERRY: Chicken. Your nan's. I only stirred it.

KIERAN: *(thick)* Will you catch it?

TERRY: Me? Not had a cold since '21. Only perk.

KIERAN: What are the other perks?

TERRY: That's the list, love. That's all of it.

He tucks the hot-water bottle in and goes. Rosie, on her dinner break from the hatch, comes and sits on the end of the bed without being asked, the way she always has, and tucks her feet under the duvet by his.

ROSIE: Budge up. Look.

She holds out her phone. A video: an operating theatre, bright and green. A child asleep on the table, maybe six. A SURGEON, masked and gowned, a rig's collar over her gown, stands beside him. A voice counts. The surgeon is there; then she isn't, for three seconds, just an empty space by the table with the nurses watching it; then she's back, and in her gloved palm is something the size of a walnut. The child hasn't moved. There isn't a mark on him.

KIERAN: Did it hurt him?

ROSIE: He never even woke up. She can see all of him in there, Kiz. Every bit. Heart going, blood going. And she knows which bit's wrong, and she takes it out, and comes home.

KIERAN: Does she ever turn?

ROSIE: Never. Not once. Four thousand of them.

She plays another.

KIERAN: You could do that.

ROSIE: In my sleep.

KIERAN: So why don't you?

ROSIE: They want exams. Certificates. Letters after your name. Paper.

KIERAN: You could do exams.

ROSIE: Marlows don't do paper.

She says it lightly, a joke everyone knows, and plays him another one.

### Scene 12. The weighbridge office, Thursday, 18:30.

Terry cooks the family's tea, sausages and mash, in his gloves, with the cordless phone clamped between ear and shoulder. He can't taste it properly; he goes by colour and smell and the clock. A pan of his own dripping gravy simmers at the back, for him.

TERRY: *(into the phone)* No, dripping first. Beef, if you can get it. Then the flour.

A voice on the line, faint and old, soft at the edges, from somewhere with a river in it. We can't make out the words.

TERRY: *(laughing)* Gin? In the gravy? You daft ha'porth. *(listens)* ...Go on, then. A drop.

Peg comes through with a basket of washing.

PEG: Who's that?

TERRY: Con.

PEG: Who's Con?

TERRY: Fella I talk to.

Peg rolls her eyes and goes. Terry tips a capful of gin into his gravy and stirs.

TERRY: Con says a drop of gin in it. *(to the phone)* I'm telling them you said so. *(listens; softer)* No, it doesn't taste of much. Nothing does. But it's warm.

Kieran is on the stairs in his dressing gown, blanket round his shoulders, listening.

### Scene 13. The weighbridge office, Thursday, 21:00. Hop five.

The hatch down, the door bolted. Kieran comes down to do his reading. Nobody is allowed to do it for him. He hauls the 20-kilo cast-iron test weight out from under the counter by its handle, which takes both hands and a lot of breath, and waddles it onto the middle of the little bridge. The display is dark; the printer chatters. He tears off the ticket and reads it.

KIERAN: Twenty point nought nought.

TESS: Same as yesterday.

KIERAN: Same as every day. That's the point.

He writes it in the log, tapes the ticket beside the others, and heaves the weight off the plate again. Tess, watching, doesn't help. She's proud of him and says nothing.

Rosie is rigged by the stove, the painting in her arms. Kieran sits on the bottom stair, blanket round him, to watch a real hop.

TESS: Three.

Rosie goes. The empty place by the stove. Tess counting. Kieran holding his breath without knowing it.

The gasp. Rosie's back, the painting in her arms, and before anyone else can speak:

KIERAN: Straight?

He says it exactly like Danny, man to man. Rosie grins at him.

ROSIE: Straight.

The window is on the right. Rosie's eyes go to the cutting. Five.

### Scene 14. The office step, Thursday, 21:30.

The yard floodlight, fog coming off the river in slow sheets, a ship's horn somewhere towards Tilbury. Tess and Rosie sit on the office step with mugs of tea. Rosie holds hers in both hands for the warmth.

TESS: Five.

ROSIE: Five.

TESS: Two more, and if she's still wrong we're another job short.

ROSIE: Then I'll do the job.

TESS: You'll do nothing till you've got some colour.

A long quiet. The horn again.

ROSIE: Were you scared? First time?

TESS: Fourteen, I was. The week your grandad went, Nan stood me on that plate and everybody clapped.

ROSIE: I know. Nan tells it at Christmas.

TESS: Nan tells it wrong. She says I was proud.

ROSIE: Weren't you?

TESS: I was hungry.

Rosie says nothing at all. Tess puts an arm round her, the way she has since Rosie was small, and feels how little there is of her under the big jumper. Her face does two things at once, pride and worry, and she keeps both of them to herself.

TESS: Drink your tea.

Rosie drinks, and leans into her.

### Scene 15. A tug-yard shed, a river somewhere, the same night.

Thirty seconds of a place we can't put a name to. Corrugated iron, rain loud on it. A tug's lights through the open doors, rocking on black water.

From a beam hangs an old salvage steelyard: a long iron arm, a hook, and from the hook, on four chains, a wooden board. A skinny LAD of seventeen stands on the board, holding the chains, grinning. An OLD MAN in a cap, seventy, slides the brass weight along the arm, a notch, another, until the arm floats level.

OLD MAN: Fifty-nine!

The shed erupts. A GIRL of thirteen bangs a saucepan with a spoon. Men whoop. The lad punches the air and the board swings.

A WOMAN in her forties, with a waterman's tide book under her arm, stands by the doors and doesn't cheer. She looks at the lad's thin arms. Somewhere overhead, a baby starts to cry. She turns her head towards it, and goes up the stairs.

### Scene 16. The weighbridge office, Friday. Hops six and seven.

**Hop six. Friday, 05:30.** Toast first, standing up, with Tess watching every bite. Three seconds by the stove. Rosie comes back from the black and sits down hard on the bottom stair with the painting in her lap. "Straight?" "Straight." The window on the right. The cutting. Six.

Tess crouches in front of her.

TESS: That's it. Done.

ROSIE: One more.

TESS: You're white as that tub.

She unwraps a glucose sweet from her pocket, the kind Peg keeps everywhere, and puts it in Rosie's hand.

TESS: Sugar. Go on.

Rosie crunches it.

ROSIE: Better. See?

TESS: I'm serious.

ROSIE: So am I. One more. This afternoon, before his plane. For Terry's year.

Tess looks at Peg. Peg looks at the order book, and at the till.

PEG: Let her, Tess.

**The day between.** Mixers turning in the yard. The phone ringing with banks. At four the phone rings with Peg's man, and Peg takes it on the step with her back to the kitchen.

PEG: *(on the phone)* He's still here till seven? ...Then tell him to wait by his phone.

**Hop seven. Friday, 16:30.** The hatch down early. All of them in the office now: Peg, Danny, Tess, Terry, Liam, Shaun, Kieran in his dressing gown.

Rosie in her rig. The painting against her chest.

TESS: Three. Not a tenth more.

ROSIE: Not a tenth.

*The black.* The glow. The linen, the layers, the woman at the wrong window, the painted-out hand. Rosie brings her face close to the woman's.

ROSIE: *(a whisper, to her)* Come on. Come round.

The count. 2.8. 2.9. She faces home.

*The office.* The gasp.

DANNY: Straight?

ROSIE: Straight.

Everyone leans in. The window is on the right.

Rosie's eyes go to the cutting on the wall. She looks at it longer this time. Then she looks at the painting and makes a face, a comic, sorry face, for the room.

ROSIE: She likes it that way.

Laughter, thin. Peg's phone rings. She listens. She says "Right," and "Thanks, Stan," and puts it down on the table.

PEG: He's gone to the airport. *(beat)* Put her away.

Rosie, still rigged, posts the painting into the safe. Her hand comes back empty.

### Scene 17. The office step, Friday, 18:55.

Dusk over the estuary, the water gone to pewter, the bridge lights coming on across the river. The family on the office step with their tea. A plane climbs out over the water, heading east, its lights blinking.

DANNY: That's him.

PEG: You don't know that.

DANNY: It's a plane, Mum. He's on a plane.

TERRY: Bon voyage.

Nobody laughs. They watch it go until it's a light among the others.

Rosie puts her mug down on the step.

ROSIE: Right. If we're not getting your year, I'm pricing your present.

TERRY: You what?

She has her phone out already, thumbing through it: OBVERSE, HARLEY STREET. She dials. The family watches her the way you watch someone do a dare.

ROSIE: *(into the phone, posh)* Good evening. I'd like to price a Restoration. For my uncle. *(listens)* Terry Marlow. It's for his birthday.

TERRY: Tell them I'm shy.

ROSIE: *(into the phone)* He's shy. *(listens)* Tomorrow's lovely. Ten's lovely.

The step laughs, properly this time. Danny slaps his knee. Rosie rings off and takes a bow sitting down.

PEG: Don't you sign nothing.

TERRY: Get me the Christmas one and all.

## Act four: Harley Street

### Scene 18. The Obverse clinic, Harley Street, Saturday, 09:55.

A Georgian door, black, with a brass plate: OBVERSE. RESTORATION. Rosie, in her good coat, goes in. Inside, white walls, orchids, the hush of money.

Framed on the wall of the hall: a menu, cream card, gold edges, THE OBVERSE ROOM, KNIGHTSBRIDGE, 2009, TASTING MENU £800. Beside it, a framed front page of a financial paper: OBVERSE TRIPLES ON ARMY DEAL. And a large portrait, softly lit: a woman in a white coat, calm, direct, and under it, I WAS CURED HERE. DR HELEN OSEI.

On the door to the inner corridor, a printed sign, very plain:

PATIENTS MUST NEVER TOUCH STAFF IN TRANSIT.

The RECEPTIONIST, thirties, immaculate, smiles and types with her right hand. Her left hand rests on the desk beside the keyboard, very still, its colour almost right. It's a good one. Rosie's eyes go to it and away. Nobody mentions it.

RECEPTIONIST: Name of the patient?

ROSIE: Terry Marlow. He's my uncle. It's for his birthday.

RECEPTIONIST: Will he be joining us?

ROSIE: He's shy.

RECEPTIONIST: Take a seat. Dr Osei won't be long.

The waiting room is empty. Rosie sits on the edge of a leather chair with her hands between her knees.

### Scene 19. Dr Osei's consulting room, Saturday, 10:05.

A tall window onto Harley Street. A desk with nothing on it but a leaflet and a pen. DR HELEN OSEI, the woman from the portrait, older in person and kinder.

OSEI: Your uncle.

ROSIE: Terry. He's sixty-six. He turned five years ago. On a job. *(beat)* At work.

OSEI: Is he on the Register?

ROSIE: We feed our own.

Osei lets that sit. She has heard it before, and she knows how it usually ends.

OSEI: He'd come in in the morning and go home in the afternoon. A clinician looks in on him in transit, a second or two at a time. Nobody touches him. And seven times in ten, he goes home and eats his tea.

ROSIE: But what do you do to him?

Osei smiles. She has been asked this a thousand times.

OSEI: That's what the fee pays for.

ROSIE: And if he's one of the other three?

OSEI: He can have another go. At the same fee.

ROSIE: You had it done. It says in the hall.

OSEI: I sat in that chair once. Hungry. It doesn't hurt. I can tell him that much.

ROSIE: How much?

OSEI: A hundred and eighty thousand pounds. Seventy per cent.

Rosie nods as if that's the kind of number she hears every day. She takes the leaflet. Osei watches her fold it in half, and in half again.

OSEI: If he's hungry now, don't make him wait for the money. The Register won't ask him anything.

ROSIE: We don't go to them.

OSEI: Everybody says that. Until they're hungry.

### Scene 20. The Obverse waiting room, Saturday, 10:20.

Rosie comes back through the door into the waiting room, the folded leaflet in her fist, and stops dead.

In the chair by the window, in a long overcoat that is too big for him, sits DS RAY AMBROSE, 54. Thin as a rake handle. On his knee a packet of cream crackers; in his hand a pocket knife, spreading white lard on one. On the low table beside him, face up, a green card with his photograph on it: HANDEDNESS REGISTER. CLAIMANT.

He finishes spreading. He looks up.

RAY: Miss Marlow.

ROSIE: You following me?

RAY: I have an appointment. I come every spring. To see if it's come down.

ROSIE: Has it?

RAY: Not yet.

He eats the cracker, neatly, catching the crumbs in his palm. Then he tilts the packet towards her, the way anyone offers anyone a biscuit.

RAY: Want one? Don't knock it.

ROSIE: You're all right. Making weight.

RAY: So I hear.

He doesn't put the card away. Rosie looks at the card, and at him, and at the lard on the knife.

RECEPTIONIST: *(from the desk)* Mr Ambrose? Dr Osei will see you now.

RAY: In a minute.

Rosie goes.

### Scene 21. Harley Street, outside the clinic, Saturday, 10:25.

Bright, cold. Taxis. Rosie on the clinic steps, the leaflet in her pocket, saying it to herself as though saying it might make it smaller.

ROSIE: A hundred and eighty thousand. Seventy per cent.

Across the road, on the corner, a jeweller's: a narrow shopfront, one ring in the window on black velvet. Beside it, a bus shelter with a poster in it: a young soldier in fatigues, well fed and smiling, walking unmasked down a plague ward between the beds, nurses behind him in full hoods and visors. IMMUNE. FED. NEEDED. JOIN THE FEVER COMPANY.

At the kerb between the shop and the shelter stands a plain grey van with its back doors open. Two GUARDS come out of the jeweller's carrying a steel case between them and lock it into the van.

Rosie watches the case all the way in. She doesn't look at the poster. The camera holds all three in one frame: the girl on the steps, the van, the soldier on the ward.

Behind her, the clinic door opens. Ray, in his big coat, on the top step.

RAY: Tell your uncle it's seventy per cent.

She turns.

RAY: They don't tell you how.

He goes back in. The door closes on the brass plate.

Rosie looks down the street. The grey van's brake lights at the corner, turning left. She pulls up her collar and walks after it, quick, and her lips are moving. She is saying its number plate over to herself, the way Tess keeps a count.

**END OF EPISODE 2**

## Plants and payoffs

**Planted here, for later episodes**

- **Kieran's log (scenes 3, 13).** The Twitch ticket (04:17, 0.31 kg, empty plate), found because the radio's "next-door event" sends him to the night's tickets before school instead of after, and taped in under the poster his school printed ("found by weighing, never by looking"; "Sixty years we pointed dishes the wrong way"; "Miss says you can't look at them, you can only weigh them"). The test weight at 20.00 every night at nine, read off the ticket because the display is dark: "Same as every day. That's the point." In 4 it reads 19.78 and Peg calls it chipped; in 9 the log proves the April step. The Twitch itself stays a wonder.
- **Peg's faith in the little bridge:** "Bathroom scale's a liar. On the bridge." And her hatred of wasting roll ("that's my roll"; "forty tickets of a picture"), which becomes "Stop wasting roll" in 4. The bridge lies from 4 April (4, 9).
- **43.1, cheered, chalked and starred**, with a 43.10 ticket on the audit roll (the twist in 3; the rolls in 10).
- **Peg's copy theory and her home-made belief about cargo.** "Bank hung a copy the wrong way round... A picture doesn't [face home]. It's got nothing to face with. You hold it loose, you let it roll about." It is Peg's own idea, made up at the table because she needs a sale; the table laughs and nobody tests it. It keeps "copy or original" open until 3, where Peg concedes "That picture was never a copy" and Ray's "so did whoever carried it" breaks the belief. Tess shows she has learned in 4 ("Then you'd be eating it"); Kieran writes "Food turns too" in 9. *Continuity:* nobody in the family states the cargo rule before 3. Episode 1's "Means we came back straight" about the burned tissue is as close as they come, and nobody puts the two together.
- **Seven three-second hops, all failed.** Each gets "Straight?" "Straight." and exactly one glance from Rosie at the catalogue cutting by the stove (re-read in 3; run through the drift table in 6). Kieran asks "Straight?" once, man to man. At 1 in 5,000 a hop, seven failures are what the table predicts.
- **Tess's rule, "And a meal between every one."** It spaces the hops at mealtimes (that's why there are only seven), and she watches Rosie eat every forkful: dinner before hop two, toast before hops four and six. On re-watch the meals did nothing, like the glucose sweet ("Better. See?"), which sets up the gel that does nothing in 3.
- **The false alarm (hop four).** Headlights on the blind and the printer chattering mid-hop: nobody's due, and the whole room goes to the window, remembering that the printer announced Ray's car in 1. It is a real lorry the family can see, not an unseen visitor. Peg: "If it's ever that copper, it's a picture of a lady off Rayleigh boot fair." Peg asks "Straight?" without turning round, and nobody sees Rosie's glance. It puts the police in the room for a first-time viewer without giving Rosie away.
- **Rosie's hiding, kept to the plan's quiet tells:** the seven glances; the roll "joke" (Tess's alarm is about her certified roll and Pinnock, not about Rosie); the sweet; "Making weight" to Ray; "She likes it that way"; Sixpence growling after hop one ("Still jealous, is he?"). The call to Obverse is made openly, on the step, as the birthday joke promised in 1, and Terry's "Tell them I'm shy" pays off at the clinic's front desk. Peg's "Don't you sign nothing" lets the family's rules against clinics and paper sit over the joke without anyone stopping it (3: "We don't go to them").
- **Tess on the step:** "Nan tells it wrong. She says I was proud." / "I was hungry." Her wound, which Rosie is living; followed at once by the Ahern shed cheering a thin boy (6: "I gave you nine"; 10).
- **The doom's first link, as a boast:** "I know what slows a crosser down"; the insurers' thicker floors; the order book trebled; Peg's "Every job just got a second longer. Good for us both ways"; Tess's "I'll find it" (3, 7). Danny's new orders fill the yard, which is why Thursday has only two hops.
- **Danny's unnamed City vault, "'24, they had us back last autumn for the floor underneath."** It is Aoife's bank. Maggie names it in 7; the Marlow vans the Aherns saw in 4 were this crew.
- **Liam:** "Thickest at the back. That's where they'd come in, off the lane" (needed in 3, where Marrable Street is thickest on the mews side); praised for his head, not his size; alone on the bathroom scale in the dark, wanting to know his number (the diet starts only when Rosie coaches him in 4, and so does the fry-up he slips to Sixpence); Peg's "Not with that breakfast in you, love," and he eats every bite, which rhymes with the fry-up he eats in 5.

He always does." / "He'll get it." The supplier behaves like a hard man who wants paying, which is Ray's cover (8: the money he never touched).
