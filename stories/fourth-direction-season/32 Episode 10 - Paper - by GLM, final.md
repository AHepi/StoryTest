# Episode 10: Paper

*The family reads its own record for the first time, and must decide who goes where, and whether to burn the record or give it away.*

---

## Cold open

### Scene 1. The weighbridge office, dawn, spring 2002 — as Peg remembers it.

Golden light through blinds that haven't been cleaned in a decade. The smell of a fry-up. The printer, newer then, chatters like a till.

FRANK MARLOW takes up the whole of the little bridge. A big man, fifteen stone if he's an ounce, donkey jacket still on, laughing at something somebody said before we arrived. PEG, forty-eight, stands at the printer with the chalk. On the stair, in her socks, TESS, fourteen. In the doorway, DANNY, seventeen, already too wide for the frame.

FRANK: Mind, our Peg. Weigh me after me dinner and you can bill me as a lorry.

PEG: You'd eat the profit.

FRANK: I'd eat the plate an' all.

The ticket prints. Peg tears it off, holds it up to the lamp the way she does, and chalks it on the board without reading it out. Numbers like that don't get read out.

FRANK: *(swinging Tess down off the stair by one arm)* And you keep off that plate, our Tess. You're not for the black.

She shrieks with laughter, fifteen stone of uncle swinging her — and he laughs, laughs at whoever is holding the camera —

— and the laugh hangs on the office wall, framed, twenty-four years later. The same big man. The same laugh.

**MAIN TITLE: SECONDS**

## Act one: Frank

### Scene 2. The loft over the office, Saturday, eight in the morning.

Dust and paper. The boxes in their rows, 01 to 26, in Peg's handwriting. The one marked 02 stands where Tess left it in May, lid squared back on.

Peg climbs the ladder first, refusing the hand offered below. Tess comes behind, having slept on the settle. Terry last, slow, one rung at a time, and sits on the old trunk to get his breath. Kieran brings the torch. Rosie stays in the hatch, sitting on the top rung, in Danny's old jumper.

Tess unrolls the 2002 roll across the boards and pins it down with Peg's brass weights, one at each corner.

The job-day tickets run down it in purple ink gone lilac. September: FRANK 78.0. Beside it, in pencil, in Peg's own hand from twenty-four years ago: *12 st 4*.

Then week on week. 76.9. 74.4. 71.8. 69.0. 66.9. Kieran reads them out steady, like a lesson, because somebody has to. Nobody tells him to stop.

The new year. 66.0. 65.9. 65.0. 64.8. Then spring, and the last of them: 64.4 — and beside that one, in the same pencil, *10 st 2*, and under it, alone, *7.1*.

The torch hums. Terry looks down the column the way only a man who has lived one can.

TERRY: That's not a man going careful. That's a man that can't eat.

TESS: September. What was the job?

TERRY: Farringdon. Four seconds. *(beat)* Only one he did all year.

Tess's lips move. One in twelve. She looks down the whole year of it, week on week, and nobody in the loft says the word yet.

TESS: *(to nobody, flat as a ticket)* Forty-four. Forty-three six. Forty-three one. Forty-one eight.

It is Rosie's year, said out loud in a loft, and everyone in the loft knows it. Rosie says nothing.

### Scene 3. The weighbridge office, 2002 — as it was.

The same kitchen, the same light, wrong about one thing only: the man at the table is thin. Sleeves too big, the donkey jacket hung on him like a curtain. His hands are steady around a jam jar, turning the lid the wrong way, and the lid won't give, and he turns it the wrong way again, carefully, as if patience were the trick.

Peg takes it off him, laughing, turns it once the other way — pop — and laughs as she spreads the jam, and talks over her shoulder about the lorries.

Frank watches her laugh.

He says nothing at all.

Cut to Rosie in the loft hatch, sitting on the top rung, wearing exactly that look.

### Scene 4. The weighbridge office, Saturday, mid-morning.

The family is round the table — Peg, Tess, Terry, Danny with his bandaged hand flat in front of him, Rosie, Shaun on his stick, Kieran at the counter end — and at the open back door, in a coat too big for him, stands DS RAY AMBROSE, suspended, unshaved, holding a buff file.

He comes in when Peg steps aside to let him, which is its own event. He lays a yard of thermal paper on the table: line under line of print, a date, a time, a weight, and the weight a little smaller every line, down the whole strip, for a month.

RAY: Marrable Street. Spring of two thousand and two. The wall had him at sixty-four point three. *(beat)* Seven point one seconds in the black. At that weight, seven point one is every tenth he had.

Nobody in the kitchen moves.

PEG: Seven point one. *(beat)* That's my hand. I pencil'd it on his ticket. A tenth was always plenty.

RAY: At seven he was still at the tray. Starving men are slow.

PEG: Did he go quick?

RAY: Between one second and the next, Mrs Marlow. That's the one mercy in it.

He squares the strip on the table, an evidence man to the end, and looks at it a while instead of at them.

RAY: He'd have known, mind. The minute he looked at a ticket. They always know. *(He doesn't look at Rosie either.)* And I wrote it all down in a book and showed nobody. Twenty-four year. That's my bit of it.

### Scene 5. The dresser drawer, Saturday, eleven o'clock.

Peg hauls it open. It comes with its tearing noise, envelopes sagging. She digs down past the crowns and the window envelopes to the corner gone the colour of tea, typed MR F. MARLOW.

She opens it herself. One slit of the thumbnail.

She reads it aloud, standing, the way she reads tickets.

PEG: "Dear Mr Marlow. The Ministry invites anyone who has crossed to attend free handedness screening at Grays Town Hall. Places on the ration scheme are available to turned crossers." *(beat)* "Applicants will be interviewed by police."

She reads the last line again. Then she folds the letter along its old crease and puts it on the table where everyone can see it, and says the only arithmetic that's left.

PEG: He never saw this. I never let him.

At the table, under the brass weight, lies Frank's last ticket. 64.4. *10 st 2.* *7.1.* Peg picks up the pencil — the pencil that has lived behind her daughter's ear for twenty years — and beside her own old marks she writes, in the sign-writer's hand:

**FRANK. 10 ST 2. TURNED.**

PEG: There's your number, Frank.

Then she takes the duster and wipes the chalk board bare. 65.1 and its star. The PURFLEET sums. Every name and every number. The board hangs empty, and the chalk dust comes off on her apron.

### Scene 6. The weighbridge office, noon.

Two REGISTER INVESTIGATORS at the counter — a woman and a man, green lanyards, windcheaters, a clipboard between them. Not police. Something politer and worse.

INVESTIGATOR: Wednesday night, between midnight and one, the Purfleet depot's walls logged two bodies in the black. Both men. One of them nine times. The other twice — the second time carrying three kilos. Every reading to the hundred grams, cargo and all.

She looks at the bare board, and at her clipboard, and back.

INVESTIGATOR: We'd like the family's weights, Mrs Marlow. To match.

PEG: Board's being re-chalked.

INVESTIGATOR: *(mild, immovable)* The audit rolls, then. We'll be back Monday. With a warrant.

They go. The lorries come and go over the big bridge, and the printer prints their weights, and the family sits inside its own record with a day and a half of it left.

## Act two: The night of writing

### Scene 7. The weighbridge office, Saturday night.

The boxes are down. The rolls run the length of the long table and over the ends, pinned with brass weights, tea mugs, the sugar bowl. One lamp. The stove going. Kieran sits where the till usually is, with his own sharp school pencil, and his exercise book open to a fresh ruled page.

The rhythm finds itself, and it is Terry's, and Tess's, and it goes like this:

TERRY: Fenchurch Street. March, '09.

TESS: Six and a half.

KIERAN writes: name, seconds.

TERRY: Turned. Maureen.

And on. Romford. The grain job. The Old Street mess. Vic, who died of "his heart" in the March of '09. Lee, off his food till Canary Wharf and right as rain after. Names Kieran has never heard said at this table go into the book, and every line of it is a line the Register will read on Monday with a warrant, and they keep writing it anyway.

Terry names, Tess dates and seconds, Kieran writes, and Shaun, when they come to the river jobs, corrects Terry from the chair. By midnight Kieran has ruled a new graph and pinned it beside his old one on the wall: seconds along the bottom, ticks and crosses going up. The crosses crowd above the eight, exactly where the ferry timetable said they would, except now they have names under them, in pencil, on the certified roll.

### Scene 8. The weighbridge office, two in the morning.

Headlights swing across the blind. This time nobody tenses. The back door opens on MAGGIE AHERN with her tide books under her arm, and JOE behind her carrying the salvage steelyard wrapped in sacking, the long iron arm over his shoulder like a spade.

Joe loops its chains over the stove-flue bracket. The wooden board swings level. One at a time, in their socks, holding the chains, they stand on it, and Maggie slides the brass weight out the arm until it floats.

DANNY: Eighty-nine dead.

TERRY: Seventy-one four.

SHAUN: Fifty-two five.

RAY: Fifty-eight six.

ROSIE: Forty-six two. Same as Whitechapel had me.

Kieran steps up last. The board creaks.

KIERAN: Thirty-eight four.

He gets down, steps onto the little bridge, and tears his own ticket off the printer. 38.0. He writes them both, side by side — the first Marlow in twenty-five years with two numbers to his name, and he's twelve.

Then Tess does the thing she has never done. Down the roll, beside each turned name, in pencil, where the person can stand and read it, she writes the odds.

Terry finds his and reads it out loud, slow, a man reading his own menu.

TERRY: Here, a five-second hop: one in six, each go. There: five in six, better if I'm quick. A handshake: one in two, and a hand.

He holds up his yellow gloves.

TERRY: I cook with these.

He leans over and takes a die out of Kieran's board game box on the shelf. He holds it up where the table can see it.

TERRY: Six, I stay hungry. Anything else, I get my tea.

He rolls it once against the table, covers it with his palm, looks, and sweeps it into his breast pocket without showing a soul. Nobody asks.

TESS: Rosie did it in three. You'll take longer.

Peg takes the pencil off Kieran. Beside Terry's name, in the sign-writer's hand, she writes — and says it as she writes, at the table, out loud, the first time in five years:

PEG: Terry. Turned. Twenty twenty-one.

Terry goes very still. Peg doesn't look round at him.

TERRY: Ta, Peg.

A beat. Then, quiet, half under his breath, because it's what he does when there's nothing to say: "'Twas on a Tuesday morning..." — and he stops there, and fills the kettle.

Tess writes Ray's number on the roll. It sits there in pencil, and nobody in the room reads it out.

SHAUN: *(reading his own)* Nine and a half's my number. *(beat)* I've given it a leg. I'll wait till someone counts it for me.

Rosie reads hers — seven in ten, at her weight, at her seconds — and doesn't answer anybody. She goes and stands at the stove end, where Terry stands.

Tess's pencil hovers over Danny's name. There's no row of the table that reaches him. Two point two seconds, and nothing at two.

DANNY: Neither.

One word, and he puts his one good hand flat on the table, and that's the whole of Danny's arithmetic.

### Scene 9. The weighbridge office, four in the morning.

The lamp. The dregs of tea. On the wall, Kieran's two graphs. At the far end, the framed receipt Peg took down herself, lying flat on the table under her hands.

PEG: It came up wet, sand on it, and the creek dry when it should have been full. Your grandad said the tide had gone out and forgotten to tell the moon.

Maggie has her oldest tide book open. She has turned the pages back thirty-one years, to the week Peg names, and her finger sits on the Nobody column.

MAGGIE: Nobody low, that morning. To the minute. And your creek stood dry.

ROSIE: There's a sea under Grays. I swam up it.

Peg gets her baking tray out of the scullery. She heaps flour at one end of it — a white hill. Kieran props his school ruler level across the middle of the tray, a brass weight under each end, its near edge just biting the flank of the heap.

KIERAN: That's us. Our ground, dead level.

Peg runs water from the tap into the tray, slow, until the water stands well above the ruler's edge — and Rosie's hand stops her.

ROSIE: There. That deep over our lino. Eight metres. I counted it.

Then Maggie takes the tray, and tips it, slow, watching the water, the way she watches a river, letting it run off at the corner — and brings it level again, and the water lies dead along the ruler's top edge. Nobody low.

And on the flank of the flour hill, the wet edge comes down through the white and crosses the ruler's edge in exactly one place.

Peg sets the salt pot on it.

PEG: Our field. It never fell. It walked out of their sea onto our field.

Kieran writes it down. Tess reaches up, takes the pencil out from behind her ear — the one that has lived there for twenty years and never come down for anyone — and hands it to him. He pockets it.

KIERAN: Next Nobody low's tomorrow morning. Five fifty-two.

Quiet, around the table. Then Ray, into it:

RAY: I'd take mine counted. Before Monday.

TESS: Grays, then. Me, Kieran, Maggie.

PEG: Mucking's mine.

## Act three: 05:52

### Scene 10. The weighbridge office, Sunday, 05:40.

Dark except the printer's green light and the stove's. Tess, Kieran, Ray, Maggie. Joe asleep in the van outside.

Ray buckles on Danny's belt, the strap knotted short for a thinner man — Shaun's knot. He steps onto the little bridge. The printer chatters: 58.0.

TESS: Eight point four, off this plate.

He steps onto Maggie's board under the flue. The brass weight slides; the arm floats.

MAGGIE: Fifty-eight six. Eight three, true.

TESS: A month back we'd have set you off at eight four and called it margin. *(beat)* She still lies. We know it now, that's all. So it's Maggie's arm we go by, and we send you for seven five.

RAY: The odd tenths?

TESS: They're yours. Nobody in this family crosses to the last of their count again.

RAY: And the odds.

TESS: About four in ten you come back straight. Six in ten you stay as you are.

RAY: Better than the Met ever gave me. *(to Kieran)* Say the count for me, son.

KIERAN: Every tenth.

Ray flexes his fingers once, the way every Marlow does, and steps off the world.

He's gone. Under the counter the printer chatters once more, unprompted, and nobody looks at it. Hands still. Eyes on the empty plate.

### Scene 11. Mucking Marsh, Sunday, 05:20.

Fog off the estuary, thick as wool. A galvanized fence out of the fog, waist high, rolls of it, and a sign gone grey: MINISTRY OF DEFENCE — and a hut beyond with one dead sensor light on it. The marsh grass lies flat. Somewhere behind the fence the creek talks to itself, low, at the bottom of its bed.

Set into the mesh, incongruous, an old five-bar gate. Oak, silvered, a different century's work from the fence around it.

Peg takes her key ring out of her apron pocket. The long brass safe key, and the small padlock key on its loop of orange baler twine gone pale.

PEG: That's our gate. My dad hung it.

The small key turns in the orange padlock as if it were oiled yesterday. Thirty-one years, and nobody has changed the lock.

Peg steps through, onto the field. She stands still in the soaking grass with her hand on the gatepost, a woman on her own ground, on the wrong side of the law.

PEG: They never even put a sheep on it.

There's a standpipe on the hut wall, dripping. Rosie hangs her coat on the gate rail, and takes her clothes off in the grey light, folding them, quick and unembarrassed, a crosser packing a job down to weight. Peg works the soap over her back, her shoulders, her hair — backs to camera, and not one word between them. The water steams off her skin.

Then the breathing: the big slow divers' breaths, and one long out, and Rosie stands empty, holding nothing, her chest still.

Terry is next. He takes his clothes off like a man getting into a cold sea he has decided about, and Peg washes his back, and his hair, and neither of them speaks. A big soft man in the fog, shivering once, and done. His clothes go folded onto the rail. The yellow gloves go on top, folded together.

05:50.

### Scene 12. The black — Ray; the black — Rosie; the office; the field. Sunday, 05:52. The same minute.

**The black — Ray.** Sound drops to his heartbeat and his breath. The glow opens an arm's length round him, grey-gold, and in it the office stands open like a cutaway: the table's grain, the flue's old soot, and at the end of the plate the green safe — its steel gone clear as water, and through it, lying flat on its tea towel, a woman at a window with a little brass balance, the light falling from the wrong side.

He doesn't move. He counts. Somewhere above the world Kieran's voice isn't, and Tess's isn't, and he counts anyway, the way he has counted everything for twenty-four years, in a book nobody read.

**The field.** Rosie, wet-haired, empty-lunged, rigged in her aunt's old belt with the gaffer-taped strap, stands in the grass inside the field. She flexes her fingers once, the way every Marlow does — and does not look for home.

**The black — Rosie.** The tugs come, the way Terry taught a boy at a stove. Home, strongest, someone calling you in for your tea. Below her, soft and wide, the feather bed of the Deep. Far off behind everything, faint, a kettle two doors down. And east, hard, like leaning on a lorry: the Hill.

She has followed the Deep once. She has never followed this one. She hunts for it, patient, small pulls at nothing — two seconds, three — and finds it, and goes along it, and steps out.

**Black.** Nothing on the screen at all. Her held breath in her chest. Her heart. Cold air moving on wet skin, once, across her shoulders. No other sound. Nothing else, for a minute.

**The black — Ray.** 6.3. Close on his fingertips: the fizz starting under the nails.

He waits it out. Hands still. Feet still. Home is one pull, and he never left it.

7.5. He steps.

**The office.** A gasp. The smell of the room: cold bacon, printer ink, dog.

Nobody asks him anything.

Kieran tears the ticket off the printer — the one the plate printed while he was gone — and pins it under the brass weight by Ray's odds.

KIERAN: Minus fifty-eight six. That's you true.

Kieran holds out Peg's meter, unopened, on the flat of his hand.

**The field.** Rosie lands on her hands and knees in the grass, gasping ours. Sand on her feet, on her shins, under her nails. Wet grass, fog, the gate, her family.

DANNY: What did you see?

She doesn't answer. She gets up, walks off along the fence line into the fog, twenty yards, alone, and stops, and lets her last breath out — hers, only hers, into our air — and stands a moment breathing like a woman coming up from under a wave, and comes back.

Terry watches her feet the whole way. The sand. He nods once. That's the report.

ROSIE: Five and a bit, Uncle. Find it in four if you can.

TERRY: Aye.

Danny holds the gate open. Peg stands on her field with the soap still in her hand. Nobody says goodbye. Nobody reaches for anything, and nobody refuses anything.

Terry flexes his fingers once, the way every Marlow does.

TERRY: One.

**The office.** Ray doesn't take the meter yet. He takes the pencil instead, and writes his crossing onto the roll — the hour, the seconds — and under it, his name.

**RAY AMBROSE**, flowing, easy as breathing, every letter running right to left.

**The field.**

TERRY: Two.

And steps off the world.

There was a man, and then there was the field.

**The office.** Faces fall. Maggie's too. Kieran's hand with the meter in it drops an inch.

Ray laughs, once, and it's the driest sound in the world.

RAY: Eleven year I've wrote back to front so you lot could read me. Give us a fortnight.

Then he takes the meter off the boy, pricks his own finger, matter-of-fact, and squeezes the bead onto the strip, and they wait, all four of them, in the green light.

The beep. **5.1.**

MAGGIE: *(steady, first to recover)* Write it under your name, then. Both numbers.

He does. Kieran is already at the stove, in Terry's spot, turning bacon with the tongs. Ray sits down at the long table under the chalk board that hangs bare as a washed window, and eats the sandwich slowly, making it last, and nobody hurries him, because it is Sunday morning and the world has changed shape.

**The field.** The fog comes through the open gate and past them, and the grass stands empty to the creek, and nobody looks at any instrument, because there isn't one, and nobody has brought one, and that was the choice they made in the kitchen at four in the morning.

Rosie watches the empty grass a long time.

ROSIE: Seven in ten, it's Nan's fry-up for me on Friday. Three in ten, I'm still this. *(beat)* It's wrote down now. It'll keep. *(beat)* I'm staying as I am. Can't catch anything. There's wards that need that.

DANNY: I'm done making weight. I'll go to them.

Peg gathers his clothes off the rail. She folds the yellow gloves in on top, against his shirt, and carries the bundle out last, and the padlock clicks behind them, and the field is left to the Ministry and the fog and whatever is standing on the shore of it, one world over.

## Act four: What the rolls are

### Scene 13. The weighbridge office, Sunday afternoon.

Grey light. The lorries have been and gone. Ray went over the river at dinnertime to be in Victoria by nine, and the flat above the bookmaker's is his till Monday. Everyone else is here: Peg, Tess, Danny, Rosie, Shaun, Kieran, Maggie, Joe. The rolls lie the length of the table, written now, in pencil, in three hands — Terry's memory, Tess's seconds, Kieran's writing — and a fourth hand in square capitals down the far end, Maggie's tide books open beside it.

Tess stands at the head of the table, and she lays it out the only way she knows, which is objects.

TESS: Twenty-five year of tickets. Printed by the machine, on certified roll, dated to the day — crown stamp, Pinnock's certificate on the wall. *(beat)* Their walls have the same dates, and the seconds, to the hundred grams. The Register holds the day every turned soul in this country claimed. *(beat)* Lay the three together, and it's the ferry sheet with names on — and it's not our word for it. It's the banks' paper and the state's paper saying what Terry's memory says. Nobody can call it made up.

She picks up the 2002 roll, the one that came down from the loft, and lets it run through her hands.

TESS: And here's what only our paper's got. Every crosser that turned, and kept it hid, and kept on working — our rolls hold their year. Falling, week on week, like his. *(beat)* The Register can put a name to every one of them.

She turns over the one page that isn't theirs: Ray's strip, the month of Marrable Street going down.

TESS: The clinic's book is ash. This is all there is of it.

PEG: And the other edge of it.

TESS: Aye. *(flat)* Without the rolls, there's nothing to match Purfleet. Nor twenty-five year of anything. No tickets, no trial.

The stove ticks. Danny gets up, one hand doing the work of two, and opens the stove door. The firebox — never raked out since the night she burned them — holds a month of cold ash, and in the ash, curled edges: the ferry timetable's print, and the corner of a cloth binding.

DANNY: Burn them tonight. We lose the bridge. We stay free.

## Act five: The vote

### Scene 14. The weighbridge office, Sunday evening.

The same seats as every job night of the season. The stove door open on its cold ash. The boxes of rolls stacked ready, either for the van or the fire; nobody has said which. By the till, where he clerked all night, Kieran's exercise book lies open.

PEG: Round the table. Out loud, so we all hear it.

DANNY: Burn.

The word sits there. Danny looks at it — then down the table, to the open exercise book by the till. The second column. The one headed *me*. Thirty-nine two, thirty-eight nine, thirty-eight six, thirty-eight three, thirty-eight nought. A boy's careful pencil, going down all spring, in a house that never noticed.

Then at the boy.

DANNY: *(quietly)* No. Give.

KIERAN: Give.

ROSIE: Give.

SHAUN: Give.

Maggie doesn't say the word. She gathers her six tide books off the table, squares them, edges to edges, and lays them on top of the rolls. Then Joe puts his hand flat on them, once, and takes it away.

Peg is last. She looks at the open stove, at the certificate, at the photo of the big man laughing, at the bare board. Then she stands, and crosses the room, and shuts the stove door herself on the cold.

PEG: Give. *(beat)* Paper talks.

And she starts stacking the boxes herself, and nobody takes one off her.

### Scene 15. Grays police station, Sunday night.

Rain on the yard. The van at the front steps. Peg carries the boxes in, two at a time, seventy-two years old, and refuses the constable who comes to help. On top of the last box, flat and wrapped in its tea towel, lies a rectangle the size of a small window. At the van she set it there herself, saying so.

PEG: And this goes back on whatever wall it come off.

Inside, a DESK SERGEANT under strip lighting. The boxes go on the counter, and the tea-towel bundle on top. He lifts the bundle aside, gently, and unwraps a corner: a woman at a window, holding a little brass balance up to the light.

SERGEANT: And this is?

PEG: Stolen. Mayfair. March. *(beat)* Mind, her window's on the wrong side. She came back like that. She's staying like that.

Rosie stands in the doorway with her rig on her belt. The sergeant unclips it — belt, collar, wrist unit, the brass disc swinging on its strap — and writes her a property receipt on a pad. The second of her life. She folds it small, and puts it where the Obverse leaflet used to live.

Tess comes in last of all of them, first through the door. She stops at the counter, and holds out her wrists.

TESS: Twenty-five year of jobs on them rolls, and every one since I was fourteen was planned off my sums. Every number's mine. Start with me.

PEG: *(to the sergeant, of the boxes)* They're in order. *(beat)* And their sea went down my drain. Write that an' all.

He begins, unhurried, at the top of the first box.

## Epilogue. December

### Scene 16. The front page.

A train carriage, December dark outside. On a commuter's knee, a newspaper: **THE TEN-SECOND CURE — A GRAVEL FIRM'S RECORDS.** Inside, boxed for cutting out, the table: seconds down one side, odds down the other, printed for anyone. In a newsagent's window, a silent telly: OBVERSE SHARES HALVE. On the news, Dr Helen Osei, calm as ever, says one line — "That price fed forty thousand people." — and below it a second headline runs: REGISTER ASKS LEAVE TO OFFER CROSSING NEXT DOOR.

Over the headlines, a ward at the Royal Free. ROSIE, HAND: T on her badge, gowned and gloved for her patients' sake, walks unmasked between fever beds nobody else can walk between without a hood. A WARD SISTER passes behind her, laying a hand on the foot of a bed as she goes.

SISTER: Your file lives in our kitchen now, love. You eat where you work.

A theatre. A rig's collar over her gown. A child asleep. The clock on the wall. She is there; then the space where she was, the nurses watching it; then she is back, and something small and dark drops into the steel dish a nurse holds out. Three seconds. Inside the limit she once called soft.

Her locker opens once, on the last shot of the scene: a water jug, and the Fever Company card, still standing where she left it in June.

### Scene 17. Visiting day.

A prison visiting room. Tess in grey turns a letter over: from Kieran, a graph drawn on the back in pencil. She reads it twice, then folds it and puts it inside her shirt.

A Register ward, quiet. Danny, at a table, healed into a smooth pale plane across the knuckles of his one hand. No door on his room locked except the pantry's, down the corridor, and the little green light above it. On his table, an appointment card: LICENSED RESTORATION — MARCH. He turns it over. Sets it down. Turns it over again. He hasn't decided.

The same day, at both doors, Ray — charged with misconduct, waiting like everybody else for his hearing — signs both visitors' books, in plain, unhurried writing.

### Scene 18. Breakfast at the bridge.

Frost on the gravel heaps. Peg — out on bail for the depot, and for putting the other hand down a Grays drain — serves Kieran his fry-up, and waits while he eats every bite, and then points her spatula at the steel.

PEG: On the plate. Everything in you counts.

The printer chatters. She tears off the ticket and writes the number on the roll, and Kieran adds his pencil under the day's lorries: *checked against Maggie's.* Beside Frank's photo, newly framed, hangs Terry's last ticket — 70.6 — and under it, in Peg's hand: *71.4 on Maggie's. Went next door.* Sixpence, grey about the muzzle, lies where Terry's feet used to be at the stove.

Across the river, in the Ahern kitchen, Siobhan steps up onto a bathroom scale, hands up like a diver. Declan leans to the dial with his back to us, squints a long time, then straightens and chalks the board, and the room cheers. Under the scale, folded, lies December's front page, unread. Maggie stands in the doorway with the tide book under her arm, and does not cheer.

### Scene 19. The hook.

The office, evening. The radio talks into the warm dark.

RADIO: ...the gravity group at Reading say the estuary tremors which began in November spell a second molecule. This time, a sugar of our own hand — and the signal, they say, is fainter than the first.

The SCIENTIST, mild and astonished on a riverbank, a cylinder behind him: "It's ours. We don't know why."

Kieran, at the counter, writes one word on the roll: *fainter.* Peg, at the stove, looks up at Terry's ticket in its frame, a long time.

**Last shot:** the Grays outfall at Nobody low water, the whole estuary running quietly out to sea.

**Cut to black.**

**END OF EPISODE 10**
