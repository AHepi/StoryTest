# SECONDS: master season plan, version 1

*Ten episodes of about 55 minutes. Built from pitch A, with the fixes from the three pitch reviews, and with every invented law of The Fourth Direction made true and put to work. Every episode writer follows this plan. Where a number appears here, it is the number: do not round it, change it or add a new one. The canonical numbers are collected in section 14.*

**How the plan is laid out.** 1 Title and logline. 2 Genre. 3 The world. 4 The law ledger. 5 The show's own new guesses. 6 The culture that never questions, and its doom. 7 Characters. 8 The ten episodes. 9 Twist schedule. 10 Knowledge map. 11 Mysteries and wonders. 12 The ending. 13 Rules the physics must never break. 14 Notes for episode writers. 15 What changed from the pitch, and why.

## 1. Title and logline

**SECONDS**

**Logline.** An Essex family of thieves who can step through walls has one proud boast: Marlows always come back straight, never mirror-turned. Then their best crosser starts starving at the family table while they cheer every kilo she loses. The woman who keeps the family's numbers in her head must find out what is turning them. The answer is the seconds she has squeezed out for them. The only proof is the thing the family swore never to keep, paper. And the only cure anyone has missed for thirty-one years is one world over, under their feet.

The title means the seconds a crosser gets, second helpings at Peg's table, and a second go, the family's cure. Nobody ever says so.

## 2. Genre

**Lead: Crime.** The criminals are the heroes, inside a family firm. The pursuer looks like less than he is.

- **Promise, from the Crime profile.**
  - *Crimes worth watching:* the Mayfair dip, the Marrable Street diamond room, the hand through the Ahern cold store, the Obverse records room, the raid on the Register depot. Each shows a particular Marlow mind at work.
  - *A plain-looking master pursuer:* DS Ray Ambrose, thin, vodka at his desk, taken for a drunk.
  - *A long fight between equals:* the Marlows against Ray, and against the Aherns across the river.
  - *Deception on both sides:* Rosie hides her turn; Ray hides that he feeds the family; Maggie hides what Joe was doing; Peg hides, even from herself, what Frank weighed.
  - *The line crossed:* Ray feeds thieves on dead people's rations; Tess goes to "them"; Danny reaches for a boy's heart and chooses something slower.
  - *A reckoning paid in kind:* the family that kept nothing on paper is undone, and saved, by paper.
- **The guarantee broken: the family table is safe.** It breaks early and from inside. In episode 3 the table where Rosie is applauded turns out to be where she is starving. In episode 5 Peg's lucky fry-up sends Liam out half a kilo heavy. After that every meal carries a threat.
- **The genre's question:** what, if anything, would exempt someone from the rules everyone else keeps? The Marlows' unspoken answer is "People like us look after our own": no police, no clinics, no letters, no paper. The ending shows that the exemption was their cage.

**Guests.**
- **Gangster**, all season: the firm, the code at the table (Peg serves breakfast while Danny plans a war), a rival family, turncoats (Tess, then Ray), a successor (Kieran, with a pencil).
- **Detective:** Ray hunts a "spinner" who does not exist; Tess hunts the thing that turns her family. The clues are fair. The answer to "who is to blame?" is no one person, only habits.
- **Horror**, in single scenes: running out, the flat stump, the black water, the boy weighed a tenth of a second short.
- **Science Fiction:** one hinge technology, the rig, and its traffic jams, followed all the way out, including one world over.

## 3. The world

### 3.1 The normal

Grays, on the Thames estuary, in 2026. Spring into summer. Container cranes at Tilbury, the QEII bridge in the haze, lorries, gravel, gulls, the Nobody Tide slapping at pontoons.

Thirty-one years ago, in 1995, something arrived in a field on Mucking Marsh. It left no crater and no trail, and it was already flickering in and out of the world. The field was the Marlows' tenancy. The Ministry fenced the field, took the object, and paid Peg and Frank Marlow enough to buy a gravel pit and a public weighbridge in Grays. From the object came the **rig**: a padded belt, a collar with a small lamp, and a wrist unit. It lifts you off the world for a few seconds. Crossing is now a trade like plumbing: surgeons, rescue crews, couriers, and thieves.

Marlow Aggregates is a gravel pit, a concrete plant, and a public, certified weighbridge. The weighbridge office is Peg's kitchen. By day the family sells concrete to the City. By night they "dip": they cross into vaults and take things out.

### 3.2 What changed, and what that changed (departures and ripples)

**Departure 1: walls mean nothing, so weight means everything.**
- Gold sits under the City as dust in concrete. The concrete is often **Marlow Mix**, poured by Danny's day crew. Every year's bank pour adds seconds to the family's night jobs.
- Locksmiths became weightmen. Vaults have **weigh-walls**, which feel a body in the dark within four metres and flood the goods with tungsten beads.
- Jobs that took two seconds in Frank's day now take eight or nine.
- Police don't bother with cuffs on a crosser who has a rig. They take the rig.
- A thief's craft includes the knot and the seal: a crosser leaves a banded, knotted, lead-sealed crate exactly as it was, and empty.

**Departure 2: people come back turned.**
- About forty thousand Britons have come back mirror-turned, from surgery, rescue work or crime. Turned, a body gets nothing from ordinary food or medicine, and no ordinary germ can touch it. It starves in about six months unless fed mirror food.
- **The Register** (the Handedness Register) feeds the turned free mirror rations. Its rules exist because a turned body's own gut germs are now mirror life, which nothing here can recognise, digest or fight: Register members use sealed "Register loos", may not work with food or swim in public water, and are cremated. Since the Register Privacy Order of 2019, the police can no longer read the list. The Marlows don't know this. The letter is in Peg's drawer, unopened.
- **Passports** read HAND: C (common) or HAND: T (turned). Most countries refuse HAND: T travellers at the border, for the same mirror-life reason. Airports test every traveller with a sugar strip at the gate, because a photograph can't show handedness and a strip can.
- **Obverse plc** began in 2009 as the first mirror kitchen, a Knightsbridge restaurant at £800 a meal. Now it makes the Register's rations under contract, and the Army's rations for the **Hand Company**, turned soldiers who walk unmasked through plague wards. Its share price tripled on the Army contract. It sells a private cure, **Restoration**: £180,000, seventy per cent success, method confidential.
- **The Hand Convention** (2012) bans stepping out onto another leaf and bans carrying seed or living cultures through the black. It followed the Almaty affair of 2011, when a government sent twenty soldiers through the black carrying a rival's seed bank to turn it. The seed came back turned. So did most of the men.
- **The Looking Act** (2016) makes looking inside a body a search. It came out of *R v Adeyemi*: police lookers saw a ring in a man's stomach and could not see why he had swallowed it (it was his dead wife's, hidden from bailiffs). Ten years of courts followed. Now police need a warrant to look in your stomach, or at which side your heart is on.
- **Licensed crossers**, such as surgeons who lift tumours out of children without a cut, are held to three seconds "for safety". The trade thinks them soft.

**Departure 3: the neighbours, felt only as weight.**
- Everyone knows there is a world a hair's breadth away, because it can be weighed. Kieran's school poster says dark matter is "the neighbours' galaxies" and that SETI spent sixty years pointing the wrong way.
- The BBC weather reports "next-door events": a black-hole merger with nothing in our sky, a gravity blip over the North Sea.
- Thames watermen shrug at the **Nobody Tide**, a slosh on no moon's timetable. The Aherns, who run tugs, keep tables for it. It is the neighbours' moon.
- Crossers map the next world by its pull, in the dark: **the Hill**, a mountain felt over Mucking Marsh ("the Hill takes you": step out there and you are inside rock), and **the Deep**, a soft, wide pull under Grays. Nobody who followed the Deep has come back.

**The main departure carries the theme.** A world where your safety depends on one number you cannot feel (your seconds, your hand, your weight) is a world that punishes anyone who will not write that number down and check it against someone else's.

### 3.3 The rules of crossing, as the audience learns them (shown, never lectured)

- **The black.** Between worlds it is black. The only light is the lamp your field carries. Within arm's reach you see the inside of everything: the inside of a wall, a guard's ribs, a painting in its crate, a beating heart. Beyond that, nothing. Radio can't cross, so you are alone with your count.
- **The budget.** On a Marlow rig, every kilo under a hundred buys a fifth of a second. Everything inside your field counts: your cargo, the air in your lungs, the breakfast in your stomach. Your fingertips tingle as the time runs out. Stay past it and you don't come back: you become a weight in an empty room, slowly spreading.
- **The spare.** A rig can fire once more after its field closes: a shorter field, for getting back. "Always carry your spare."
- **The coin.** Any crossing can bring you back turned, and you can't feel it. What the odds are, and what changes them, nobody outside Obverse knows.
- **Facing home.** The only thing that tells you which way is home is the tug of home. Follow a different tug and you step out one world over.

### 3.4 Everyday surplus (taken for granted, never explained)

Phones have a "turned mode". The weighbridge ticket printer chatters like a till. Peg's chalk board of everyone's weight hangs beside Frank's one photo, a big man. Peg is diabetic and pricks her finger every morning; her glucose meter lives in the cutlery drawer. Peg's old brass shop balance, from the farm, weighs her baking. The dog, Sixpence, an old lurcher, has always growled at Terry. Dilys's launderette van comes on Tuesdays. The Register's white tubs have batch codes printed on the lids. Lorry drivers eat bacon rolls in Peg's kitchen while they wait for tickets. Estuary mud, fog, the Tilbury ferry. Posters on the tube: "IMMUNE. FED. NEEDED. Join the Hand Company." A crossers' saying: "The Hill takes you; the Deep keeps you." A minute's silence is held beside a lost crosser's weigh-wall reading, as the lost are mourned now.

## 5. The show's own new guesses

The Fourth Direction's own stipulations are all accepted (many leaves carrying worlds; the field holds atoms for seconds, longer for less mass; gravity felt at human scale; the neighbours' life is the other hand). On top of those, the show makes **five** guesses of its own. They are declared here once. No writer may add a sixth, on screen or off.

1. **Drift grows with time.** The chance of coming back turned rises with every second in the black, because the longer you tumble in the dark, the likelier you are facing the wrong way when the field closes. The table: 2 s, 1 in 200; 3 s, 1 in 50; 4 s, 1 in 12; 5 s, 1 in 6; 6 s, 1 in 4; 7 s, 1 in 3; 8 s, 1 in 2; 9 s, 3 in 5; 10 s, 7 in 10. (Revised from the pitch, whose 1 in 25 at two seconds would have turned a Marlow every year of the short-job era.)
2. **Budget.** On a Marlow rig, every kilo under a hundred buys a fifth of a second. Everything inside the field counts. Clinic rigs buy more; nobody outside Obverse knows how much.
3. **The grip, and the cargo.** When two crossers, each in their own field, hold each other in the black, one of the two always comes back turned relative to the other, and which one is a coin toss. Whatever rides inside one field (a painting, a crab, a breakfast) turns or slides with its carrier, always. So nobody can turn a thing without turning with it.
4. **Next door, here.** Under Grays the neighbours have a sea whose surface stands about eight metres above the weighbridge. Over Mucking Marsh they have a mountain rising out of that sea, an island: the Hill. Where the Hill's shore meets our ground level, at the Nobody Tide's low water, is the Marlows' old field. On 4 April the neighbours move the mountain, and Grays scales read 1.1% light from then on. (The source's own dial, applied here.)
5. **The spare.** After its field closes, a rig can fire once more, within a minute, for half its seconds. It is how a crosser who steps out in the wrong place can get back.

**Derived, with nothing new added.** Radio can't cross, so the crosser is alone. A mirrored canvas can't be a forgery. Glucose can't revive a turned body, and a straight glucose meter reads LO on turned blood, because its enzyme never crossed. Stepping out inside the neighbours' rock kills you ("the Hill takes you"); stepping out at the bottom of their sea drowns you ("the Deep keeps you"). Facing home keeps you on your own world but does nothing about turning. A turned reader sees print the right way round only in a mirror. A weighbridge measures weight, the pull on what stands on it, so a mountain moved next door makes it lie. A rig counts mass, a beam balance or steelyard compares mass, and a weigh-wall feels a body's own pull, so none of those three lies. Because cargo turns with its carrier, food can't be turned cheaply and mirror food must be built by chemistry. The step onto the neighbours' shore is itself a crossing, so it is itself a toss: a turned crosser who turns on the way arrives straight, starving there and a danger to them. The neighbours' sea rises and falls to the same moon that makes the Nobody Tide. To the neighbours, our life is the mirror life. A turned person who has trained herself to write so that straight people can read it will write in mirror script the day she is cured.

**On screen.** Guesses 2, 4 (the Hill and the Deep as lore) and 5 appear in episode 1. Guess 1 is glimpsed in episode 1 (surgeons held to three seconds; Terry's eleven failed hops) and stated in episode 6 (the protocol). Guess 3 appears in episode 1 (the painting turned with Rosie), episode 2 (the clinic sign), episode 4 (Tess on the Almaty seed) and episode 6 (Danny's grab). The Hill's move is episode 4; the field as a door is worked out on screen in episode 10 from things shown in episodes 1, 7 and 9.

## 6. The culture that never questions: the Marlows

The Marlows are warm, funny, loyal and admired in Grays. Their code is spoken, never written, and every rule was right once.

| Rule | Why it was right | How it grows the doom |
|---|---|---|
| **Nothing on paper** | Paper is evidence; its fibres even show police whether a thief turned | No pattern across years can be seen, so each turning feels like one person's bad luck |
| **Never go back twice** | In Frank's day a second visit walked thieves into a police stake-out, so a Marlow takes the whole haul in one trip | Since the odds of turning grow with time, one nine-second trip turns far more people than three short ones. Admired, and now lethal |
| **Make weight** | Lighter means more seconds; Peg made it law after Frank "ran out because he was fifteen stone" | Thinness is cheered, so a starving crosser looks like the most devoted one. It doesn't cause the turning; it hides it |
| **Lightest goes longest** | The lightest has seconds to spare | The best and youngest get the longest jobs |
| **Face home** | It keeps you on your own world; the lost followed other tugs and never came back | The family believes it also keeps you straight, so a turn means you failed and the turned hide it. And it kept every turned Marlow from ever finding what lay one world over |
| **We feed our own** | It kept the turned off a list the police could read | Each turned mouth costs £300 a day: more jobs, longer jobs, more turned. And since 2019 the police can't read the list anyway (the letter is in the drawer) |
| **Never go to them** | Clinics and, once, the Register reported crossers | It cuts them off from the one number that would save them, and from the letter that would have saved Liam |
| **Blood pays blood** | For twenty years it kept rivals away | A turning with an enemy nearby becomes a war |
| **Official letters go in the drawer** (Peg's own rule, never stated) | They were always bad news: the Ministry, the field, the bailiffs | Twenty-five years of unopened warnings |

**The doom, step by step.**
1. When jobs took two seconds (1998 to about 2010), almost nobody turned. Police forensic reports said "clean translation" and Peg framed the cuttings. "Marlows come back straight" became the family's pride. Frank, who turned on a four-second job in 2001, was the exception nobody wrote down.
2. Families like theirs got so good that the City moved to weight. Marlow Aggregates poured the bead-concrete floors. Every year's pour added seconds to the family's own jobs.
3. The family answered with its rules, more carefully and more lovingly: never go back twice, so each trip got longer; make weight, so each crosser got lighter; lightest goes longest. Tess grew proud of squeezing out tenths.
4. Long jobs turn people. Each turning read as a failure to face home, so the turned hid it (Frank, Rosie) or were fed at home (Terry), which meant more jobs.
5. With no paper, nobody saw that every turned Marlow had been on a long job. The family's cure, "go again" with short hops, never worked, and nobody counted.
6. One turned near a rival family (Aoife Ahern), and blood paid blood: Con, Shaun's leg, Liam.
7. Then the world changed again, one world over. The neighbours moved a mountain; the family's own scale began to lie; a letter saying so went into the drawer; a boy was sent out half a kilo heavy with no margin.
8. And all along the cure for the turned lay one world over, in the Deep and the Hill every Marlow had felt on every crossing for thirty-one years. "Face home" and "the Hill takes you" made it unthinkable.

**Why "always" is earned.** Frank shows the code was already killing its founder twenty-five years ago, hidden by a memory that turned. The Aherns, who keep the same code with one difference (Maggie opens her post), show where it goes without that difference: Aoife, Con, and in the last image a girl cheered on a bathroom scale. The world always changes eventually, here twice in one spring. A family that cannot change its mind was only waiting.

**One right person is not enough.** Tess works it out on the ferry in episode 7, and is thrown out for it. Alone, she makes things worse: she betrays Rosie to the ward. Only when the whole family writes its crossings down together, in episode 10, does anything change.

**Asking is not punished.** The one time Rosie breaks "face home", she finds the food, and her landing gives the clue that explains Liam. Kieran's homework log saves the family. Tess is wrong often (Rosie's nine seconds, Liam's missing margin, the ward), so the heroine is plainly not always right.

## 7. Characters

Eight principals. The inner circle, where care must run deepest, is Tess, Rosie, Peg and Danny.

**TESS MARLOW, 38, the weigher.** She keeps every number in her head. The family's lightest crosser from fourteen to twenty-six, she never turned; jobs were short then. Now she plans every dip. Single mother of Kieran.
- *Want:* keep the family fed and whole; this season, keep Rosie working and get her well.
- *Wound:* the week her father died, Peg made the fourteen-year-old the family's crosser, and made her light. She has counted every mouthful since.
- *Flaw:* she answers every loss with a tighter number, and she decides things for other people. "Margins are for people who can't count."
- *Secret:* she found Rosie's 9.4 seconds by pushing her to lose four more kilos.
- *Levers:* competence (the pleasure of watching her plan), interiority (we see her mouth move as she counts), love for Kieran and Rosie, and the pencil behind her ear that she never uses.
- *Arc:* she keeps the numbers in her head; she writes them down (episode 7, the back of a ferry timetable); she hands the pencil to Kieran and asks each person instead of deciding for them (episode 10).
- *Voice:* numbers instead of feelings. Short sentences, tenths, no swearing in front of Kieran. "How much?" "Near enough isn't." When frightened she gets more exact.

**ROSIE MARLOW, 22, Danny's daughter, the lightest adult crosser.**
- *Want:* to be the best ever. Secretly, to lift tumours out of children like the surgeons in the videos she watches. After Mayfair, to fix herself before anyone knows.
- *Wound:* she has been praised for being hungry since she was twelve.
- *Flaw:* pride that hides weakness.
- *Secret:* she has been turned since the cold open of episode 1.
- *Levers:* competence, humour, courage; from episode 3, the audience re-reading every cheer.
- *Arc:* she hides, then tells, then finds the door, then chooses to stay.
- *Voice:* quick and jokey, turns everything into a boast. "Show-off, me." When she is lying she gets funnier.

**PEG MARLOW, 72, the founder.** Frank's widow. Diabetic. Runs the weighbridge and the kitchen.
- *Want:* a family that is together, proud and owes nobody. Underneath: her field back.
- *Wound:* the Ministry took her farm, and the black took Frank.
- *Flaw:* certainty. She puts official letters in the drawer unopened, and remembers what she needs to.
- *Secret, kept even from herself:* she weighed Frank the morning he died, wrote "7.1" on his ticket, and sent him.
- *Levers:* warmth, humour, the fry-up, the chalk board, her daily finger-prick.
- *Arc:* the code in person becomes the first Marlow to write on paper ("Frank. 10 st 2. Turned."), and carries the rolls to the police herself.
- *Voice:* Essex, sayings of her own making. "We made that floor, we can have what's under it." "Hills don't move." "Then you've had a good day."

**DANNY MARLOW, 41, the foreman.** Runs the concrete crews by day. At 88 kilos he is good for a 2.4-second hop, or for sending his hand through a wall.
- *Want:* keep his kids safe; then revenge.
- *Wound:* at seventeen he was too heavy to take his father's place, and his little sister was chosen.
- *Flaw:* every loss is an attack.
- *Secret:* he said yes to Liam.
- *Levers:* love for his kids, humour, grief, the physical craft of his hand.
- *Arc:* he turns himself trying to turn an enemy; carries his son's printout in his wallet from episode 5 to episode 9; asks the question that explains it; refuses to send Kieran; chooses prison over exile.
- *Voice:* jokes, then silence. Talks with his hands. Never says "I'm sorry"; says "I'll sort it."

**DS RAY AMBROSE, 54, Met Handedness Unit.** Thin, vodka at his desk, lard on crackers; colleagues think he's a drunk. He turned eleven years ago carrying a child out of a collapsed building. His unit's real job is finding unregistered turned people, because of what they carry, and he treats it as a job about hunger.
- *Want:* stop the Marlows dying, and catch the "spinner" he believes is turning them.
- *Wound:* his first case, in 2002: a Marrable Street strongroom weigh-wall read 64.3 kilos where nobody stood, spreading for a month.
- *Flaw:* he hoards what he knows, and thinks feeding people is the whole of caring for them.
- *Secret:* he is the Laundry. For five years he has fed Peg's back kitchen on the rations of dead claimants.
- *Levers:* competence, decency, dry wit, his own hunger.
- *Arc:* from secret feeder to open accomplice; cured by the family's first real count.
- *Voice:* precise and dry, never raises it. "Linen has a hand, Mrs Marlow." Writes everything down, and shows no one.

**MAGGIE AHERN, 44, the Ahern weigher across the river in Gravesend, Tess's mirror image.** The Aherns run tugs and salvage and dip by the same code; Frank taught it to her father, Declan. One difference: Maggie opens her post. The Aherns also keep the watermen's tide books, including the Nobody Tide, and they time every job to the water.
- *Want:* protect her son Joe, win the war, then end it.
- *Wound:* her daughter Aoife turned last autumn on a nine-second job while nursing her baby, Niamh. Her milk could not feed him. She went again to cure herself, and ran out. Maggie makes up Niamh's bottles at 3 a.m.
- *Flaw:* she matches every blow.
- *Secret:* she sent Joe to watch the Mayfair dip, and later into the black beside Peg's safe.
- *Levers:* competence, a son, grief, being Tess's reflection.
- *Arc:* from enemy to the other half of the count. Declan never changes.
- *Voice:* clipped, sardonic, waterman's words: "slack", "make fast", "she's running".

**TERRY MARLOW, 66, Peg's younger brother, the cook.** Turned five years ago on a seven-second job. Went again eleven times; the black never gave him back. Eats from the Laundry's white tubs and cooks the family's food, which he cannot taste properly. The dog has always growled at him.

- *Wound:* the family's quiet shame; Peg has never once said the word "turned" about him.
- *Flaw:* he gives up gently and jokes it away.
- *Secret:* he has known Rosie was turned since Kieran's birthday, because the level in his pot keeps dropping at night. He tops it up and says nothing.
- *Levers:* warmth, humour, food, songs, his friendship by phone with Con Ahern (two turned old men swapping gravy recipes across a war).

- *Voice:* gentle, food words, old songs half sung. "Five in six I get my tea."

**KIERAN MARLOW, 12, Tess's son.**
- *Want:* to cross and be a real Marlow. Also, privately, to find the neighbours. Every night for his science project he weighs a 20 kg cast-iron test weight on the bridge and writes the number down, charting the Nobody Tide in grams. The family laughs, and allows it because it's homework.
- *Wound:* his dad left; the family is his whole world.
- *Flaw:* he wants in so badly he would cut weight for it.
- *Secret:* since Easter he has been skipping school dinners to make weight for the day they let him cross: 40 kilos at his birthday, 38 by episode 9.
- *Levers:* specificity (his log, his pencil, his graph on the office wall), humour, vulnerability.
- *Arc:* from wanting to cross to the family's first person who checks. He ends writing each lorry's weight on the roll and pencilling "checked against Maggie's".
- *Voice:* precise, school words, "how do you know?" asked about his own graph, never as a speech.

**Supporting.** *Liam*, 19, Danny's big-boned son, desperate to be a real crosser. *Shaun*, 26, cousin, wiry, 58 kilos. *Joe Ahern*, 17, Maggie's son. *Declan Ahern*, 70, who never changes. *Con Ahern*, turned, Maggie's uncle. *Aoife Ahern*, lost before the season begins, seen only in a photo and a weigh-wall printout. *Niamh*, her baby. *Siobhan Ahern*, 13, the girl on the bathroom scale. *Dilys*, who runs the launderette and drives the van. *Mick*, a lorry driver. *Mr Pinnock*, Trading Standards. *Sixpence*, the lurcher.

**Act five: 02:04.** The ticket printer chatters. 59 kg. Nothing on the bridge. Danny with a torch: nobody. Kieran keeps the ticket for his log.

**Twist.** The Aherns lost Aoife last autumn. She turned on a nine-second job in a bank where a Marlow crew was working the floor below. They have been at war for months, and the Marlows never knew.

**Hook.** Maggie rings Tess: "Con hasn't eaten in four days."

**Gaps.** *Opens:* the 02:04 visitor; why the bridge and the test weight read light (planted, not pressed); why the neighbours moved a mountain (a wonder). *Closes:* why the Aherns hate them.

### Episode 5: "Delivered"
*The war turns lethal, and the family's heaviest boy starves himself to join it.*

**Cold open.** With Rosie too weak to hop into it, Peg opens her own safe with its key for the first time in years. A bullet sits on the painting. The safe was locked. "It's been delivered."

**Act one: the radius.** Ray brings a coroner's report: Southend, 2019, a bullet in a skull with no entry wound. "Coroner wrote 'delivered'. That's what your safe's telling you. Whoever did it stood beside your safe." Peg: nobody stood there. Kieran shows his 02:04 ticket: 59. Peg moves Kieran's bed into the office: "Nobody stands near that boy without the bridge knowing."

**Act two: Con.** Con Ahern dies of hunger. Terry hears it from Maggie, on Con's phone. "Con used to ring me about gravy." The Marlows are killers too.

**Act three: the stump.** Shaun's reprisal, to leave a bullet in Declan's safe: he comes back a hair slow, his leg below the knee still in the black when his field closed. The stump is a clean flat plane, bloodless for a second, then not.

**Act four: no margin.** Liam, who has secretly lost ten kilos, begs for the next dip: the Ahern salvage lock-up, cash for Rosie's cure. Danny says yes. Tess weighs him on the bridge: 62.4, so 7.5 seconds. The job needs 7.4. No margin. "Margins are for people who can't count." Peg sends him off with the lucky fry-up; he eats every bite.

**Act five: the tenth.** In the black, Liam counts. The tingle comes early. A tenth of a second from home, he runs out.

**Twist.** Liam runs out, though every number said he would make it. The Ahern weigh-wall goes on reading him.

**Hook.** Maggie lets the family stand one minute beside the reading, as the lost are mourned now, and gives Danny the printout: 63.6 kg. He folds it into his wallet without looking. At home he weighs himself: 88. "Two seconds is enough to grab someone."

**Gaps.** *Opens:* why Liam ran out (the audience heard 62.4 and sees 63.6; nobody in the family compares them). *Closes:* who delivered the bullet (the Aherns, by implication).

### Episode 6: "Ten Seconds" (second major reversal, part one)
*Danny tries to turn an enemy, and Tess breaks into a clinic for a cure and finds a clock.*

**Cold open.** 02:04. The printer chatters; Kieran wakes in the office. Danny, rigged and waiting, steps into the black: 2.4 seconds. By his lamp he finds Joe Ahern mid-crossing beside the office: Joe's ribs, his beating heart within reach of Danny's hand, an envelope inside Joe's jacket. Danny's hand hovers at the heart. Then he grabs Joe and wrenches him round. "Got him."

**Act one: breakfast.** The bacon tastes of nothing and Terry's pot smells like home. Sixpence growls at Danny. Peg's meter: LO. A grip in the black turns one of the two, and Danny is the one. Terry slides his pot across without a word. In the safe, delivered after all: an envelope addressed to Tess. Peg hands it over unopened: "From them." Tess pockets it. The Laundry's bill trebles.

**Act two: the records room.** Tess decides to rob the cure. Rosie is too weak and Danny too heavy, so Tess crosses for the first time in twelve years: 61.0 kilos, a 2.5-second hop into Obverse's records room on Harley Street. The tingle comes back like an old song. By her lamp she reads the spine labels inside a locked cabinet and lifts one binder out.

**Act three: the protocol.** In the car she reads: "Transit 10.0 s. Expected restoration 0.70." Below it, a table: the odds of turning, second by second, from one in two hundred at two seconds to seven in ten at ten.

**Act four: the sums.** At the office, silently, she runs the season through the table: the surgeons' three seconds; Terry's eleven two-second hops; the painting's seven three-second hops; Marrable Street's ten seconds, a seven-in-ten chance of curing Rosie that came up wrong; Mayfair's 9.4.

**Twist.** The cure is nothing but time, and so is the curse. That is why surgeons are held to three seconds, why the short hops never cured anyone, and why Rosie, sent in for 9.4, turned.

**Hook.** Tess pricks her own finger with Peg's meter: 5.4. Straight. She looks at Rosie. "I gave you nine."

**Gaps.** *Opens:* what's in Maggie's envelope; whether Tess will tell the family. *Closes:* Restoration's method; why Rosie turned; what the grab did.

### Episode 7: "Two Counts" (second major reversal, part two)
*Two enemy weighers meet on the ferry and lay their families' secrets side by side.*

**Cold open.** The Nobody Tide slaps the Tilbury pontoon under no moon. On the ferry at Nobody slack, Maggie sits with a waterman's tide book open at the Nobody columns. The envelope said: "Ferry. Nobody slack. Thursday. Just you."

**Act one: the loft.** Before she goes, Tess finds what nobody knew the family kept: twenty-five years of audit rolls, one ticket for every job-day weigh-in. Weight gives the seconds. Memory gives who turned. She and Terry sit on the loft floor naming names, and the family's stories come apart: "Gary went to Spain" (he couldn't; HAND: T; he's on the Register in Leeds), "Uncle Vic's heart", "Maureen's nerves".

**Act two: the Ahern side.** On neutral water Maggie brings the Ahern count: their tide books time every job to the second, for the tug pickup. Joe felt a weight at 3:12 at Mayfair and flinched; Maggie had sent him to watch the Marlows work. Tess lays down Kieran's 02:04 ticket, 59 kg. Maggie: "He's fifty-nine, my Joe." The Mayfair log puts the two bodies six metres apart the whole time. Aoife turned on a nine-second job. Joe came back from Danny's grab straight, and still made his drop: this invitation.

**Act three: the graph.** On the back of a ferry timetable the women draw the first graph either family has ever made: seconds along the bottom, turned or not. Eleven Marlows and seven Ahern crossers turned in twenty-five years. Nobody under three seconds. Most over eight. Maggie, folding the timetable: "Your Hill moved our tide. Forty minutes, since April."

**Twist.** Nobody spun anybody except Danny, and he spun himself. Rosie's "something took hold of me" was pride. Con died in a war about nothing.

**Act four: the stove.** At the table Tess lays the timetable down. Peg feeds it to the office stove. Danny: "And Liam? Did your sums do Liam too?" The count can't explain running out, and Tess has no answer. Peg: "You went to them."

**Hook.** Tess is put out of the house. Next morning the Laundry van doesn't come.

**Gaps.** *Opens:* who is the Laundry, and why has it stopped; why Liam ran out (pressed hard now). *Closes:* who is turning the Marlows; the Ahern war's cause.

### Episode 8: "The Laundry"
*With their secret supply cut off, the Marlows find out who has really been feeding them.*

**Cold open.** Ray, suspended, faces an audit: seven claimants on his caseload have been dead for years and still draw rations.

**Act one: go again.** The pantry empties; Terry, Rosie and Danny turn grey. Peg's answer is the family cure: fourteen two-second hops for Rosie in the flooded pit, blindfolded, facing home. Nothing.

**Act two: the batch codes.** Tess, living in her car, traces the van to Dilys's launderette and the batch codes on the lids to Ray's caseload.

**Act three: Ray's flat.** Tubs, vodka, lard, and a folder of every Marlow's weight since 2002 in his own hand. "I watched your dad's weight spread for a month. I wasn't watching it again." He never once asked them to change. Tess: "You fed us so you'd never have to tell us anything."

**Twist.** The Laundry is Ray. For five years the man hunting them has fed them on dead people's food.

**Act four: more.** Tess tells the family: "We never fed our own." Peg's pride breaks, and she answers with more: raid the Register's depot at Purfleet, long crossings. To keep Rosie off it, Tess calls Ray's colleagues. Rosie is taken to the Register ward in Whitechapel: no locks, only food. Rosie, as they take her: "You decided. Again." A nurse, handing her a T-meter: "Police don't get the list, love. Not since 2019."

**Hook.** Rosie eats her first real meal in months, beside a sealed Register loo, while the ward TV reports that the next-door tremors since April have spelled out a molecule: a sugar of the other hand. The Register's scientists say it may mean the neighbours' life is the other hand. Front pages: "COME ROUND FOR TEA." The next advert is the Hand Company.

**Gaps.** *Opens:* what's next door; will Peg's raid work. *Closes:* who fed them; why the van stopped.

### Episode 9: "The Deep" (third major reversal, part one)
*The family raids the state's pantry while Rosie breaks the family's oldest rule and finds what it hid.*

**Cold open.** Peg does the numbers now, on the chalk board. Shaun's lost lower leg weighed 5.5 kilos. "Eleven tenths, Shaun. You're worth more without it." He takes the longest leg of the raid: nine seconds.

**Act one: the lightest.** Kieran begs to go: 38 kilos, 12.4 seconds, the lightest Marlow ever. Peg's chalk says yes. Danny looks at the bridge ticket, then at the boy: he was 40 at his birthday. "You've been making weight." Liam did too. Danny says no: "Not him. Not after Liam." His first no. Terry goes, "for another roll".

**Act two: the depot.** Purfleet: sealed Register stores behind weigh-walls. The family lifts sealed cases out through bead floors. Terry comes back from his two-second leg still turned; Shaun comes back from his nine-second leg turned. Peg: "Bad luck." Three weeks of food. Kieran sleeps in the office.

**Act three: the ward.** Rosie, fed, is 46.2 kilos and strong. "COME ROUND FOR TEA" is on every paper. She reasons it out to the night nurse: the Hill takes you; the Deep is soft. The only thing holding her is food, and she believes the food is next door. She walks out through the door, because there is no lock, and catches the last train to Grays.

**Act four: the wrong tug.** Night on the bridge. Kieran asleep in the office. Rosie takes her rig from the rack and, for the first time in her life, does not face home. She follows the Deep's soft pull and steps out one world over into black water: cold, ears like the pit at eight metres, salt, no floor. Her own held breath. Something brushes her face. She fires her spare, faces home, and lands on the weighbridge in a gush of sea. The printer chatters. Kieran wakes. "It's sea. Under us. All of it."

**Act five: the bucket.** Morning. The bridge is wet, strung with weed; a small crab picks across it. Sixpence growls at the puddle as he growls at Terry. Tess smells it: wrong, like something gone off. Rosie smells it: the seaside. Terry: "Southend, 1968." Both are right. Terry cooks the crab and eats it. "It's crab. It tastes of crab." Kieran, for his project, has weighed a bucket of the sea on the office platform scale and on Peg's brass balance, and the two numbers disagree by 1.1 per cent. Peg tips the bucket down the drain before "they" come. Kieran points at his graph on the wall: the step on 4 April, there all along. Tess opens the dresser drawer: the April Trading Standards letter ("a local gravity change of minus 1.1 per cent; re-verify within 28 days") and the 2019 Register letter. Danny unfolds his printout: 63.6. Tess, from the roll: 62.4. Danny: "Why's it a kilo out?" The sum: 1.1 per cent of Liam, plus half a kilo of fry-up. His real seconds were 7.28, for a 7.4-second job.

**Twists.** Next door is the other hand: the Deep is a sea full of food for the turned. And the family's own scale, lying since the Hill moved, killed Liam.

**Hook.** Tess rings Ray. "What did my dad weigh?" "Sixty-four kilos."

**Gaps.** *Opens:* what Frank really weighed, and why the legend says fifteen stone; what went down the drain (a wonder). *Closes:* why Liam ran out; what the Deep is; whether next door is food.

### Episode 10: "Paper" (third major reversal, part two)
*The family reads its own record for the first time and must decide who goes where, and whether to give the record away.*

**Cold open.** 2002, as Peg remembers it: a big Frank on the bridge at dawn, joking while the ticket prints.

**Act one: Frank.** In the loft, Frank's tickets: 12 st 4 the week after his four-second Farringdon job, then down every week for six months to 10 st 2 the morning he died, with Peg's own pencilled "7.1". It is Rosie's curve. The cold open replays as it was: a thin man who can't open the jam jar, Peg laughing as she opens it for him. Ray, at the kitchen door: 64.3 kilos, held still in the black until the seconds ran out. From the drawer Peg takes a 2001 envelope addressed to Frank from the Ministry, free handedness screening for anyone who has crossed, and opens it herself. Then she picks up the pencil and writes beside the ticket: "Frank. 10 st 2. Turned."

**Twist.** The family's founding lesson was a memory that had turned. Frank wasn't too heavy. He was starving, and the family sent him.

**Act two: the night of writing.** All night the family writes every crossing they remember onto the rolls in pencil, Kieran checking dates. Maggie crosses the river with her tide books, her salvage steelyard and Joe. The cluster grows. Kieran lays Rosie's depth (the pit at eight metres), Maggie's Nobody Tide tables and the place the object landed side by side: "The thing in Nan's field didn't fall. It stepped out. Where their ground is our ground." At Nobody low water, the Hill's shore meets the old field. Tess hands Kieran the pencil.

**Act three: Ray's cure.** Dawn. They offer Ray the cure. The bridge gives him 8.4 seconds, Maggie's steelyard 8.3. A month ago they'd have sent him for 8.4. They send him for 7.5, and tell him the odds: about four in ten. He goes; he comes back; he signs the roll. Mirror script. Faces fall. Ray laughs: eleven years he has written backwards so the straight world could read him. Peg's meter: 5.1. He eats a bacon sandwich slowly.

**Act four: the field.** 05:52, Nobody low water, Mucking Marsh: the Ministry's fence and hut, the Marlows' old gate. Rosie scouts: three seconds along the Hill's shoulder, a step onto a shore we never see, one breath of their air, the spare home. Sand on her boots. "What did you see?" She doesn't answer. She breathes out for Terry, and he breathes in: "Southend." Terry chooses. It will take him five seconds to find the shore: one in six he arrives the wrong way round, starving there and a danger to them. "Five in six I get my tea." Peg says the word "turned" to him for the first time in five years, and then: "Go on, then." He steps off the world at the fence. Rosie chooses to stay turned: "Can't catch anything. There's wards that need that." Danny, who could go too: "I'm done making weight. I'll go to them."

**Act five: the rolls.** The rolls are twenty-five years of evidence, and the only honest record on Earth of what time in the black does, when the Hill moved, and where the door is. Tess holds the boxes and, for the first time, asks each of them instead of deciding. Kieran: give. Rosie: give. Danny: give. Maggie puts her tide books on the pile. Peg carries the boxes to the van herself and drives them to Grays police station; the office stove stays cold. At the desk they cuff Tess. Rosie could lift her out of them. Tess says no.

**Ending hook.** The epilogue (section 12): in December the neighbours send a second message, a sugar of *our* hand. Last shot: the Grays outfall at Nobody low water.

**Gaps.** *Closes:* what Frank weighed and why; whether Ray can be cured; where a turned person can go; whether the Marlows will be caught, at what price. *Leaves open, as wonders:* what Rosie saw; whether Terry arrived straight or turned; why the neighbours moved the Hill; what went down the drain.

## 12. The ending

**Epilogue, December.** Six short scenes, no dialogue longer than two lines.
1. Front pages: "THE TEN-SECOND CURE: A GRAVEL FIRM'S RECORDS". The drift table is public. Turned people are cured on licensed rigs for the price of an appointment. Obverse's shares have halved. The Register has opened a consultation on licensed crossings next door, at Mucking, for turned volunteers only.
2. Tess in prison, having claimed every number as hers. On her cell wall, a letter from Kieran with a graph drawn on it.
3. Danny in prison, turned, eating the state's rations he refused all his life. On his table, an appointment card for a licensed cure in March. He hasn't decided. Ray, charged with misconduct, visits; he signs the visitors' book in plain writing.
4. Rosie on an isolation ward at the Royal Free, HAND: T on her badge, walking unmasked between fever beds. She is training with the licensed crossing surgeons who operate on patients nobody else can go near, and she lifts a tumour out of a feverish child in three seconds, inside the limit she once called soft.
5. Peg, on bail for the depot raid and for "release of other-hand matter", runs the re-verified bridge with Kieran. He writes each lorry's weight on the roll and adds in pencil, "checked against Maggie's". Beside Frank's photo hangs Terry's last ticket, 71.4, with "Went next door" in Peg's hand. Across the river Siobhan Ahern, 13, steps on a bathroom scale while Declan chalks the number and the family cheers. Maggie, in the doorway, does not.
6. **The hook.** On the office radio, next-door events: since November the tremors have spelled a second molecule. This time it is a sugar of *our* hand. A scientist: "It's ours. We don't know why." Kieran writes it on the roll. Peg looks at Terry's ticket. **Last shot:** the Grays outfall at Nobody low water, the estuary running out to sea.

**What it settles.**
- Why the Marlows turned: time in the black, lengthened by their own concrete and their own rules.
- Who fed them: Ray, on dead people's food.
- Why Liam died: the family's scale, the fry-up, the margin cut to a tenth, the letter in the drawer.
- What Frank weighed, and why the legend said otherwise.
- Where a turned person can go: back into the dice, next door, or on as they are, and the whole country now knows the odds of the first.
- The war, ended between the two women who compared numbers.
- The family's normal has changed: it writes things down and checks them against someone else's. The world's normal has changed too: the drift is public and a door is known.

**What it costs.** Liam, Con and (before the season) Aoife. Frank's legend. Tess's and Danny's freedom. Danny, turned for good unless he takes the dice. Shaun's leg, and Shaun turned. Terry, gone. Ray's career. Peg's pride. The firm's name. And the Aherns, unchanged under Declan, still cheering a child's hunger.

**Residue.** Something from next door went down a Grays drain. Somewhere one world over, an old man is either eating crab or starving among people for whom he is the plague. And every viewer has a bathroom scale.

**The hook for a second season.** The neighbours have answered, in the only honest channel there is, with a molecule of our hand. It can be read three ways and the season shows all three rules without saying so: they can feed us too; they have found our life in their sea (thirty-one years of lost crossers drowned in the Deep); or they have met someone who arrived the wrong way round. Season two begins with the Register's first licensed volunteer at the Mucking fence, and a gravel firm that now keeps paper.

## 13. Rules the physics must never break

1. **Nothing crosses between leaves but gravity.** No light, sound, radio or smell from next door, except what a crosser physically carries back inside a field.
2. **Next door is never shown.** Not through Rosie's eyes, not in flashback, not in a dream. We see only what comes back: water, weed, a crab, sand, a breath.
3. **Sight in the black is lamp-lit and arm's length.** Inside that circle, every inside is visible; outside it, black.
4. **The budget is a fifth of a second per kilo under a hundred, on a Marlow rig.** Everything inside the field counts: cargo, air, breakfast. Nobody outlasts their budget and comes back.
5. **Running out is dispersal.** No body is ever found. What remains is a weight, read by instruments, spreading slowly.
6. **A turn cannot be felt, and cannot be seen by the person it happened to.** Only chemistry, or a comparison with something that never crossed (Peg's meter, the catalogue photo, the airport strip, food), settles it. Never a look in a mirror.
7. **The drift table in section 5 is law.** Every crossing's odds come from it, and outcomes must be believable at those odds: no long runs of failure at good odds, or of success at bad ones.
8. **The grip turns one of two; cargo turns with its carrier.** Nobody can turn a thing without turning with it.
9. **A turned body gets nothing from ordinary food or drugs and catches nothing ordinary.** It lives on water, spirits, fat and mirror food, and starves in about six months without mirror food.
10. **A turned person's own germs are mirror life.** This shows only as law, borders, rules and risk. It is never shown as a growth, a dish, a flask or a mould.
11. **No wall, lock, seal, knot or skin holds a crosser with a rig.** Take the rig.
12. **Gravity from next door falls with the cube of distance.** The Hill's move makes Grays scales read 1.1 per cent light, Gravesend's barely, London's not at all. Weighbridges and platform scales lie; balances, steelyards, weigh-walls and rigs do not.
13. **The spare fires once, within a minute, for half the seconds.**
14. **Facing home keeps you on your own world. It does nothing about turning.**
15. **Stepping out next door is a crossing, and so a toss.** A turned person who slides arrives where the food fits; one who turns arrives straight, starving there and dangerous to them.
16. **The neighbours' life is the other hand.** Turned people can eat it. Straight people cannot, and it smells wrong to them.
17. **No new rules.** If a scene needs one, change the scene.

## 14. Notes for episode writers

**Tone.** Plain, vivid, concrete, funny until it isn't. Essex kitchen warmth set against the black. The world is ordinary and works: inspectors inspect, nurses nurse, the state feeds the turned. Only two families, across one river, never ask.

**Words that never reach the screen.** No character or caption may use, as ideas: conjecture, criticism (as a principle), error correction, refute, falsify, fallible, "good explanation", "hard to vary", growth of knowledge, static or dynamic society, tradition of criticism, authority (as a lecture topic), optimism (as doctrine), "nothing is settled", "question everything", "the problem of", infinity. Nobody makes a speech about asking questions. The words in this plan's scaffolding ("the culture", "the doom", "the code" as an abstraction) stay in this plan. The questions on screen are ordinary ones: "How much?" "Why's it a kilo out?" "What did my dad weigh?"

**Never echo The Catch.** No mint, and no taste test as a ritual (identity is settled by Peg's meter, the catalogue, the strip, or food). No backwards signs or print from a turned character's point of view; the camera stays on the straight side of every mirror. No dishes of germs, no flask, no mould, no growth shown. No hands meeting on glass. No episode ends on a meal that can't be shared. No kind figure from next door; no ship; no child's drawing; no toy with a letter; no lift, lift engineer or the word "lift" for a job (it's a "dip"). No oxygen cylinder, charge bar, gravity needle or ledge.

**Never echo Caraway.** No rite at a set age, no scripture, no heretics, no twins, no moving the machine, no dead fed to a machine. **The Marlows never send a message in mass;** only the neighbours do.

**Never end the way the rejected pitches did.** Nobody on our side converses with next door by weight in a rhythm (no dances, no steps, no knocks). Terry's going is not the season's climax, and nobody watches a trace to see whether he lives.

**Crossing grammar.** Sound drops to heartbeat and breath. The lamp's circle is the whole frame. The wrist unit counts tenths. Close-up on fingertips when the tingle starts. Return with a gasp and the smell of the room. Until episode 7 every return gets "Straight?" "Straight." After the ferry the family stops asking and Peg's meter comes out of the drawer instead.

**Care before threat.** Every episode shows the normal (the fry-up, the chalk board, Sixpence, the lorry drivers' bacon rolls) before it breaks it. Ray and Maggie get scenes of their own that have nothing to do with the Marlows. Liam gets a whole scene in episode 5 of wanting to be a crosser before he is weighed. Aoife is never used only as a motive: her photo, her baby, and Maggie's bottles make her a person.

**Kieran never crosses this season.** He is weighed, he trains blindfold in the pit, he begs, and he is refused.

**The minute.** When a crosser is lost, the family stands one minute beside the weigh-wall reading. Used for Liam (5); remembered for Aoife (4, 7); not used for Terry, because he isn't lost.

## 15. What changed from the pitch, and why

Written for the owner. Each entry says what changed and why.

**The big changes, from making every law true**

1. **Next door is now food for the turned.** In the pitch, Rosie followed the Deep, found a sea and nothing else, and the book's ending was tested and failed. Now the neighbours' life really is the other hand. The sea that lands on the weighbridge smells wrong to Tess and right to Rosie, the dog growls at it as it growls at Terry, and Terry eats a crab from it that tastes of crab. *Why:* you asked for every law, including this one. Followed honestly, it makes the season's point sharper, not softer: for thirty-one years the cure for every turned Marlow lay one world over, in the Deep and the Hill they felt on every crossing, and "face home" made it unthinkable.
2. **Exile is a real cure, and someone takes it.** In episode 10 Kieran works out that the Marlows' old field, where the object arrived, is where the neighbours' ground meets ours at the Nobody Tide's low water. Rosie scouts it and brings back one breath of their air. Terry goes. *Why:* you asked for "exile is a cure" to be true and to do work. To keep what the reviews liked about the pitch (it didn't simply walk into the book's own ending), the heroine doesn't go, the finale isn't about the going, and it isn't free: the step next door is itself a crossing, so it is a coin toss (one in six for Terry). Rosie, who found the door, chooses to stay.
3. **Terry is not cured in the depot raid, and is now a principal.** In the pitch he came back straight and ate a bacon sandwich. *Why:* on a two-second leg the odds are one in two hundred, so a cure would have been a fluke; and he is the right person to go next door: the family's pariah, who went again eleven times, cooks food he can't taste, and has never heard Peg say the word "turned". His phone friendship with Con gives the war's first death a face on the Marlow side.
4. **Mirror life is accounted for and does jobs.** A turned person's own germs are now the reason for the Register's rules (sealed loos, no food work, cremation), for HAND: T passports being refused abroad (so Rosie can't run for a cheaper cure), for Ray's unit existing at all, and for who may step next door (to the neighbours, a turned person is safe; a straight one would be the plague). Peg tipping the neighbours' sea down the drain is the season's residue and one of her charges. *Why:* the review of the book's physics flagged it as the gravest gap; you asked for a use that is not The Catch's dishes and flask or Caraway's mould. It is never shown growing.
5. **A second message closes the season.** The neighbours spell a sugar of our hand. *Why:* it is the one honest channel, used again; every reading of it grows from rules already on screen (the lost drowned in the Deep; Terry's one in six); and it gives a second season a question no one can answer by looking.

**Changes to the show's own guesses**

6. **The drift table is lower at short times** (one in two hundred at two seconds, not one in twenty-five). *Why:* at the pitch's odds, the family would have turned someone every year in the short-job era, which contradicts "almost nobody turned". The new table also explains, exactly, why every short "go again" failed.
7. **The grip rule now says cargo turns with its carrier.** *Why:* the fourth review caught that the painting turned with Rosie although the old rule said a grip always turns one of two. The new line also blocks cheap mirror food and makes the crab fit Terry.
8. **A fifth guess, the spare.** A rig can fire once more for half its seconds. *Why:* nobody could step out next door and come back without it. It is shown in episode 1 ("always carry your spare") and declared once.
9. **Next door is mapped a little further.** Their sea's surface is about eight metres up; the Hill is an island; its shore meets our ground at the old field. *Why:* exile needs a place to land, and tying it to where the object arrived joins the family's origin to its ending.

**Fixes the three reviews asked for**

10. **The doom's engine is now the concrete and "never go back twice".** "Make weight" stays, but as what hides the starvation, not what causes the turning. *Why:* the originality review saw Caraway's admired fast in the pitch's chain of cause. Danny now pours the floors the family robs, and a one-trip rule, right in Frank's day, turns people now that jobs are long.
11. **Rosie's tells are hidden in plain sight.** The make-up mirror, the spoon by fridge-light and the phone in turned mode are gone. Mirror writing is her old party trick (a card from when she was fourteen is on the fridge); she has always been bad with jars; she prices a cure "for Uncle Terry"; the lard is "for cold-water swimming". *Why:* the fourth review found the first reversal was no reversal for the audience. Now the meter scene lands on the audience with the family, and every cheer re-reads.
12. **Ray is revealed as turned in episode 2**, eating lard in the Obverse waiting room. *Why:* it gives episode 2 a real twist, and makes the lard in Rosie's van a fair clue.
13. **Peg's glucose meter settles who has turned.** It reads LO on turned blood because its enzyme never crossed. Tess tests herself with it instead of eating a biscuit. *Why:* sugar tastes sweet in either hand, so the biscuit proved nothing (fourth review); and a meter keeps the show away from any taste test, which was The Catch's and Caraway's device.
14. **"The Chemist" is now "the Laundry".** *Why:* the name pointed straight at Breaking Bad.
15. **A job is a "dip", not a "lift"; Hatton Garden is now Marrable Street.** Frank's death, Ray's first case and the episode 3 diamond room are all on Marrable Street. *Why:* "lift" echoed The Catch; Hatton Garden was a borrowed real robbery; one street for three events gives Peg a line ("your grandad's street").
16. **Kieran has a want and a hobby.** He wants to cross, and every night for school he weighs a test weight on the bridge and writes it down. *Why:* the originality review said his label stated the theme; the fourth review said the neighbours were wallpaper until episode 9. His log catches the Hill's move in April and becomes the proof the bridge lies.
17. **Liam's answer moves to the end of episode 9.** Kieran weighs a bucket of the sea on the office scale and on Peg's brass balance, and they disagree. *Why:* the fourth review found the finale overloaded and episode 9's discovery paying nothing; the theme review wanted Rosie's rule-break to be rewarded. Her landing now brings both the food and the clue.
18. **Maggie's "scale" is a salvage steelyard; Peg's check is a brass balance.** *Why:* any ordinary scale near the Hill would read light too. Balances compare masses, so they tell the truth. It keeps the physics honest.
19. **"Never go to them" is shown to be out of date.** Since 2019 the police can't read the Register; the letter is in Peg's drawer and a nurse says so in episode 8. *Why:* the theme review warned that the ending made the family's fear of the state look right.
20. **Peg doesn't bin letters; she puts them in a drawer unopened.** *Why:* the letters have to be found later (April's gravity notice, the 2019 Register letter, a 2001 screening offer addressed to Frank). And it says something about her: she can't open them, and can't throw away anything from the Ministry that took her field.
21. **No cars at the gate.** Peg drives the rolls to the police herself. *Why:* the theme review asked that prison come only from choosing to give the evidence, never from being caught.
22. **The sums are fixed.** Shaun's lost leg (5.5 kg) buys "eleven tenths", and he is now light enough (58 kg) for the long leg. The rolls cover twenty-five years, reaching Frank. The ferry shows a cluster, not a line. The weighbridge error is 1.1 per cent throughout. *Why:* the fourth review's arithmetic check.

**Other additions, each to put a law to work**

23. **Aoife Ahern and looking-glass milk.** Maggie's wound is now her daughter, who turned while nursing, could not feed her baby, went again, and ran out. *Why:* it uses the milk and gives the Aherns a reason for war as strong as the Marlows'.
24. **The Twitch** opens episode 2. *Why:* the book's nuclear test next door; it floods every City vault at once and lengthens every job again.
25. **Danny's hand hovers at Joe's heart,** and he sees Maggie's letter in Joe's jacket and doesn't read it. *Why:* the book's tumour-and-heart pairing and the radius of sight; and the irony that Danny's revenge interrupts a peace offer.

27. **Danny's first plan is to turn the Aherns' food, and Tess stops it** ("Then you'd be eating it"), with a line about the Almaty seed. *Why:* the book's seed-vault weapon, used to teach the carrier rule the crab later depends on, and to foreshadow Danny turning himself.
28. **The Looking Act comes from a named case about a ring in a stomach.** *Why:* the book's interrogator example, used to explain why Ray must wait.
29. **The Hand Company** gives Rosie somewhere to go and Obverse's share price a reason. *Why:* the book's plague-proof soldiers.
