1. **The Emre reveal is guessable at fifteen percent in.** Where: the premise and Chapter II. Problem: a brother missing at this exact site, plus a presence singing the one lullaby only their mother used, plus a readership that has met time-slip caves before — most genre-literate readers will call "he's still inside" by the end of Chapter II and spend eleven chapters waiting for the book to agree. Why it matters: the owner's first mandate is that time travel be the *last* plot point revealed; a guessed mechanism converts wonder into procedure even if no scene technically confirms it. Fix: (a) make Emre's file region-vague — "missing, earthquake, September 1999," rubble somewhere in the province — so the site-link is Nilay's private, unprovable suspicion rather than file fact, and keep the jacket copy equally vague; (b) fork the presence in-scene: the mother alive in the village and denying it, a neighbor woman accused once and never cleared, storm damage as the ribbon's mundane reading; (c) include one warm presence in the book (a woman's voice in Ch. V, with its own mundane fork) that is never re-keyed at all, so the reader learns presences are plural and stops pricing each one as Emre.

2. **"He never once asked what year it was" is the loudest leak in the book.** Where: Chapter XI, clue 38. Problem: this is a near-explicit statement that Melek's visitor was dislocated in time, delivered three chapters before confirmation — it hands the reader the mechanism in Melek's mouth. Why it matters: it violates the spirit of "nothing confirms time travel before the final reveal" without technically breaking the letter. Fix: keep the confession's payload, retune the wording to be innocent on first pass and lethal on reread: *"Forty years, and he never once asked me what was new."* First read: a hermit's serenity. Retro-read: identical charge. Purge "year" from Melek's lexicon everywhere.

3. **Interlude X confirms cross-time burial four chapters early.** Where: Interlude X ("burying the man who came to measure — keepers have always buried scientists") landing in the same chapter as Márton's death. Problem: any reader who has already linked Interlude VIII to the coring will read X as "the keepers buried our geophysicist" — keepers handling a 2025 corpse is the mechanism, staged at chapter ten of fourteen. Fix: antique the buried measurer past denial — knouted cord, brass instruments, a 1924-flavored surveyor — so the first pass reads as deep past and only the post-reveal reread doubles for Márton, which is where that shiver belongs.

4. **Interlude XII tells the reader the far keepers are future humans.** Where: Interlude XII, "the far lamplighters are us, later." Problem: it's framed as belief, but it's the only theory in the book delivered in a serene, authoritative diction from inside the site, two chapters before the reveal — readers will weigh it above Kaya Bey's hermits and Yusuf's server. Fix: translate the claim into the site's own road-idiom, temporal only in retro-read: *"they walk the road farther along than we do."* Same belief, no "later," no "us" — the reader can't cash the sentence until XIV hands them the currency.

5. **Halden's most frightening fact is asserted in the character sheet and absent from the outline.** Where: §13 claims "Márton's last good hour was spent in conversation with him"; Chapter IX stages only a mailed file. Problem: Johan-grade dread is built in intimate, banal scenes the reader *shares*; as outlined, Halden is a signature on effects, and the one scene that would make the reader complicit in his kindness exists only as a note to self. This is the difference between a villain the reader respects and one they fear. Fix: stage the hour — end Chapter IX with Márton and Halden at the dig-house table (daughters, weather, tea gone cold), thirty lines of nothing, then the descent at dusk. Add one more weightless scene: tea with Priska after the inquest in Ch. X. The on-page budget survives; the fear doubles.

6. **The governing-fiction mirror is the one visibly borrowed Johan move.** Where: §13 — "listens until a person tells him their governing fiction, repeats it back once, slightly altered, and leaves." Problem: this is Johan's exact method transplanted, which the orchestrator's note forbids, and it makes Halden a psychological virtuoso when his true resource is paper. Fix: replace mirroring with administrative precocity — gratitude that arrives before the favor it thanks, memos dated before the requests they grant (the site-closure memo, dated the day before "the team's request"), the car ordered before the decision. Deniable as efficiency, backdating, or something read off the Wall; every reading is worse than mirroring. He never reflects anyone. He anticipates the file.

7. **Chapter XIV is carrying a novel's payoff in 3,200 words.** Where: the Ch. XIV beat list. Problem: protocol, wax, vigil, second person, tin box, three letters, Emre, ribbon, mechanics, ochre bowl, palmprint, certificate, memo, thirty-one points, Book Zero line, Halden's filing, Göbekli coda, looped interlude — a beat every ~300 words. The reunion the book spends 40,000 words earning will be elbowed by paperwork. Fix: sequence for breath — Emre and the ribbon get the chapter's one long uninterrupted passage; the envelope and certificate arrive afterward in aftermath prose; cut the Göbekli coda to ~150 words (three sentences and the water); give Interlude XIV the final white space alone.

8. **Nilay's grief idles for eight chapters.** Where: Chapters III–X. Problem: the engine fires in II and goes silent until XI while the book services instruments, archives, and theories; the POV owners risk becoming delivery systems for their explanations — the reader admires the middle rather than feeling it. Fix: thread Emre through the village, not the clue-machinery — the vigil women who were girls in 1999 and avoid his name; have Nilay sit one stranger's vigil in Ch. VIII (every vigil is someone's Emre); give Melek real scene-time in I, V, and XI beyond her function as oracle.

9. **Chapter VIII is an aftermath chapter parked directly after the peak.** Where: Ch. VIII. Problem: VII is the encounter; VIII is reports, contradictions, and a closure memo — a structural valley at the worst possible place, and readers put books down in valleys. Fix: make VIII a dossier chapter — the four reports as verbatim found documents (documents aren't head-hopping), contradicting in count and speaker with the narration refusing to adjudicate, plus one live event inside the writing: a fact re-checked twice that has changed between drafts. End on the coin's empty square in the evidence box. This also fixes a propagating arithmetic bug: the blueprint's "nine chapters on Nilay… plus IV" counts IV twice — she has eight.

10. **"The corridor is longer coming out than going in" is House of Leaves with the serial numbers filed off.** Where: §5b evidence list. Problem: interior-that-measures-wrong is the most famous spatial-uncanny device in contemporary fiction; genre readers will date the move instantly — and it points at the wrong engine (space, not tenure). Fix: cut the spatial tell entirely; keep the room count as pure tally-refusal (geometry stable, the number refuses); replace corridor-length with combustive and temporal tells — same stride count out as in but two lamps' less oil burned; eight breaths in, six out.

11. **The far-keeper evidence stack tips from uncanny into proof.** Where: Ch. VII–VIII (2°C thermal image, no-C14 ash, no-tritium water, living-tissue/dead-carbon einkorn, aperiodic basalt mats, the surviving sandal frame). Problem: each is deniable; five together constitute a case file. The owner's mandate is that no reader ever feel confident they know what is happening. Fix: keep exactly two impossible objects — the ash and the einkorn, the thematic pair; demote the 2°C to Yusuf's recollection of a screen whose file corrupted; tritium to one line in a lab email; let the mats be felt underfoot and never sampled. Scarcity is this genre's oxygen.

12. **The best subliminal devices may fire below the reader's detection threshold — and the loop can close harder.** Where: the drifting tide (18→19→20 across 200+ pages) and the wrist migration (III→IX). Problem: a one-minute increment in a repeating paragraph, three times, is below most readers' conscious notice; devices only the writer can hear are decoration. Fix: (a) add a fourth tide occurrence in XIV, post-reveal, returned to eighteen — the loop visibly exhaled, still never remarked; (b) amend "unremarked" to "flagged, never explained" for the watches — III notes they ride the left wrist "where the artery is closest," IX repeats the phrase about the right — the flag makes the pattern-match fire while the inexplicability survives; (c) let the relay formula's final recurrence address the next keeper with no name at all — the seat the reader is sitting in.

13. **Chapter IV takes the reader off-site at the exact moment the hook is set.** Where: Ch. IV (Valletta and Malta, 3,000 words). Problem: after two chapters establishing the site's pull, a full travel-and-archive chapter at the quarter mark — and it front-loads Halden's mystique so early that XIII re-treads the same cold tea. Fix: compress Valletta to the first third; open at Saflieni's Oracle Room with the knock and the answering silence; end with something pulling her home — a missed call, the fourth door spoken of for the first time — so the reader returns to the village leaning forward.

14. **The XIII confrontation is shaped like a summary, not a duel.** Where: Ch. XIII. Problem: Nilay assembling the pattern aloud is a recap-lecture with the villain as audience, and "thanks her for a fair summary" is stock quiet-villain furniture. Fix: have her name two hinges and stop — and let Halden supply the third himself, as a courtesy ("And the hospital?" "The transfer was the least I could do."). His completion of her indictment is worse than his listening. Alternative: she opens his file to make her case and finds her own sentences already in it, dated last month.

15. **The proper nouns blur at speed.** Where: throughout. Problem: the Register (list), the Registrar (man), Book Zero (ledger), the Counting Wall (stone), plus forty rooms, a ninth room, a fourth door, and a fourth of September — a numeric and nominal soup producing wrong-way confusion exactly where the reader must be oriented enough to be disoriented correctly. Fix: characters call the site list "the List"; fix doors (vertical levels) versus rooms (horizontal count) in one establishing paragraph of Ch. I; let the door/date echo occur once, quietly, and never again.
