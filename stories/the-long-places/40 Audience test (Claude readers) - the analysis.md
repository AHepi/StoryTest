# 40 Audience test (Claude readers) - the analysis

*Five simulated readers, each played by a Claude helper, read the finished book (file 36, with the one-word fix from entry 38) and reported how it played for them. A sixth Claude helper wrote this analysis from their five reports. It replaces the MiMo panel in file 37, whose readers thought only briefly before answering.*

---

Five made-up readers, each with a different taste, read the whole finished book and wrote a report. This page puts their five reports side by side.

## The short version

- **Average score: 7.0 out of 10.** Four of the five gave it 7 or 8. Joe, the thriller reader, gave it 6.
- **Everyone was moved by the same things:** the 1999 chapter, Priska hearing her dead father's cough, the breach, Melek's forty winters, and above all the mother folding the ribbon. All five picked the ribbon as the moment they will remember.
- **Everyone complained about the same things:**
  - the same phrases and the same polished way of talking, repeated through the whole book;
  - the new side-stories in chapters XII and XIII, which open just before the ending.
- **Halden works as the villain.** All five quoted "The transfer was the least I could do," and all five found him chilling. Four of the five were upset that nobody in the book ever holds him to account.
- **The top change:** say the repeated phrases and beats less often, and let more of the characters sound like themselves.

## Scores

| Reader | Who they are | Score | In a line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ana | Nurse, 34, reads for character | 7 | "The heart of this book is a 9." It loses points for "how much puzzle scaffolding surrounds that heart." |
| Tom | Engineer, 52, checks the logic | 7 | "On evidence it plays fair, better than most crime fiction I watch." |
| Priya | Film student, 23, reads for structure | 7 | "On structure alone it's closer to an 8.5... On pacing it's closer to a 6." |
| Joe | Retired bus driver, 67, reads thrillers | 6 | "The book kept giving me little oddities when I wanted the boy." |
| Lena | Philosophy teacher, 41, reads for belief | 8 | It would start "the best argument we've had in years" at her church book group. |

**Average 7.0. Middle score 7.** The spread is narrow. Nobody disliked the book, and nobody thought it was finished as it stands.

Every reader gave the same kind of warning when recommending it: it suits patient readers, not people who want pace. Four of them (Ana, Tom, Priya, Lena) compared it to *Piranesi*. Ana and Joe said the people it would mean most to are those who have lost someone and never had a grave to visit. Lena added a caution for exactly those readers: the last chapter "gives what life almost never does, and some readers will be hurt by that rather than consoled."

## Where the panel agreed

- **The book's heart is grief, not the puzzle.** Asked what the book was about, all five gave nearly the same answer: a sister grieving a brother with no body, and learning to keep something instead of demanding an answer. Nobody said it was mainly about time travel.
- **The characters they loved were the side characters.** Priska, Melek, Márton and Seher were named again and again. Nilay was "admired more than loved" (Ana's words, echoed in some form by all five). Joe: "I wanted her to shout once."
- **Grown-up Emre speaks too much like the letters.** All five, independently, picked the same single line as the only moment the boy came through: "I have never once had shoes." That is a striking agreement.
- **Halden frightens them.** They called him "chilling because it's polite" (Priya), "cold, and I resented him" (Joe), and a man who "fascinates me, and I don't like him" (Lena). Tom: "That's a crime."
- **The italic letters were the best puzzle.** All five enjoyed working out, slowly, that the letters come from the far side of scenes they had already read. Joe: "When I twigged that one I laughed out loud."
- **Márton's death was seen coming, and that was fine.** All five knew he wouldn't come back by the end of chapter IX. Most felt the dread was worth more than the surprise.

## Where the panel disagreed

- **The fingerprint and the lab certificate.** This is the biggest split.
  - Tom thought it was "the best crime-fiction beat in the book." The prints were taken "in plain sight at the co-op table."
  - Ana and Lena felt the paperwork spoiled the wonder. Ana: "The paperwork turned wonder into proof." Lena: "Philosophically, the book blinked."
  - Priya and Joe simply felt they were told twice. Priya: "The document is the stronger beat. Drop the narrated line."
- **How much should be explained.** Tom wanted more. He wanted the book to answer its own physicist, who said "find the fuel bill." Ana, Priya and Lena wanted less, and all three disliked Emre's explanation speech. Joe was glad the book finally said "time travel" plainly.
- **The small changing numbers.** These are the air shaft's timing drifting from 18 to 20 minutes and back, the elevation changing by 10 cm, and the watches moving wrists.
  - Lena and Priya loved noticing them.
  - Joe liked spotting them but didn't know what they meant.
  - Tom felt two of the three were set up and then dropped.
  - Ana found them "a checklist left for puzzle readers."
- **Márton's unclear death.** Lena: of all the places the book refuses to judge, this is where the refusal "felt like courage rather than evasion." Ana: "I wanted to grieve him and was being asked to decode him."
- **The letters dated before the events they answer.** Joe enjoyed "every one of those." Tom counted six and was "braced for it" by the fourth.
- **The switch to "you" in the last chapter.** Priya thought it was "jarring in a good way." Ana and Joe were briefly lost ("Who's 'you'?").

## The moments that worked for nearly everyone

Each of these was praised by at least four of the five:

1. **The mother and the ribbon (XIV).** Five of five. "The best shot in the book, and nobody speaks in it" (Priya). "The best moment in the book, and it's barely a sentence" (Tom). Lena cried at the ribbon, "as the sealed sheet said Nilay would."
2. **The 1999 flashback (II):** the slippers, the red ribbon, the humming that doesn't stop for his name. Five of five. Joe: "the hair went up on my arm."
3. **Priska's father's cough at 05:40 (V):** "the cleanest reading of her career and the only dishonest page in it." Five of five.
4. **The breach (VII):** the camera found standing upright and wiped, and "Is the fire mountain awake?" Five of five.
5. **Melek's confession (XI):** "If it was not him, then I have been faithful to a room." Five of five, including Tom and Priya, who otherwise found her an "aphorism machine."
6. **The margin note "4–5.ix. Kept the hours. A.H." (IV).** Five of five sat up at it. Priya called it "the best single cut in the book."
7. **Halden's line "The transfer was the least I could do" (XIII),** and his question "Would you want it explained, if explaining it ended it?" Both were quoted by all five.
8. **Seher's "Wanting is also a gas"** and her "I am thirty-four. What am I supposed to do now?" Ana is thirty-four: "I put the book down for a minute there."
9. **Márton going down alone at the end of IX.** Four of five.

## Where attention drifted

These are listed by how many readers drifted there:

| XIII: the List, the second Malta trip, the drowned chapel | 5 of 5 | everyone |

| XI: the tourist queue, Recep's price list, the traffic correlation | 4 of 5 | all but Lena |
| The "three ordinary explanations" routine after every strange event | 3 of 5 | Ana, Priya, Joe |

| VIII: the committee and the lab results | 3 of 5 | Ana, Joe, Lena |

The pattern: the reading is strong in I–II, V, VII and XIV. It sags in XI–XIII, just when readers wanted the book to narrow toward Emre. Ana put it this way: "the book was opening new doors just when I wanted it to narrow." Joe said something similar: "the question I cared about, what happened to Emre, goes quiet for most of the middle."

The drowned chapel itself was not disliked. Ana called it "haunting on its own terms," and Tom called it "a moving fable." The trouble is its length and its place in the book.

Guessing early cost the book almost nothing. What readers resented was being told the same thing twice, not guessing it.

## Confusions that paid off, and ones that didn't

**Paid off:**

- **Who writes the letters.** Five of five. It was the book's best slow reward.
- **The air shaft's timing drifting and returning.** Four of five were pleased; Ana wasn't.
- **Who the people in the polished room are.** Tom worked out from the numbers that the woman is about two and a half metres tall. Priya: "I'm still not sure, and I like that I had to work for it." This is exactly the "never quite sure" effect your brief asks for.
- **The early-dated letters leading up to "The transfer was the least I could do."** It paid off for Joe and Ana, though Tom was tired of the trick by then.

**Did not pay off:**

- **The chapter VI letter says the "boy with the sun in his hand" slept by the lamps two nights.** On the page, Yusuf went down for one morning. Four of five could not make this fit. Ana: "I couldn't place" it. Joe: "I couldn't make it square." Lena: "a dropped stitch, not a mystery." Tom took it as intended, but saw it as proof the book has no steady rule for time. The cold full teacup is meant to be the clue, and nobody connected the two.
- **Márton's watches on the wrong wrist, and stopped at 03:12 and 05:41.** Four of five tried to assemble a meaning and couldn't. Priya: "For the most emotional death in the book, I wanted at least one more clue." Only Lena was happy to leave it as mood.
- **"The grandparents dug upward."** Tom and Lena each read it three times and gave up. Lena thinks it may be "one of the best ideas in the book, and it is buried too deep."
- **Halden's nature.** Is he one of the far keepers? He appears in a 1949 photo and never ages. Joe and Lena wanted at least a hint of which way to lean.
- **The chapter X letter about a surveyor who dies of fever.** Joe thought it was Márton, then couldn't make it fit.
- **Tom's two logic points.** He was the only reader to raise them, but both are real on the page:
  - **The grandmother's wall clock.** It "was an hour behind" every time she came up from a night below. The clock stayed at home, so it is not clear why it would lose time.
  - **The sealed box.** Nilay finds a sheet dated the fifth in her own hand. She then writes it out again, and both copies go into a drawer. Nothing ever goes back into a sealed box, so the found sheet has no source. The book notices the two copies ("differed in nothing she could find but their creases") and moves on. Tom: "In a loop story this is the one thing that has to close."

## Small wording and timing points

**These actually bothered readers:**

- **Stock phrases, repeated.** Five of five. Tom counted them, and I checked his counts against the book. "The whole of" appears 31 times, and "I did not follow past the lamp. I never do" appears 7 times. Readers also named "two breaths," "flat on the palm," "oil to the knuckle," "past the name, nothing," "which is the whole of it" and "the way X does Y." Priya: "Cut the other instances by half and it would land harder." She means Emre's "I never do," which "sounded like quotation, because it was," and which is the one repeat that pays off.
- **Everyone talks alike.** Ana and Lena said this outright, and Tom called the prose "a chant". Ana: "In the letters it's liturgy and it's beautiful. Across 50,000 words of prose it becomes a lacquer." Lena: "liturgy works because the rest of life isn't liturgy." Ana singles out Yusuf's lowercase statement as the one real change of voice: "it felt like opening a window."
- **Emre's explanation speech** ("a road that's long instead of far"). It bothered Ana, Tom, Priya and Lena. Tom: "the one scene where I wanted anything but lore."
- **Naming "time travel" and "giving the words back."** Ana: it "clanked." Tom: "Commit or don't." Joe, on the other hand, was glad of it.
- **The handprint is told more than once.** Emre hints at it, then the narration says the print "had always been hers," then the certificate arrives, then the fingerprint note. Four of five noticed the repetition.
- **Too-pleased lines:** "Oxygen debt writes sermons" (Lena), "the moon's laundry," which appears twice (Joe, Priya), and "where wounds are legally inflicted" (Joe).
- **"Havva Nine" and "Fatma Nine."** Ana and Joe both thought "Nine" was a surname or a number. It is never explained that it means grandmother. Joe also didn't know "kızım," or that "the slope" meant Halden's handwriting.
- **Melek's "hers for forty years before it was mine" (XI letter)** sits in the same chapter as "It chose me sixty years ago." It tangled Priya. Two critics caught this back in round 4, and it was never fixed.
- **Single-reader catches, all confirmed on the page:**
  - "Nobody counted twice" appears twice in one paragraph (Priya).
  - The flashback slips into the present tense: "Nilay has never asked her directly. Halime is alive" (Priya).
  - Two chapters are called "The Threshold" and two "The Measure," which Joe took for a printing mistake.
  - The recognition moment, when Nilay says "Emre," breaks into a long bracketed aside about the name. Lena: "the one beat I most wanted left clean."
- **Timing:**
  - The chapter X title spoils Márton's death (Priya).
  - Leyla Hanım's death "drops in from nowhere" (Joe, Priya).
  - Chapter XI crams Melek's collapse, the hospital, the confession and her death so close together that Lena had to re-sort them.

**Nobody noticed:**

- The small leftovers the critics flagged in the last round and left for this test:
  - the sitting-night tally in V, given before Havva's night and repeated unchanged after it;
  - "the little knife from Melek's oilcloth" in XIV, which never came with the notebook;
  - the XII sentence whose verb has no object ("to hear a flat young voice in his own accent say,").

  Not one reader mentioned any of them.
- More generally, none of the readers raised the kind of fine continuity arithmetic the revision rounds spent their last passes on. The exception is Tom, who checked dates and weekdays and found them all correct ("Somebody did their homework"). What readers noticed was repetition, pacing and voice.

## How the reactions line up with your brief

- **Time travel as the last reveal.** It is the last thing confirmed. Three readers suspected "time runs wrong" early, but that didn't spoil it, because the confirmation still came at the end.
- **The four explanations (fumes, the dead, a parallel world, a wormhole).**
  - Fumes and the dead landed hard with everyone.
  - The parallel world ("two builds," 40 or 41 rooms, "different build?") was noticed by the careful readers.
  - The wormhole is the one readers pushed back on. Marques's lecture felt like "another genre walking into the room" (Ana) and like "the book taking out insurance with physics" (Lena).
  
  Your brief requires all four, so the answer is to shorten the lecture, not remove it.
- **People from the far future, never certain.** This works as intended. Readers ranged from "fairly sure" (Tom) to "not sure, and I like that" (Priya) to "one mystery too many" (Joe).
- **Belief and science.** This lands. Lena will use "When the mask works and the visitation stops, what did we cure?" in class. Seher's "It costs the question" was called "the best short account of faith I have read in a novel in years."
- **A villain like Johan.** This works. All five found Halden quietly frightening, and almost everything he does happens off the page or through paperwork. Tom has one complaint: Halden also delivers every document the plot needs, so "I could see the author's hand up his sleeve."
- **Impact and consequence.** This is the gap. Four of five felt the book lets Halden off. More on this in change 3.

## The three changes the panel most supports

**1. Say the repeated things less often, and let people sound different.** All five asked for this. This was the reviewer's repeated point that earlier rounds left for this test, and every reader has now confirmed it.
- Keep the letters as they are. Every reader loved their voice.
- Outside the letters, cut the stock phrases roughly in half. Start with "the whole of," "two breaths," "flat on the palm" and "I never do," so that Emre's "I never do" in XIV is heard fresh.
- Drop some of the "three ordinary explanations" routines and a few of the early-dated letters.
- Give a few characters who aren't keepers a plainer, less polished way of speaking: Kaya Bey, Marques, Márton in his banter, and some of Halden.

**2. Shorten the side-trips in XII and XIII, and some of XI.** All five drifted in XII and XIII, and four drifted in XI. This was the "chapter XIII is dense" point also left for this test.
- Cut the consultancy emails down to the one line readers kept: "Four million includes the state."
- Compress the List and the drowned chapel to their strongest pieces: the lamp rowed out by boat, and the founder's unsent letter.
- Thin Recep's price list and the traffic correlation in XI.

Everyone's aim was the same: get back to Emre sooner.

**3. Let one person say to Halden's face what he did.** Four of five asked for this, in strong words.
- Ana: "I wanted one person to be angry."
- Tom: it was "the one scene I wanted and didn't get."
- Joe: "I wanted her to throw the cold tea at him."
- Lena wished the book's idea of freedom "had been asked of Halden, and not only granted to keepers."

At present Nilay swallows the accusation twice, because "accusations want a court." One spoken sentence would do it. It should not be a trial, and he should not be punished; his untouchability is what makes him work, and he should stay that way. The sentence could come from Nilay, or from the mother. It would also answer the complaint all five made about Nilay being "behind glass," and it serves your brief's "impact and consequence." One caution: this reverses a deliberate choice in the book, and Priya did not ask for it. It is your call.

**Close behind, and cheap:**
- **Confirm the handprint once.** Keep the certificate and the fingerprint note, because your brief asks for a confirmation at the end. Cut the narrated "which had always been hers..." line, so the paper does the work.
- **Trim Emre's explanation speech,** and give him one or two more lines as the boy he was.
- **Fix three small things:**
  - explain "Nine" once;
  - make the chapter VI letter's "two nights" fit Yusuf's one morning, or cut it to one;
  - fix "forty years" against "sixty years" in XI.

## How far to trust this panel

- **The quotes are real.** I checked 40 of the quotes in these reports against the finished book, and every one is there, word for word apart from capital letters. Tom's word counts also check out.
- **Every point I've called real above is on the page.** I checked each one I list as real (the two sealed-box copies, the wrist change, the clock, "Nine" never being explained, the doubled "counted twice," the tense slip, the repeated chapter titles) against the book.
- **This panel scored lower than the earlier panel run by MiMo (file 37).** That panel averaged 7.9 against 7.0 here. Both panels agree that the ribbon, chapter II and Melek are the book's heart. Both agree that the measurement sections drag and the repetition wears.
- **Where this panel adds something:**
  - The earlier panel said all five readers were blindsided by the fingerprint. Here, one reader saw it coming by chapter X, two by XIII, and four of five felt it was told too many times.
  - The earlier panel had two readers say XII ran long. Here all five drifted in both XII and XIII.
  - The earlier panel split on Halden: two found him unsettling and two called him "a function." Here all five found him frightening, and four wanted him held to account. Nobody on the earlier panel raised that.
  - Only this panel caught the "two nights" letter and Tom's two logic points (the clock and the sealed box).
- **This panel backs the earlier rulings against MiMo as a critic.** For six rounds MiMo argued that "Halden is not frightening." All five readers here found him frightening. MiMo did get one thing right: "chapter XI sags" matches four of the five readers here.
