# I. The Lamps Are Old

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me:*

*I taught the child today, and I will set it down the way I said it, so that it stays said.*

*First, the lamp is not a light. A light you strike for yourself, and it is finished when you are. A lamp is an errand. Someone set it for you, and you will set it for someone, and the dark between the two of you is only the road the errand travels.*

*Watch my hands, I told her, and not the flame. The flame will lie to you about being difficult. The hands do not know how.*

*The oil goes to the first knuckle of the thumb and no further. Oil past the knuckle is for the greedy or the frightened, and the lamp can tell the two apart, though I never managed it. The wick is trimmed with the nails, pinched, never cut. What is cut bleeds. What is pinched remembers.*

*The flame you carry cupped and low, near the body, the way you carry a thing you are only holding for someone: as if it were yours. You offer it to the wick the way bread is offered, not pushed. Hold it near, and wait, and the wick comes to it. Then the lamp took the dark a spoonful at a time. That is the true speed of every morning there has ever been. The child wanted it faster. They all want it faster. Faster is how you learn the smell of your own eyebrows.*

*Doors go down and rooms go along, and the count of one is never traded for the count of the other; keep them like two names you must not call by the wrong one in the house of a proud family. The round is this: the near rooms first, then the far, then the deep, then the near again, so that the last thing in your eyes on the climb is something small and close. You count as you go. Counting is not arithmetic. Counting is noticing, with numbers, so the rooms know they have been seen. When the round is finished you cut the day's mark, one line, low, beside all the other lines, and you do not go looking for yours among them. The wall is long. Looking for yourself in something long is how people get lost.*

*She asked me who lit the first lamp. The question is on wrong feet, I told her. Nobody lights a first lamp. You come down and they are lit, and someone is owed a measure of oil and a thank-you, and that is every morning there has ever been. She frowned. The frown is the correct face for a true answer.*

*If company comes, you say the name of the room, and then nothing. To speak past the name is how the shamed were treated once, and we are not here for shaming. If it stays, it stays. If it goes, it goes. You are not the reason for either. You are the lamp.*

*Sleep above the fourth door when rain has been on the land. That one is not a mystery; it is weather. Water pushes the old air up, and the old air does not love you. The rules that keep the body are rules too, and first. A keeper who is not kept keeps nothing.*

*I am tired in a way sleep does not find. The room has known it longer than I have. When your turn comes, the room will know that too before you do, and will be kind about it in its way, which is stone's way, which is slow.*

*At the third lamp her hands shook, and at the fourth they did not, and at the ninth she yawned, which is the true ordination. If you want the whole of teaching in one sentence: fear spends itself where it is allowed to.*

*The oil is measured. The wicks are pinched. Before, it is dark the way it always was, and after, it is dark the same way, and between the two there will have been light for as long as my knuckles hold out, and after that for as long as yours.*

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me: begin.*

The road gave up two villages before hers, and the minibus gave up with it, and Nilay Arat got down at the edge of the mulberries with two bags, a tube of rolled plans, and the permit folded in her inside pocket, where it went soft with her own heat. June. The dust already smelled cooked. Erciyes stood up out of the haze to the east with snow on it, like a breath held a long time.

She had spent nineteen summers here as a child and then, after the summer she was eighteen, none at all. Twenty-six years of arriving as a stranger in the place that had made her, and the strangeness never lived in the village. The village was exactly itself: the same three dogs' grandchildren, the same argument at the co-op, bread and dung smoke, her mother's particular way of being glad that consisted of assigning you a bed and a towel within four minutes. Her mother had come back for good some years ago, after the last hospital winter in Kayseri, and had grown small and exact, and ruled one room. They were kind to each other across a subject they had worn smooth by avoiding it. That was the whole of it. The strangeness lived somewhere under the village, and had always lived there, and she had come, at forty-four, with a permit to dig at it.

The permit had come in March, in the Ministry's familiar folder: excavation and survey, Kırk Oda, 2025 season, Dr. N. Arat, principal investigator. And beneath the Ministry's stamp, a line she had not expected: *sponsoring institution of record — the Anchorhold Trust, Valletta.* She had applied to no trust. With the folder had come a letter, copied out by hand in a sloping clerk's hand — not printed, copied — thanking her for work she had not yet done, in those words, *in advance*, and signed *A. Halden, Registrar*. She had looked the Trust up that evening: a foundation older than the republic, registered above a pharmacy, a registry of tended sites and no publications at all. Old money liked a letterhead; she had met stranger sponsors over levees and bones. She noticed the *in advance*, and liked the letter's manners, and forgot it.

Her field had a habit she beat into every student before she let them underground: *doors are vertical, and you count them down from the surface; rooms are horizontal, and you count them along; never let the two counts touch, and never tell me a room is under anything.* Kırk Oda ran four doors down. The first was the mouth itself, a fitted tuff threshold behind the low cemetery wall where the hill folded; the fourth was the deep one, and about the fourth door the village had a saying that was less a saying than a weather rule. Along, the rooms went forty, and the name said so — *Kırk Oda*, the Forty Rooms — though she would learn that the name was less a count than a courtesy. She had grown up, summers, on a floor that had other floors under it. Every child here learned early which games were for above and which for below.

The earliest cuts went down in centuries when the ice was still leaving, contemporary with the oldest built thing anyone had ever put a name to — Göbekli Tepe, far off on the Şanlıurfa side, the hill her enemies in the discipline still would not let her finish arguing about. Her heresy was ten years old and simple: nothing about that hill said abandonment. The backfill was deliberate, careful, generation on generation of it. You bury a failure carelessly, in a season. You bury a finished thing gently, the way you close a house you mean to go on being a house. They had not abandoned it. They had retired it. Kırk Oda was the counter-case, the one nobody had ever put down: never buried, never finished, lamps lit in it through Pre-Pottery Neolithic hands, Hittite hands, Roman, Byzantine, Ottoman, and now, tonight, Melek's. Eleven thousand years, if the village women's round was what it seemed. The oldest open question in her discipline was not what the first holy places were for. It was whether anyone, anywhere, had ever simply carried one forward without once putting it down.

Melek Yılmaz was at the mouth before her, because Melek was always somewhere before her. Seventy-eight, built like a doorpost, with the site's other register in her hands: knuckles like burl, a burn gone silver across the back of the right one, nails trimmed to nothing so the wicks could be pinched. She filled each small lamp to the first knuckle of her thumb, no further, from a tin can that smelled of the press and of some older oil than this year's. She lit nine lamps and not forty, in nine niches the villagers called the grandmothers' cupboards, and over each niche the ceiling was black, and the black had depth, layer under layer under layer — the only archive in the province nobody had ever needed to open.

"Why nine?" Nilay asked, on the second evening, because she had learned to ask Melek small questions and get large answers.

"Because they are nine."

"Who are they for?"

Melek pinched a wick and considered the flame the way you consider a remark made by a child at table. "Company likes a light left on," she said. "The grandparents like company."

"And who are the grandparents?"

"Older than the question, kızım." She set the ninth lamp in its niche. "You shouted in the middle gallery when you were small. Once. The shout is probably there still. Don't shout." And then, at the fourth door, in passing, as a housekeeping rule: "When the rains come you sleep above this. Water pushes the old air up and the old air doesn't know you."

That night, trimming wicks at her table, Melek told the clock story, meaning nothing by it. Her grandmother had kept a wall clock, a wedding piece. Every time the old woman came up from a night below, the clock was an hour behind. Every time, she set it by the church bell and thought nothing more about it, and said, *a clock is a donkey, you beat it and it walks.* "She thought nothing of it," Melek said, pinching. "I think nothing of it. You will think a great deal of it, because they pay you to think a great deal of nothing." An old clock, an old woman, a bell nobody had timed against her climbs — there was nothing in the story that required explaining, and Nilay filed it with the ninety harmless things she would carry home.

In the second week of June the muhtar called the village to the room above the co-op to discuss the dig. Selami Bey opened his file to show Nilay what the procedure required, and the procedure was already complete: household consents, forty of them, signed and stamped — dated in April. The meeting was in June. Nobody in the room found this remarkable. "The Trust's young woman came in April," the muhtar said, "before planting finished, while the road was still bad. Very polite. She filled the forms and left them with the co-op." Some remembered a clipboard. Some remembered good manners. One man remembered a scarf; his wife remembered no scarf. "People here sign in April for what they decide in June," someone said at the back. "It saves June." The room laughed, and Nilay laughed too, and only afterward, walking down, did she notice that she could not find the meeting at which the signatures had been explained — only the one at which they were counted. But backdating was the least mysterious practice in the province; paperwork here ran on its own calendar, like everything else.

On a still morning the following week, before the lamps were relit, she counted rooms for the registration form. The air below had not moved all night; the residue of yesterday's smoke hung in the galleries in shelves. Twice in the middle gallery the candle she carried lay down at knee height and stood up again when she lifted it, and she wrote *ventilation* in the margin with a pleasure she did not stop to examine. She counted openings along, going in: forty-one. She stood in the far room with the number in her mouth. She walked out counting from the other end and got forty. She counted the middle gallery a third time and got forty, and forty, and forty; and on the form, under *number of rooms*, there was a crossed-out forty-one in her own pencil, and a forty above it. She had flown in on a red-eye; she had counted a bin or a blind alcove that the far direction didn't return; the shelves of smoke made the openings lie. When she mentioned it to Melek, not seriously, Melek only said, "Forty is the polite number. Some mornings the house is bigger. You don't write those down. You embarrass the rooms."

In the evenings she entered the 1924 survey into the site register, item by item, because the ministry form wanted the historic inventory and there was nothing else to do before the team came. The old surveyor had been a lister, not a describer. *47. Stair, rock-cut, seven treads, worn. 48. Niche, sooted. 49. Niche, sooted. 50. Spirals, red ochre, three, continuous line. 51. Hand print, right, red ochre, above the first marks, lower wall. 52. Bin, plastered, empty. 53. Lamp, ceramic, handle broken.* Item fifty-one took her no longer than item fifty. Someone had wet a hand in ochre and set it on that wall before there was writing anywhere on earth to sign a thing with, and the surveyor had called it what he called the spirals and the bins, and moved on, and now so did she, typing it between fifty and fifty-two. Her own notebooks held six years of renderings of the lower wall's tally lines — short carved strokes, one beside another, thousands — which she had privately called the grandmothers' minutes, and which no one had ever read, including her. The renderings were half transcription and half what her hand did while her eye counted; she had never decided which half was the record. She typed item fifty-one. She typed item fifty-two.

The permit file wanted, as permits do, the shapes of her life, and this year the sponsor's office had pre-filled what it could, as sponsors do, in the same sloping hand as the March letter; she checked its version of her against her own. Next of kin: mother. Under *Siblings*: *Brother — missing, earthquake, September 1999 — province.* No site named. No day named, in this version; the version she had typed herself, in other seasons, had had a day. The province was large. The province contained everything. She read the line once, as you press a bruise to check that it is still yours, and closed the folder, and the folder closed the way folders do, on nothing.

The team came up on Kemal's tractor at the end of the month: the geophysicist Kállai's crates with their Swedish customs stickers, and a padded triple case he carried himself, scolding the tractor; Vogel's steel boxes stenciled with a Zürich university, and a handshake like a thesis defense; and the ministry's student, Yusuf Demir, nineteen, from the near side of Derinkuyu, who introduced himself and his phone tripod in the same breath and apologized for neither.

On the last night of June, with the dig to open at dawn, she took a borrowed lamp down to the first door and sat on the threshold to learn the place's breathing, because the ventilation plan would want it.

At the mouth of Kırk Oda the air shaft breathed. The breath went out for a long while, mild, carrying stone and cold water and something like the inside of a pocket; then a pause long enough to be mistaken for stillness; then in again, the same length, the same patience — a tide with no sea. Anyone with a watch and an hour to waste could time it, and anyone who timed it came away with the same figure: eighteen minutes, and eighteen again, obedient to nothing anyone had ever identified, keeping its own time the way the sea keeps the moon's.

She thought about where the baseline CO₂ readings should be taken, and lost that thought, and sat.

The warmth came against her right shoulder the way a cat commits itself: suddenly, entirely, with weight. The lamp stood on her left, on the stone. The village cats administered themselves to warmth wherever it pooled, and were shameless about it. The stone had drunk the sun all day above and was giving it back now like a bank. She did not turn her head. You do not turn your head on a night bus when a stranger's child falls asleep against your arm; you go still, and go careful, and carry the debt of it yourself. The lamp at her left burned small and even. The warmth at her right stayed exactly the weight of a sleeping child.

It was homesickness, she decided on the climb up. It kept homesickness's hours and homesickness's appetite. Only it stood on nothing. Homesickness has a floor — the place, the table, the bread. This had the place, the table, the bread, and under them the rooms, and under the rooms the deep air going in and out, in and out, on a tide with no sea — and somewhere in the middle of all that, a warmth leaning its whole weight on her shoulder, and nothing under any of it but the kept dark.


# II. The Threshold

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me:*

*The water came at the land in the night, and the land took it loudly, the way it always has. I was at the lamps in the deep rooms when the stone of the long room came home to its socket. The stones are faithful, not clever. A faithful door shuts on whatever is behind it, and does not count what that is.*

*I did not hurry. Hurry is for the surface, where things can still be changed. I finished the lamp I was at — oil to the knuckle, wick pinched true — and then I took the light along, and there was a weeping ahead of me, going and coming, the kind that has been crying longer than you have been listening.*

*A girl. How she came to be below I did not ask, and did not let my face ask. A question is a lamp you hold up on somebody, and the young cannot stand the light of it. She had wedged herself where the wall gives its shoulder, the place the frightened find without being shown, and she was shaking like the last leaf a storm means to save.*

*I set the lamp down where she could see it if she chose to see it, and I said the name of the room, once, and after the name, nothing. To speak past the name is how the shamed were treated once, and she had done nothing, and had been done-to enough.*

*She asked who was there. I gave her the name of the room again. It is a true answer. It has always been a true answer.*

*Then I sat where the wall lets a body sit, at the good distance, which is farther than your wanting and nearer than hers, and I hummed. Not sang. Singing is a performance and costs the one who listens. I hummed the way you hum for a child that is not yours: the tune everyone's mother used, the one nobody owns, low, with nothing in it to follow but the going on.*

*The mountain drank all night. In such weather the rooms are the throat of it, and the water sounds in the stone like footsteps that never arrive anywhere. She flinched at it, and at the thunder, and toward the small hours at nothing, which is the door the spent go out by. Weeping spends itself where it is allowed to, same as fear. I kept the lamp and let her spend.*

*Near morning she came closer by degrees, the way cold walks a body over. In the end she slept against my side, entirely, all at once, the way the tired pay down a debt they have carried too long. I did not touch her beyond being warm and being there. Comfort that lays hold of you is only weather with manners.*

*When the grey came in at the top of the doorway and lay down along the floor, the stone took one finger, as the stones do once the wind has finished its argument, and she went up into the grey, and I did not follow past the lamp. The round was half done and the day's mark not cut, and there is no mark for a night, and none for two. One line, low, with the others. The wall is not a diary. It is a handrail.*

*She will keep that night wrong, and be kept by it. That is allowed. The room is in every story about the room, and needs no telling.*

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me: when the stone shuts, the round continues. That is the whole of what I know about doors.*

The Ministry's envelope came up the hill on the post moped in the second week of the dig and held the 1998 survey, folded for twenty-seven years into quarters that had become opinions. Yusuf flattened the sheets under mugs and the tin of nails while Nilay weighted the 1924 ink copy beside them, and for a while the dig-house table was a century and a quarter wide.

The 1924 man had been a lister. The 1998 team had been measurers — four days, one quiet October, a joint inventory of the province's rock-cut sites that was never published past its own mimeograph. Between them they agreed to the millimeter on the seven worn treads, the spirals, the bins, the fall of the middle gallery true enough to overlay against a lamp. And in the middle gallery, between the far niches, where 1924 had drawn solid rock and written nothing at all, 1998 had drawn a chamber. Numbered. With a niche in it.

She did what a professional does: hated both documents equally for a morning, then forgave both equally. The '24 surveyor had tired — twice in his inventory the same line twice, *niche, sooted, niche, sooted* — and a man who can write the same line twice can pass a doorway in the dark of his own habit. The '98 draftsmen had corrected in an office in Ankara from somebody's field squeeze, and offices invent smooth rock. Or the ceiling had shut a room sometime before 1924 and a hand had cleared it since; tuff closes and opens like a poor man's purse. On Thursday she went and looked. The gallery gave her forty, twice, on a loud morning with the generator running, and the stretch where 1998's chamber belonged was a wall like the other walls, rounded at every corner — corners are a luxury of hard stone — with a jamb-shaped hollow worn into it at knee height, worn, not cut, the way feet wear a threshold rather than tools a door. There was nothing in any of it that a tired surveyor and an Ankara office could not account for between them. Her own crossed-out forty-one sat in the site register two pages away, and she chose not to set the three numbers in a row. Nothing had ever come of a row.

The registered letter was at the co-op, in the drawer under the ledger weights, because registered post in the village was a social occasion and the muhtar wanted witnesses. It was thin. *In connection with licensed activity at the registered site, the undersigned file is reopened for administrative review. No new findings are anticipated.*

*Enclosures: gendarmerie summary, 1 leaf; correspondence, Valletta, 7.ix.1999, 1 leaf — not reproduced.*

And under the stamp, the whole of it, the way it had been the whole of it for twenty-six years: *Male, thirteen. Missing. Earthquake. September 1999. Province.* The copy she had been allowed to read at nineteen, in the gendarmerie, in a room smelling of paraffin and gun oil, had carried a day. Somewhere between that room and this drawer the day had worn off the file — in copying, in re-typing, in the ordinary weathering of paper that nobody is ever blamed for. She read *Valletta* twice, the way you read a name you have lately seen on a letterhead, and put it down to the fact that half the world's paper passes through somebody's island.

She took the letter out to the flat stone below the mulberries, and the summer of 1999 came up the hill at her.

The shaking that year had all belonged to the northwest; the province caught only its hem. Chimney feet cracked, a wall of the old school let go its plaster in one sheet like a snake, and the province called it the earthquake summer anyway, because everything that happened in it was the earthquake's fault, the way everything is the weather's fault. Her mother's heart went wrong at the end of August — grief has organs, whatever the textbooks claim — and Nilay, eighteen, counted her mother's pills into a weekly box before she knew that counting pills was a thing the well did for the unwell, and Emre, thirteen, carried water and checked roofs and was glorious with usefulness.

He could whistle on a blade of grass held in both thumbs. He raced paper boats in the irrigation channel and knew every thief-eddy where the channel stole them, and could stand at the take-out grating naming the ones he would get back. As a small child he had been afraid of the deep rooms, honestly afraid, wet-palmed; at thirteen he performed contempt for the fear, loudly, the way thirteen performs everything. He knew every cave mouth in the thorn hills — the herders' boys did, it was their alphabet — and that summer he had begun, off and on, to say that when he was older he would map them all, properly, like his sister.

The fourth of September was a Saturday of tomatoes and white cheese and the door open to the yard. After the meal he went out in his house slippers, to see about a kid that had not come in with the evening flock, which meant he meant to be gone ten minutes. Two people saw him at the fountain at last light. At twenty past nine a small shock went through, the land clearing its throat, and the cracked chimney shed grit down the outside wall like a slow hand letting go. By midnight the family was walking the lane with a lamp. By morning the village was walking everywhere. By noon there were gendarmes, and dogs from Kayseri that worked beautifully until the thorn and then sat down in it and would not, and men sounding the wells with a stone on a string, and the river walked to the weir, and the dry lake gridded with string, and the cave mouths of the province opened one by one and shouted into, and the shouts went nowhere, or went and stayed.

Kırk Oda was one mouth among the many, and not the first. On the sixth the sergeant and two men went down with electric torches, and Melek went ahead of them the whole way, so that the rooms would meet her before they met the light. At the head of the deep stair she stopped them with the rule — it had rained on the fifth; water pushes the old air up; the old air does not love you — and the sergeant, who had a mother, accepted it as a toll. The log says: *subterranean complex, upper levels, examined. No findings.* *No findings* was the refrain of that whole fortnight, entered by careful sergeants in careful hands, against well after well and room after room, and it was the kindest sentence the summer produced and the cruelest.

Nilay became, at eighteen, a clerk of the search. She bought a roll of red ribbon at the bazaar and tied a strip to everything the looking had entered — thorn bush, door iron, post, mouth — so that the looked-into could be told from the not-yet, and she kept the master list in a school notebook, numbered, dated, in the hand she still has. The first register she ever kept was the absence of her brother.

The storm came on the third Monday and stood on the land. The fourth of September had left her one entry: past the middle gallery, beyond the last lamp niche and below the fourth door, the low corridor the gendarmes had not walked — stopped by the rule, and by its narrowness. She went at dusk, before the rain, taking the tin lamp from the niche at the mouth without asking, and she filled it herself, past any knuckle, greedily, the way the frightened do.

The stone at the corridor's mouth swung for one finger, heavy as a decision, light as a door. The way was low; she went crouched, then on hands, the lamp ahead of her, and the dust of the floor lay thick enough to read, and she read it with the flame: nothing, or nothing she had the eyes for. The room at the end had a sooted ceiling and one shelf niche and a dry floor with cracks she walked on her knees and checked with her fingers, and she said his name twice, quietly, the way you say a name in a house where someone might be sleeping. Then the storm arrived overhead, and the whole hill began to drink, and the rooms were the throat it drank by.

Going back, the stone had come home in its socket. She pushed with the flat of her hand, the heel of her hand, her shoulder; the pivot gritted and kept its opinion. Wet tuff swells. She had known it in her hands, that night, before she knew it in words. The flame lay down and would not stand — the still air pooling low, the bad breath the rule was about — and the third time it lay down she pinched it out with her nails, doing without knowing she had learned it the thing she had watched Melek do all her childhood.

She cried in the dark with her whole body, the way she had not permitted herself in front of anyone since the fourth, and when the crying was spent the warmth arrived, the way it had arrived all her life at the mouths of rooms: suddenly, entirely, with weight, at her right side. And a little way off, near the stone, someone was humming. The tune was the one everyone's mother used — hers, and not hers, as she would one day be told — and it had no words in it, and it paused where the words would be.

She said, "Emre," once.

The humming did not change and did not stop, not for her word, and that was either nothing or everything, and in the dark the two were the same size. She slept.

The grey came down the corridor thin as skimmed milk, and the stone took one finger, and the morning outside was washed and ringing, birds loud in the mulberries. The lamp stood where she had left it, cold, its wick folded black over — pinched, or folded; a spent flame folds a wick and a keeper pinches one, and she had never since decided which she had been looking at. And on the door's iron, where going in she had tied a strip of the red so the way back would have a color in it, there was nothing. Wind, or anyone.

Her absence had been discovered at dawn — the village had spent one bad hour searching for the searcher — and at the bread queue the comfort required a body, and the women supplied one. Halime had been below that night, someone's cousin said; everyone knew how Halime was about the rooms. Halime, who answered everything, answered nothing, and so was never cleared. Nilay has never asked her directly. Halime is alive, or was at the last accounting; the one question that would clear her has sat in Nilay for twenty-six years like a stone in a fruit, and taking it out now would only cut her.

Her mother, that night, was four floors up in Kayseri with wires on her chest. When Nilay finally asked her, two years ago, on one of the cardiology corridors, which ninni it had been, the one from the dark, her mother looked at her with the mild contempt of the convalescent. "Which ninni, kızım? I sang the one everyone sings. It was never mine to have." So: Halime, or any of the mothers, humming to a shut-in girl because someone should. Or a mind building a mother out of warmth and tin-oil and the smell of a wick, because it was eighteen, and had buried nobody, and lost everything, and could not get out of a room. There was a third way to hold that night. Her body had voted for it at the time — sitting still, going careful, carrying the debt of it — and she had never once let it become words, and she was not going to begin now, at forty-four, in a licensed capacity, with the Ministry's stamp drying on her.

In the evening she lettered the footnote into the site register in her driest hand: *plans of 1924 and 1998 differ as to one chamber, middle gallery; unresolved.* She fetched the first of her rendering notebooks — six years of the lower wall's tally lines, the grandmothers' minutes — and confirmed what she had suspected: neither century had troubled to mark the wall. The wall belonged to no plan.

At the mouth, Melek was at the wicks. Nilay sat with her and did not tell her about the letter, and the not-telling was easy, being old. Years ago, the only time she had ever described that night, and only the lamp part of it, Melek had heard her out and said, "You filled it badly. Past the knuckle. The greedy or the frightened," and pushed the figs at her, and that was all. And once — only once, lightly, as if asking after the harvest — Nilay had asked whether anyone else had been below that night. Melek had pinched a wick, and set it true, and said: "The rooms were full of weather."

The first night rain of July arrived while the generator was still warm, and nobody was below the fourth door, and the hill began to drink. She put the letter in the drawer with the two plans and the crossed-out count, one problem laid on another, and turned off the light, and the tune came back the way it always came back, at its own pace, pausing where the words would be. In twenty-six years she had never once let herself wonder what the words were. Sleep, and grow, the ninni says, to whoever is listening — every mother's mother's song, hers, and not hers. She lay in the dark of the dig house, not sleeping, listening to the hill drink, which was a sound, only a sound, the weather moving in the stone. Grief for the unburied keeps its own address. It does not come to you. You go to it, all your life, down and along, and the province is large, and the province contains everything.


# III. The Measure

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me:*

*The measurers come every few lifetimes, as comets come, or bad kings, and this one came in the dry season, on foot, with a rope.*

*He was polite, which the measurers mostly are. Politeness is what they bring instead of a gift, and it is accepted, as figs are accepted. I watered him and set him where the wall lets a body sit, and he ate little and drank much, and talked all through the meal about the rope, which was knotted, he said, at the length of his own forearm, one knot to a forearm, and the forearm, he said, was the same as his grandfather's forearm, and the rope did not care whose. I believe this was a joke. I gave it the laughter it was owed.*

*In the morning he began. A knot to a forearm, a forearm to a step, and he went along the gallery saying numbers to himself in the voice people use for prayer and for sums, which is the same voice, and should be. I went before him with the lamp, because the rooms should meet the light before they meet the stranger in it, and I did not count. My counting is for the round, and my round was done.*

*He came to the end and had a number, and was pleased with it, and I was pleased that he was pleased, and then — because they always do it — he went back along the way he had come, counting again.*

*He came back with a different number.*

*He stood at the mouth of the gallery and looked at the rope. He counted the knots, and the knots were the number of knots there had always been. He counted them in the other direction, the way you do, and they consented to be the same number backwards, which knots will do, and rooms will not. Then he went along the gallery a third time, slower, touching each opening with his hand as a man touches the heads of children he is afraid of loving too much, and he came back with a third number, which was the second number, and not the first.*

*A string is honest, and a knot is honest, and together they had made a number that was not, and there was no one to blame anywhere in it, and he sat down among all that blamelessness like a man let go by a court that had found him innocent of everything.*

*He asked me how many rooms there were. The measurers always ask, at the end, in the voice of a man asking the way to a place he has already passed. I told him the number that is given, which is a true number, as far as it goes, and farther than it goes it is not false either; it is polite. He wrote it somewhere inside himself, and his shoulders came down off his ears, and I do not say he slept well, but he slept.*

*He wanted to know why his count would not stand. He put the question three ways, and each way was a good way, and I did not answer any of them, and I did not tell him why, because the why is not for saying. Not because it is a secret. A secret is a thing withheld, and nothing is withheld down here; the rooms keep nothing from anyone, being rooms. But the why does not fit in a saying. If he had stayed thirty years, and kept the lamps, and eaten at my fire, one morning the why would have arrived whole, without words, the way the round arrives; and he would have stopped asking, without ever having been answered. That is the only teaching this house has, and it cannot be given. It can only be grown into. And the measurers have ropes, and seasons.*

*He left in the morning, richer by one polite number, poorer by one certainty, which is the fare this house charges. I did not follow him past the lamp. I never do.*

*The day's mark is cut, one line, low, beside the others. The rooms were the size they have always been, which is not a size.*

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me: when the count will not close, finish the round anyway. The mark is for the day, not for the number.*

Dr. Márton Kállai wore three watches, all of them atomic, all of them on his left wrist, where the artery is closest. Blood heat, he told anyone who asked. An oscillator is an animal of temperature, and the wrist is the cheapest constant oven in the world. This was true, and it was not the reason. The reason had a year in it, and the year was 1997, and he did not volunteer it. At conferences, when the watches drew the question, he said, "They keep one another honest," which was a joke, and also the nearest thing to the truth he permitted himself in public. Two clocks, he had decided long ago, can only argue. Three are a committee, and a committee can be outvoted.

He was sixty-eight, a big man gone thin, with the swollen knuckles of a life spent carrying delicate things badly suited to hands. He had brought the triple case up the last stretch of hill himself, having scolded Kemal's tractor into obedience short of it, and he let nobody else lift the gravimeter but Yusuf, once, watched, and Priska, never. In the first days, while Nilay's trenches were still strings and intention, he walked the hill with the instrument cradled like a christened child and put his stakes in around the mouth and down the middle gallery and along the deep stair to the threshold of the fourth door.

Melek watched him level the instrument on the flat stone at the mouth.

"Does it have a name?" she asked.

"It has a serial number."

"Poor thing," she said, and went in to her wicks.

He worked mornings, when the hill was still honest with its heat. Every reading was washed before it was kept: he subtracted instrument drift, and the tides of the solid earth, and the moon's pull twice daily, and the faint gravity of the sea he could not see — every number he had ever loved had first been cleaned of the moon, and he trusted no figure that had not been. Then he built the hill in the computer from Nilay's two plans, and here his contempt woke, because the plans disagreed. A century apart, agreeing to the millimeter on seven worn treads and a set of bins, and differing by one entire room in the middle gallery. He asked her to arbitrate. "They disagree by one room, Doctor Kállai," she said. "Arbitrate with your own instruments." So he modeled both hill versions, the 1924 hill and the 1998 hill, and subtracted each from his own.

Both hills left the same thing behind.

The residual sat under the deep rooms and past them, farther down than the plans went, below the fourth door and beyond the last mapped floor: a gravity deficit of thirty-four microgal, give or take the moon's laundry. He converted it the way old men convert everything, into buildings. It was a hollow of some thousands of cubic meters. A nave's worth of absence, deeper than the fourth door — the village church, nave, not towers, and the rock on every plan pretending to be solid where it stood.

He was rigorous about hating it, which is how he loved things. The water table, then: it fell all July, the co-op kept a log, the muhtar's nephew lowered a float every Monday and Friday into a school notebook going back to a flood — desaturating rock loses mass, and he needed every microgal of it; but the well's fall was worth perhaps four, and in the wrong part of the hill. Drift, then: he re-occupied the base station at the mouth each evening, and the drift curled inside its tolerance like a sleeping animal. The density tables, then: tables written by men who had never met this hill — so he weighed and dunked offcut blocks from Nilay's first spoil heap and built his own table, and the anomaly sat down from thirty-four microgal to twenty-nine and refused to go lower.

"Groundwater," Priska said over his shoulder. "Or your tables. Or — forgive me, Professor — your faith."

"Your air," he said, "is the least interesting fluid on earth and the most guilty one."

Into the site register, in the column for remarks, he wrote the sentence he would later be unable to defend and unable to retract: *Mass absent. Extent unknown. Cause unknown.* And for the elevation reduction he read the figure aloud, slowly, to be witnessed, while Yusuf filmed it for his own obscure purposes: "Threshold, fourth door: one thousand one hundred forty-two point six meters above the sea." He made Yusuf repeat it back. Figures that are spoken twice, he believed, are harder for the world to quietly amend.

The triangle he set in the middle gallery, in the stretch where the two plans had quarreled, out of spite. Three tripods, three corner prisms — one at the stair head, one at the far niches, one past the sooted lamps — and the laser ranger on a crate he had carried from Uppsala for the purpose. A hundred paces of perimeter. Each side measured three times, forward and back. And the triangle would not close: eleven centimeters short, obediently, every session, as if the gallery were saving the distance for something.

Priska arrived for the refraction verdict with her stratification maps rolled under her arm like warrants. Her air, all July, had been plotted like weather: still days pooled dead air in the low rooms in shelves, her candles lay down at knee height on schedule, on demand, at printed times — her anomalies kept appointments, which was the whole difference between her science and his. Air bends light, she said; layered air bends it in layers; his beam was crossing a lasagna. He computed the gradient her theory required and found the gallery would have to be lying in temperature strata like soup, forty degrees from floor to ceiling, which would have cooked the tuff and them with it. They ran the generator to churn the air, and the triangle stayed eleven centimeters short. He cleaned the prisms with lens tissue and, once, her breath, which he logged as a controlled variable and did not log further. He set the tripod feet on ceramic tiles so the tuff could not eat them. On a still night, when the whole hill held its air like a held note, the closure came back ten point eight, and then, on remeasurement, eleven, and he refused to average on principle, because an average is where anomalies go to be forgiven.

"Eleven centimeters in a hundred meters," Priska said. "I have socks with more error than that."

"Your socks, Priska, have never once been asked to close. If they were, you would buy better socks, and I would lose my only honest colleague."

They had been conducting this war for fifteen years, in corridors at conferences from Vienna to Reykjavík, and neither had ever once yielded the other an inch in public or missed a session where the other spoke. She sent him her reprints with insults penciled in the margins; he kept them in a folder she had labeled, in her own hand, DOUBT. He trusted her disbelief more than most people's belief. The cruelty between them was a way of keeping faith, and both of them knew it, and neither had ever said so, there being no need.

The singing room he found by accident, placing a station, when his own footsteps came back to him with the room attached to them. He hummed, the way a man hums who is alone and testing, and found the note where the walls stood up behind his voice — a low A, one hundred and nine hertz, one hundred ten, felt in the sternum a half-second before the ear agreed. Melek called it the singing room. "We don't sing in it," she said. "It sings." He entered it in his grid as Room 9, deep series, and the number and the name took to each other at once, and by the first of August even Nilay's register said *the Ninth Room*, a christening and a baptism on the same stone. He asked to leave a tripod below the fourth door overnight. Melek looked at the sky for a while. "When the land is dry," she said, "and not a night past the first cloud." So he carried his instruments down in the mornings and up in the evenings, like a man who takes his dog to work and calls it fieldwork.

The watches agreed. That was the humiliation of July. Each dawn and each dusk he compared the three against the portable receiver at the mouth, and they agreed to fractions of a nanosecond, night after night, with a perfection he came to resent like a guest who is punctual to the minute for a month. He had spent his life measuring things too polite to refuse him — rocks, ruins, colleagues' patience. This place declined. It gave him a hollow the rock would not admit to and a triangle that was short by exactly enough, and then, when he set clocks in front of it and asked it to perform, it gave him nothing at all, beautifully, repeatably, and he had been in love before, several times, but never with anything that could say no.

In the second week he wrote to Valletta, because a sponsoring institution holds archives; sponsors always hold archives; it is what sponsoring is. The reply came by post, in due course. It was a letter copied out by hand, the whole of it, in a sloping clerk's hand — he checked it against Nilay's March letter at the dig-house table, and the slope was the slope, and a registry with a photocopier had copied two letters by hand for no reason either of them could name, and they set the two envelopes side by side and looked at them as one looks at twins. The letter itself said that the Trust held no instruments, having found the sites to require little but attendance, and enclosed what there was.

What there was: forty years of the site's annual reports. Each year, one line. *Nothing to record.* Forty sheets, one line each, in typescripts and hands that aged down the stack and never varied by a word.

Márton tabulated them, of course. One column, forty rows, the only complete time series he had ever seen of nothing. Then, since the content would not vary, a second column for the hand: typescript, typescript, a hand, and in 1999, once, the slope of the letters, and then typescript again. "A change of clerk," he said, and noted it, because he noted everything.

"That is not a dataset," Priska said. "That is a liturgy."

"Forty years of nothing," Yusuf said, filming the fan of sheets on the table, "measured once a year, never missing. That's the most consistent reading anyone's taken here."

Everybody laughed, and nobody entirely, and the sheets went into the archive box between her air maps and his triangle, which was where the summer kept everything it could not spend.

It was Yusuf, in the end, who pulled the year out of him — Yusuf, who introduced people and tripods in the same breath, and who put the camera up on the third still evening and asked, cheerfully, on the record, "Professor, the watches. Three. Why?"

"I am equipment," Márton said, "not content," and turned off nothing, because it was not his camera.

But that night, at the table, with the tea gone cold and the generator off and the hill drinking somewhere under them, he told it, flat, in the voice he used for instrument logs.

1997. A gravity survey in the Carpathian bend. A half-ruined monastery above a river — one wall standing, the undercroft dry and swept, an old monk who still kept it and fed the survey team tomatoes off his own vines and died two winters later. He had carried two portable caesium standards down into the undercroft overnight for a timing tie, ordinary procedure, the kind of thing you do to sleep well. In the morning they disagreed by eighty-seven nanoseconds. He swapped their positions and left them another night, and they disagreed again, and not in the direction of position. He reported it. The board reported back: thermal transient in one oscillator; operator handling; a firmware revision which the manufacturer confirmed existed and could not rule out. One referee used the word *wishful*, in a footnote, which is where wounds are legally inflicted. He withdrew the paper. The chair in Sweden came the next autumn, and was a good chair, and he had been reasonable about the whole affair for twenty-eight years, which is a long time to be reasonable about eighty-seven nanoseconds. The newspapers at the time had used a word he had never used and never would — a word out of physics lectures and bad films, a hole through everything — and he had spent a career declining to be the man who said it. The two clocks he still owned, retired, in a drawer in Uppsala. They had never disagreed again. It was the cruelest number he had.

"Do you think it was real?" Yusuf asked, no camera, the first fully serious sentence Márton had heard from him.

"It was measured," Márton said. "That is a different thing from real, and a different thing from false, and I have had twenty-eight years to learn the difference, and I know nothing more tonight than I knew that morning."

Nilay told the clock story then, to change the subject — Melek's grandmother, the wall clock, *a clock is a donkey, you beat it and it walks* — and Márton laughed at the donkey, properly, for the first time that evening, and wrote it in the back pages of the instrument log, where he kept things that were not yet data, between a remark of Priska's about his socks and the monk's recipe for tomatoes.

That night he sat up late with the site register. The hollow was still under the deep floors, going about its absence. The triangle was still eleven centimeters short, folded in the book like a moth. On his left wrist, where the artery is closest, the three watches ticked in committee, unanimous, to the tenth of a nanosecond, and he resented the agreement with his whole heart. He was sixty-eight years old, and he was happy, and he noted the fact in the log, because happiness, at his age, in his field, was a reading too. Then he went up to bed like a man who, in a great dark house, has heard one floorboard, far away, and has already laid out his clothes for the morning.


# IV. The Register

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me:*

*The island is warm, and the stone of it is not mountain stone. It is sea pressed into stone. Cut it and hold the cut face to the lamp and you will see the old water's weather in it still: shells small as patience, everything gone round, and round, and then gone hard. All of it was made by things agreeing together for a long time.*

*The house below this island goes down by ways that wind. The chisel went where the stone was sweetest, and so everything curves; a square room down here would be an accusation. There is salt in the air even below, arriving by roads I do not know, and I do not question the salt, because salt is older than questions.*

*The house is full as a seed is full. We do not dwell on the fullness. It is why the lamps. That is all the saying it needs.*

*One room sings, and I will set down how, so that you do not spend a season being surprised, as I did. There is a note — the chest finds it before the throat does, the way a door knows your weight before you knock — and when you give the note to the room, the room does not keep it. The room sets it down in other rooms, rooms your legs will never enter, so that the house, which is larger than any round, can hear itself being lived in. People weep in that room and do not know why. Let them. It is only the room reading them back to themselves, at the note, and not everyone can bear being read.*

*Company comes. This is what to do, and it is little, and it is everything, like all the true duties. Knock twice, with the flat of the hand, on the sounding stone, and then wait the length of two breaths, which is longer than any want. We knock so company is known. Not to summon. A summons is a demand, and demands are for the surface, where things can still be changed. A knock is a courtesy. It says: the house knows you are in it, and is not against you, which down here is the same as being glad. If company answers, you answer once, and then no more, and whatever is between you happens in the quiet after, which is where it has always happened.*

*I renewed the red today. The coils that go in and in, in and in, on the ceiling of the singing room, I wet them with the ground red, and the room took its color back the way cold hands take warmth: slowly, by degrees, without thanks.*

*A child asked me who the house was for. I said: for whoever it holds. The frown, again, is the correct face for a true answer.*

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me: knock, and wait out the waiting. If nothing answers, the knock was still heard. That is all a knock has ever been for.*

---

The two hand-copied letters lay in the site register between the plans, the March one and the July one, twins of hand and slope, and it was on account of the twins that she wrote to Valletta. A licensee is owed her sponsor's file; a registry that copies its letters by hand has a file worth the flight. And in the drawer at the dig house a gendarmerie file listed a leaf from Valletta, of September 1999, and did not reproduce it. There was also the Ministry's annex to the permit — the subsurface-sounding schedule, Márton's coring, the hollow under the deep floors — and the annex wanted the sponsor's countersignature in person, at the office of record: an unusual clause. Clauses like that are usually some dead man's habit wearing a stamp.

The reply came by post at an entirely ordinary speed, in the slope: the office would receive her on the Monday; the Trust advised the six-forty from Rome. She had told them the Monday. She had not told them the aeroplane. The six-forty was the sensible one, and sensibleness explains everything, if you let it.

Her hotel held the room, and held besides an envelope with her name in the same slope: a permit for the Hypogeum at Paola, seven-forty in the morning, an hour and twenty minutes before the first public admission, by arrangement of the Anchorhold Trust, as a courtesy to the licensee. She had asked for no courtesy. She noted it, in the way of her trade, as a stratum, and read the stratum as: institutions a century old keep keys for one another. That was all. That, and nothing.

That night she looked the name up, because names with old smells deserve their etymology read. *Anchorhold*, said the dictionary: the cell of an anchorite; and *anchorite*: one who is walled into a building, alive, for life, as an act of religion. She read it twice, and closed the phone, and said it to no one, and has not said it since.

Paola on a Monday was a working town doing its working — bread, buses, a church squared up against the sky with the hypogeum crouching at its flank like something the church was keeping an eye on. Mrs. Vella met her at the ticket desk with keys on a ring the size of a bracelet and thirty years of the rooms in her face, and the modern stair corkscrewed down through the archaeology, and the airlocks breathed them in, and then the stone took over.

It was not her stone. It was honey where her tuff is ash; it kept the chisel's opinion of it, every stroke legible, and the ceilings were rounded the way her rooms were rounded, and over the wall niches the black had depth, layer under layer, the same archive nobody had ever needed to open. The modern world had broken in by accident, cistern-cutters after water; everything since had been apology.

In the middle level was the room with the spirals, red ochre, going in and in on a ceiling low enough to worry at, and through its wall a rounded mouth, head height, giving onto the dark of the next chamber. The oracle, Mrs. Vella said. A voice put in there arrives whole in the room beyond.

"And this," she said, laying two fingers on a slab that answered them like the side of a bell, "we teach properly, because the tours teach it badly. You knock so company is known. Twice, softly — with the knuckles it's too much like a demand — and then you wait. Two breaths' worth."

"And if it answers?"

"You answer once. And then you leave it the quiet. The quiet is the point, dear. Anyone can make a noise." She smiled the smile of a woman who has watched ten visitors a day, eighty a day, make noise for thirty years. "It has answered me twice in my life, and both times there were workmen on the level below. Draw your conclusions. Everyone does."

Nilay knocked. Twice, softly, and she waited out the waiting, both breaths entire, and was surprised at her own keeping of the interval, the way you are surprised by feet that have learned a dance without you. Nothing answered. The nothing was complete, was furnished, was almost courteous itself, and she caught herself standing in it a moment longer than the experiment required, in the manner of her trade and of one or two other trades she did not name to herself.

She hummed to find the room's note and found it where the walls stood up behind her voice — sternum first, ear consenting after — and stood in Malta holding the Ninth Room's exact gift in her chest, a low A, one hundred and ten hertz, give or take the courage to claim it. Two chambers a sea apart, arrived at the same note by accident of dimension, the way two strangers' pockets can carry the same coin.

On the way up, in the empty top room where the light was being persuaded to come on, Mrs. Vella told her the story, because she was of the trade and would hear it regardless. In the war, they said, a school party came through, thirty children and their teachers, on a visit, and were never seen again. "There's no list, dear. No parish that lost a class, no complaint, no names. The archives burned in the bombing, so nothing can be checked, and nothing ever has been found, and nothing ever will be. We don't tell it on the tours now. It frightens, and fright sells, and the two have ruined enough between them." She turned her ring of keys over. "I wrote to your Trust about it once, in the eighties, wanting it struck off the record or put on it — one or the other, you understand; a person can live with either. They answered by hand. Two sentences. *The Trust does not correct what it did not record.* Very courteous. I've kept the letter half a lifetime, and it has kept me curious exactly as long."

Valletta at noon was stone and glare, and the pharmacy had a green cross and a smell of eucalyptus, and beside it a door the width of a respectable cupboard, and a stair, and at the top a brass plate: THE ANCHORHOLD TRUST, and beneath, smaller, REGISTRAR.

He opened the door at the moment knocking became necessary, so that she never quite knocked, and she wondered afterward whether that was courtesy or bookkeeping. Gray was the whole of him; he had the age of good paper, dated and sound; his voice had been poured and allowed to set. He asked after the flight, after the rooms, and then — before she had said a word of her own people — after the keeper of the lamps. Melek, is it. We corresponded in the eighties. A strong hand and a short letter. My favorite combination. He poured tea with both hands and set hers by the window and drank none himself, and the tea went cold by its own decision somewhere in the middle of the afternoon, like everything in the office.

The business took four minutes. He read the annex through, signed it slowly, dated it in a hand that was the slope. "Clause eleven is the founder's," he said, of the in-person countersignature. "He liked to meet the diggers. It is the only vanity he permitted himself, and the Trust has kept it as a clause, the way one keeps a godparent's duty. It spares the Trust a good deal of correspondence later, knowing early which of them will stay."

She asked about the letters, since the letters were why she had flown. "Carbon remembers wrong," Halden said. "A machine copies the letter whole, its mistakes and its flattery included. A hand that copies has to read. The Trust prefers to have read what it keeps." He said it the way a man states a filing practice, and reached for a cloth, and wiped the desk where the teapot had stood, because the water had marked it, thirty years ago, and he was still keeping that from happening again.

Then he showed her the Register. A shallow room, a wall of slim green volumes, site by site, decade by decade, and the light came in at the angle of offices and did the work of churches. Each page was a year. She took down the Kırk Oda volume because it was hers now, in the way licenses make things yours, and read, page after page, one line, in typescripts and hands that aged down the stack and never varied by a word: *Nothing to record.* Forty lines, and a blank page waiting for the forty-first. One page in the forty was in the slope. Nineteen ninety-nine. The line was the line — *Nothing to record* — and in its margin, small, in the same hand: *4–5.ix. Kept the hours. A.H.* It was the only page in the volume with a day on it, and the day was the one that had worn off her brother's file.

"Forty years," she said.

"Fifty, at some sites. The line does not vary because the visit does not vary. One goes. One keeps the hours. One writes the line."

"And if something happens?"

"Then the line is still true. *Something to record* would mean we had intruded. *Nothing to record* is not a finding, Dr. Arat. It is a result."

She asked whether he had kept the hours at her site, and he said, "Twice," and she asked when, and he said, "Before your birth. And once since." The page lay open between them and had already said when. "I filed my line," he said, and put the volume back with its spine even with its brothers, and the door of that subject closed as softly as it had opened, and she found she owned no crowbar polite enough for it.

The annex was a back room of shelves and boxes closed with string and bone buttons. He brought down the Kırk Oda box for her — the correspondence, the copied consents (April; the meeting they preceded, June; backdating, the least mysterious practice in either country), the two disagreeing surveys folded into their opinions, a leaf in a sleeve of its own, which he had set on top before she asked — and, because she had asked on the stairs, the Saflieni box as well.

The leaf was two leaves. The gendarmerie's, typed: *Any survey held of chambers below the fourth door, Kırk Oda, in connection with a missing juvenile, male, thirteen. 6.ix.1999.* And under it, in the slope: *The Trust holds no survey of any chamber below the fourth door at Kırk Oda, and advises that none be entered. — A. Halden, Registrar. 7.ix.1999.* She read them twice. They were true. The Trust held no such survey; the 1998 chamber was in the middle gallery, and the founder had counted nothing below the fourth door. The sergeant had never gone below it. Nobody had, until the third Monday, when she had. "The sergeant asked what the Trust held," Halden said. "The Trust answered what it held." He wiped the desk where nothing had stood. "The founder asked that nothing below the fourth door be counted. The Trust has kept every one of his requests. It would be a poor registry that kept them only when they were cheap." She put the leaves back in their sleeve because her hands needed an instruction, and did not ask the question that was in her mouth, because a question is a lamp you hold up on somebody, and she did not yet want to see his face by it.

In the Kırk Oda box, in a tray of small custody, stood a phial the length of her finger, corked, labeled in a harder hand than the slope: *Pigment, hand-mark, first marks, lower wall. A.V.L., 1924.* Item fifty-one, in glass. The founder had shaved a little of the grandmothers' ochre off the wall in 1924 and bottled it, in the manner of his century, and no one had asked him to account for it, and no one had asked the phial anything since. She held it to the window: a red between brick dust and rust. She set it back between the surveys, where it had lain out the century.

The Saflieni box held a file, a folded survey of 1938, an accession card, and a small box in tissue. The card read: *Shoe, child's, leather, left foot. From a niche, lower level. Accessioned 1962.* The 1938 survey, unfolded on the desk, drew that niche hatched and noted, in a surveyor's cautious hand: *sealed, no access.* She did for the contradiction what she did for a living. Three ways to hold it, all true enough: the accession number was a duplicate — the same number sat in the 1907 series against a shell bead, and the ledger had shrugged at the coincidence for a century; or the shoe was a stray of the old excavations, surfaced in some curator's drawer and given a polite new grave in 1962; or the 1938 survey had called a niche sealed out of the caution surveys are made of. None of it required anything.

The shoe itself was a left shoe for a five-year-old, or a small six, and the outer edge of the sole was worn round — a child who dragged the left foot a little, and went on dragging it somewhere, in some year, unrecorded. A list can hold a person only as an absence. The shoe was present. She put it back in its tissue, and the archive closed around it, keeping nothing, recording nothing, holding it the way hands hold what they are not agreed about.

"Records disagree," Halden said. "We do not improve them."

"The story, then," she said. "Nineteen forty. The Trust never said a word against it."

"Correct it with what? A record that does not exist cannot be amended, only contradicted, and contradiction wants a better witness than a war."

On the way out she asked for the founding papers, because it costs nothing to ask on the stairs. "There is a volume," Halden said. "It does not leave this floor. It is not consulted. It is produced when it becomes relevant, and relevance arrives on its own schedule, like post." At the head of the stair he gave her the countersigned annex, and with it a slip of paper. "From the site file. The founder's hand, 1924. I pass it on as received."

The slip said, in the hard hand: *Below the fourth door, count nothing.*

"The Trust does not interpret." He held the cupboard-wide door. "Safe over the water, Dr. Arat." It sounded like a wish and scanned like a record.

She landed at evening with the sea still in her ears, and Kemal's tractor took her up in the dark, and her phone, waking, showed one missed call from the dig-house number, Monday, 21:14, no message. Yusuf explained it off the wall, apologetic: Melek Hanım had called about the first cloud over Erciyes and the professor's sticks below the fourth door, the message had died between Kemal's boy and the co-op, and the tripod had spent two nights below, and nothing had happened to it, which is what nothing does.

They carried it up on the Wednesday, Márton lashing it like a man bearing his own verdict. "The cloud," he said, "is a meteorological fact. My hollow is a geophysical one. One of us will still be there in September."

"I called."

"The cloud."

"The cloud." She pinched a wick true. "Now you have it where the phone can't lose it."

"You went to the island rooms," Melek said, after a while. "Did you knock?"

"I knocked. Nothing answered."

Melek nodded at the wick, unsurprised, and offered no doctrine, and stood, and looked down the dark as one looks down a road. The half you have is for the body: sleep above the fourth door after rain, because water pushes the old air up and the old air doesn't know you. The half you don't have is for the house. Below the fourth door, you don't count."

"The singing room took to him. Rooms take to people the way dogs do, for their own reasons. Let him have his nine; it's a small number and it likes him. The deep way past the fourth door is not for numbers. If you must say a number down there, say forty, and be polite, and mean nothing by it."

It was the first time Melek had ever spoken of the fourth door as anything but a rain rule. The founder's slip lay in Nilay's pocket; she did not take it out. *Below the fourth door, count nothing* — a notary's hand, a century and a sea away, and here was the same rule in a lamp-keeper's mouth, arriving, like everything true in that country, by more than one road. She filed it with the ninety harmless things, where it kept company with an hour-behind clock, a crossed-out forty-one, a lullaby nobody owned, and two leaves of September 1999 she had copied out by hand on the plane, having learned in Valletta that a hand that copies has to read; the file was getting fat, and nothing in it had ever once been harmless.

Melek finished the round and did not hand Nilay the lamp so much as let her arrive at it — the ninth, filled to the knuckle, the wick pinched true. "Not pushed," Melek said. "Offered, the way bread is offered. Near, and wait."

Nilay held the flame near, and waited, and the wick came to it, and the ninth niche filled.

In her pocket, countersigned, dated twice in the slope, was the paper that would let them go deeper than the plans went. Somewhere over the Taurus she had known where, under the fourth door, the first bore would go, and she had not written it down. She had crossed a sea for a signature and come back carrying it the way one carries a lit thing: cupped, and low.


# V. Sleeping Air

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me:*

*Company comes to this house in the ordinary way: footsteps, oil, a name given at the mouth, bread if the season has been good. Once a year, company comes to the niche in the long gallery in a way that is not ordinary, and I will set it down, because when my year is over you will be the one standing where I stand, and it is not right to leave you unbriefed for her.*

*The niche stands at the height of a heart. The back of it is black, and the black has depth, and we do not clean it; the dark has its own errands and does not need ours. To the left of the niche the stone sounds thin — you find the place with the hand, not the eye — and a lamp stands in the niche, and my first office of her season is to fill it before she comes, so that she does not arrive at a dark glass.*

*She comes at the far end of the night, in the season when the oil is lowest, with the smell of the outside on her, rain or dust or threshing, whichever the year has been. She sets her back against the wall as a woman sets down a load, and she speaks into the niche the way you speak into a room where someone is sleeping: certain you are heard, careful not to wake. The words are four, in our tongue, and they are the same four, in the same order, every year of my keeping.*

*I came. The lamps are kept. No one is forgotten. Sleep.*

*Then water. She sets a small jar at the lip of the niche, and folds her hands in her lap like two birds settling, and waits. And I answer her the once, as is allowed: I give her the name of the room. Past the name, nothing. It is the rule, and the rule is a kindness, at both ends of it, though she has only ever needed the one.*

*The one who taught me waited for her before I did, and had it from her teacher before that. We do not compare notes. There is one note.*

*Once, when I was new, I wanted to ask her who she believed she was speaking to, and my teacher watched the wanting cross my face and said nothing, and when the woman had gone out she said: now you know why the rule is a kindness. The words land on whoever is in the room. You do not get to choose your size. You only get to consent to it. I have consented for a long time now.*

*Understand what the words do, because in a hard year you will doubt it. They carry nothing. They are not news. They are old shoes; they have been on many feet; they fit anyway. They go in below the ribs, where the cold keeps its ledger, and a line is struck through, and the striking is warmth of the true kind, which is small, and on the right line.*

*She stays the while a small candle spends. Then she goes, and going out she weeps, every year, in the manner of a person given exactly the thing she came for, and I do not follow her past the lamp. I never do. The weeping is hers to carry into the morning. The words are mine to keep the year with. The water I drink at first light, and it is the best water in the year, being carried water.*

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me: fill the lamp before her season; answer the once, and past the name, nothing; do not follow her into the morning. Whoever she takes you for, be the right size.*

In ninety-one caves on three continents, Priska Vogel had never met a presence she could not vent. That was the whole of her position, and she held it the way you hold a tool you were fitted for: bad air pooled, breath thickened, the blood went sour in its own smoke, and the mind — a faithful organ, an organ of the family — set a warmth beside you, a figure at the edge of the light, a voice under a knock. Give her a flue and a fan and six hours, and the company went out with the air. A chemistry you can pipe outdoors is a kind of mercy. She had watched the word *hypercapnia* do, in nine languages, the work that priests claimed and laboratories would not.

Bad air had a family face for her. Her father had spent thirty-one years keeping the road tunnels of the canton, the mountains' holes, and the mountains had kept a spoonful of him back, in the dust; she was fourteen when the cough began and twenty-six when it ended, and somewhere inside those years, without any decision she could find, she had become the scientist of the thing that was killing him, which is one of the ways there are of loving a man you cannot help.

After the wheat came in and before the grapes, the nights belonged to the rooms. The sitting nights, the women called them: each household took one, and a woman who had buried people kept her mat where her people had sat, and Melek led, and the round came first, oil to the knuckle, wicks pinched. Priska asked leave to bring her meter down and set it in the corner of the middle gallery, and Melek considered the pump the way she had considered Márton's gravimeter. "It may sit," she said, "if it is quiet." It was not quiet; it clicked, six strokes to the minute; the women folded it into the night the way a household folds a clock.

They came at dusk with rolled mats and bread and one another, an economy of grandmothers. Seher came too — the schoolteacher, two years widowed, the youngest there that night by forty years — with a canvas bag of school papers from which, more than once, Priska saw the corner of a printed table protruding, and recognized, with a peculiar itch at the back of her neck, her own figures.

The lamp round done, the women sat along the walls of the middle gallery, and the night organized itself around the niche — a shelf cut at the height of a standing person's heart, its back sooted black and shining, the stone to its left worn smooth to the height of hands. The answering niche, the village called it, in Turkish a plainer thing. Melek rose, laid the flat of her hand on the sounding stone, and knocked twice, softly, and then stood in the waiting, two breaths entire, and the waiting was the rite, if it was a rite; Priska had stopped deciding which parts were.

On the second breath of the after-quiet, something came under the knock. Low. Shaped. With the fall of a sentence in it.

"She answered," Seher said, not loudly, the way you report the time.

"That was the lamp," Nilay said. "Third wick's drowning in its own oil." Behind them, old Sungur Hanım had been humming a row of something, or had not; she denied it at breakfast; the woman on her mat swore to it. Two listeners, one sound, and Priska could referee nothing, because the pump clicked in her ears, six strokes to the minute, and what reached her was the edge of a thing with its face turned away. She wrote in the log: *possible audible, source unresolvable* — and knew, writing it, that unresolvable was the most honest word she had brought into the country.

The rest of the night was data. Fatma Nine, waking from a doze, said without opening her eyes, "It came to my left, where he used to," and put her hand flat on the mat and slept again. A woman nearer the mouth spoke of the green soap, the one in the green paper, her mother's soap — the co-op still sold it; every mother in the room had used it — arrived whole at her face out of the dark. Havva Nine, small and eighty-four, said in the morning that someone had done her hair, the way for school, and laughed at herself, and did not take it back. Priska took the accounts at their word and the words at their chemistry, and both books balanced: twenty-two sitting nights so far, fifteen such events, every one of them below the second door, every one on a still night, when her forecast sheets — her candles lay down at printed times, at appointed places — had pooled the gallery in dead air like a held note. The village called those nights generous. She called them pooled. Both parties kept straight accounts.

It had not rained since July. The village rule was a rain rule — water pushes the old air up — and the rule had no clause for thirty women breathing out a still August night at the bottom of a hill. The rule missed it. Her science caught it. That was the whole of the season in one sentence, and she would spend the rest of the summer being forgiven for it.

On the third still night of the last week of August, Havva Nine went from her mat in the quiet hour, and Melek's round came back with a sentence held out flat in both hands: "Havva is at the fourth door, and will not wake."

Priska went down ahead of them with the lamp and the meter, and the readings climbed as she descended, room by room, like stairs, and at the fourth door, knee height, at the jamb where the old woman sat with her chin on her chest, flushed as a woman in her bath, the figure arrived like a signature: four point zero percent. Forty thousand parts per million. They carried Havva up between them, Melek and Seher and Priska, the three of them and a rolled carpet's weight, out through the doors to the night at the mouth, and in four minutes the night had her; she surfaced like a diver, and her first words were serene, and complete, and unbearable in a way Priska would not put in any report.

"I spoke with my mother. She was cross with me for the hour. She said I had woken the whole house."

The women received this with satisfaction — gently, nodding, *she reached her*, and one of them, *the grandparents were generous with her tonight* — and Melek, gathering the mats, said only, "The sleeping air put its arm around her," and Priska wrote the euphoria down in her head as what it was in the textbooks, in the paragraph nobody assigns: the serenity is a symptom. What she did not say aloud, because there was no instrument for it, was that a symptom and a mercy look the same at three in the morning, from the outside.

Before light she went down alone and sat where Havva had sat, because a time series is the only apology a scientist can make to an event. The hill had spent the night breathing and the numbers came back clean, 0.7, 0.6, 0.5, the lamp at her knee, the doors above her shut in their sockets. It was while she was writing the 0.5 that the cough came, beside her, at her left, where nobody was: twice, and then the little knock at the end, like a knuckle on wood — and before it, the half-second's polite warning, the shape of a man who apologized with his hand before he coughed. Her father's cough. Twelve years of it, two notes and the knock, a language she had sat inside at his bedside in a dark canton bedroom while it finished with him.

She looked at the meter, because the meter was the appointed place for her eyes, and the meter said 0.5, and the pump clicked, six strokes to the minute, and nothing else in the gallery moved. She built the forks as she had taught a decade of students to build them: the stone ticking as it gave up the first temperature of the day; a drip finding a hollow; the lamp spitting in its oil; her own chest, which had climbed four doors' worth of stairs and knew that cough better than her ears did, and could perform it from the inside. The theory took all four forks and survived. What it did not survive was the half-second in which she had not wanted it to survive — the half-second in which she had sat absolutely still, in a clean reading, being spoken to in the only language the man had had left at the end, and had not wanted it explained.

At 05:40 she wrote: *0.5, still.* It was the cleanest reading of her career and the only dishonest page in it.

Above ground, in the grey, Havva slept it off under a coat with the women around her, breathing like sewing. Priska stood over her with Nilay, and heard herself ask the question she had come two thousand kilometers not to ask.

"When the mask works and the visitation stops — what did we cure?"

Nilay looked at the sleeping woman for a long time and did not answer, and the not-answer held both of them, and Priska went down to the co-op and wrote the report anyway, because a report was a thing you could finish.

The Ministry's fax came in two days, and Kaya Bey came in three — a folder, a good tailor, and a genius for arriving when the tea was hot — carrying a draft order to close the site to all attendance pending inquiry. Institutions prefer a cause they can mandate, he said pleasantly, and here, Doctor, was a cause. She spent two evenings taking the closure apart and putting her own thing in its place: a galvanized duct at the mouth, a solar fan, a watcher above with a meter and a written threshold, eleven women and nine lamps below the second door and no more, and scrubber masks, soda-lime canisters of the mine-rescue kind, below the second door on sitting nights. The order as issued named accumulated carbon dioxide in still conditions as the cause of the incident of the twenty-eighth, mandated the masks, annexed her plan entire. For the first time in her career her explanation had a stamp on it. The fumes were canon now, administratively, with signatures — the only doctrine in the valley that came with paperwork.

And they worked. On twenty-two nights before the masks, fifteen events; on twenty-six nights after, two — both on the one week the fan was down for a bearing. She tabled it, footnoted the exceptions, and the phenomenon, deflated, behaved. The village's manners cooled by exactly one degree, which was the temperature of a courtesy observed: a child on a wall clicked its tongue at her, pump-fashion, and ran; Havva, recovered, patted her hand at the bread queue and said, "You fixed the air, dear. Such trouble," and the door of the sentence shut as softly as it opened. Her daughter, at the window, did not pat anything. "Four percent," she said, "and nobody watched her." The queue let the sentence stand, and moved along. Melek tried a mask on once, at the mouth, below the second door, and said, "The grandparents will not know us in these," and wore it anyway, because the rules that keep the body are rules too, and first.

For the report she took twenty-seven accounts, at kitchen tables, over tea she was sorry about by the third. She kept a checklist, and the checklist never once cleared: a maiden name, a debt, a document, a buried thing, a date, a fact that could be checked and would stand. No presence, in twenty-seven kitchens, had ever produced one. The company consoled; it did not inform. She wrote that into the report as the refutation. The village, she understood, would have written it as the manners. Both texts were true; the page held one.

The schoolhouse smelled of chalk. Seher poured the tea herself, and had Priska's tables out on the desk, printed, corners folded down, read to the fold lines.

"I read your paper twice, Doctor. It explains every night except the one I needed."

Priska asked, gently, what she had needed.

"The fortieth day. I sat below, on my mat, in the low room. It came to my left and stayed until the trimming of the lamps. It did not speak. It never speaks." Seher looked at her own hands. "It is not talk that is missing, Doctor." Then, evenly, holding the two explanations out where Priska could see them, one on each palm: "I had fasted that day, and the room was low, and the night was still, and I wanted it. I can draw your diagram on the board for the children; I have drawn it. Wanting is also a gas." She squared the printed pages. "Doctor Kállai lent me the papers about the third man on the glacier. I read them twice also. They say the air thinned and the companion came. Nowhere do they say the companion was not there. The papers decline. You decline too, Doctor — kindly." And, at the door, without anger, reporting a fact about her nights: "When your fan runs, I hear it until morning."

At the dig-house table Márton read her tables like a man reading another man's mail. "Your guilty air has warmed a grandmother, answered a knock, and stopped my clocks," he said, "and now it installs with a fan. Oxygen debt writes sermons, Priska. The theology is finished; the fumes remain." She refilled his glass and did not dignify it, and lay awake doing the arithmetic of what he'd said.

Later, the generator off, she said to Nilay, flat, into the dark between them: "If it can't be falsified, it isn't knowledge. It's comfort."

Neither of them poured the tea for a while.

The splinter came in Kaya Bey's final packet, clipped behind the mask mandate: a single page copied out by hand, in the slope she knew now from three letters — the Trust to the Ministry, copied to her *as a courtesy* — noting that the calibration certificate for her instrument's serial could not be located in the service's files for the relevant quarter; that the service's accreditation for that quarter had lapsed, for administrative reasons; that readings from the period should be *considered indicative*. She recalibrated that same evening against her own standards, and it passed, of course it passed, and it did not matter: a restored certificate is a certificate with a biography. Her canon kept its stamp, and the number it stood on had become a story about paperwork. Someone had read her report more closely than the Ministry had, and had not touched the canon — only the number's provenance — and the splinter was exactly splinter-sized, and she sat a long time with her tea going cold, thinking of the fourth door at 05:40, and the acquittal of 0.5, and understanding that the acquittal was anecdotal now as well.

At dusk the queue formed at the mouth, mats under arms, the women going down into their nights wearing their masks the way women at a threshing wear cloths against the chaff. The lamps below were the same lamps. The duct breathed. Seher went down last with her bag, and Yusuf's phone came up at the mouth, low, out of old habit, and Melek looked at it until it went away again. Priska sat on the flat stone above with the watcher's meter quiet at her hip, in the country she had made survivable and moved out of, and did not fill in the one row of the one table that would have read: *coughs, unexplained — one.* It was in no report. She kept it where she kept her father, which is nowhere the Trust can reach.


# VI. Server Lag

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me:*

*A boy came overland by the herders' road at the end of the dry season, on the morning after the first rain, on foot, alone, and he carried the sun in his hand.*

*I had been told of it once, as you are told of hail out of a clear sky: do not fear it, and do not argue with it either. The sun in the hand is a true sun to whoever carries it and a small matter to everyone else, and both of these hold at once, which is usual down here.*

*It lay flat on his palm, a little less than a hand long, and gave light the way stone gives cold: wholly, and without spending itself. I put my hand above it, because a keeper checks. There was no heat. The oil did not know it. A flame leans into the world; this did not lean. He looked into it more than he looked at the lamps, the way you watch a fire that other people are sitting at.*

*He gave his name at the mouth before he was asked, which is more than the grown manage, and at his hip a small box ticked, slow, like a cricket that had been taught to count, and he did not seem to hear it any more than I hear my knuckles; and he asked for mine back, which nobody does, and I gave him the name of the room, which is the only name I keep that is mine to give.*

*He was hungry for the how of everything. How old is the house. Older than the question. Who cut the first room. The question stands on wrong feet. How many rooms are there. I gave him the polite number, and he took it in somewhere inside himself, and — I will be fair to him — he did not once ask me why, which put him ahead of every measurer I have ever watered.*

*He slept by the lamps two nights. The sun in his hand went dark when he slept, as suns do not, and from that I learned it was his, the way the lamp is mine: a thing that keeps his hours and not the world's. On the second night he shone it low along the wall, along the lines, and read them with his finger held a finger's width off the stone, touching nothing. Nobody had taught him that. Some guests arrive already taught, by roads I do not know, and you do not ask.*

*Was I afraid of him? I was afraid the way you are afraid at the top of a stair you have climbed a thousand times, in the moment you notice the stair. Not of the boy. Of the length. For one breath the house felt long — longer than my keeping of it — and then the breath passed, and I filled the lamp, and oil to the knuckle settles more than a wick.*

*He went up into the morning and the sun went with him, small in the daylight and no less his for that, and I did not follow past the lamp. I never do. The day's mark is cut, one line, low, beside the others. There is no mark for a sun. There has never needed to be. The house keeps what it keeps in its own way.*

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me: when the sun in the hand comes — and it comes; the house is long — do not fear it. Give the name of the room, and water, and the good distance. Light that somebody carries is still light, and the somebody is the half of it.*

The first time Melek looked at his phone until it went away was an evening in July, at the wick hour, and after it Yusuf stopped filming people.

His mother's people were from the near side of Derinkuyu, where the houses drank downward, storeroom under storeroom, and the children went down for pickles at four years old with a torch in both hands like a loaf. Dark had never been content for him; dark was furniture. Content was the light you carried into it, and the fact of being seen. He had decided at fourteen that if the world would not look at him of his own accord he would hold up a lamp until it did, and the phone had been the lamp ever since.

The crossed-out forty-one in the site register had been living in him since July. He had built the counter in four evenings that month, at the dig house: a tally screen, a toggle for each opening along, and condition tags he named himself — LAMPS (COLD/WARM), AIR (STILL/MOVING), GENERATOR, PEOPLE (n). He gave his name at the mouth before going down, having watched the women do it and been told by Seher, the schoolteacher, that it was manners; he knocked at the head of the deep stair with the flat of his hand, twice, softly, and waited out the two breaths, every time, though nothing had ever answered him and he would have said he did it for the bit. He and Seher disagreed, mildly and permanently, about the low corridor: he remembered her saying first, at the wick hour, that the way below the fourth door was older than the house; she remembered it the other way on, and could produce the evening, the rain, and the wall she had been leaning against. Both were certain. He wrote it in the field book, because he wrote everything now: *low door — said it first: her (certain) / me (certain). unresolved.* Then he counted along, opening by opening, touching each with two fingers as you touch the heads of children at a doorway, and he did not trade the count of a room for the count of a door, ever, because Dr. Arat had made him repeat it twice on the first day like a border formalities question.

By the last week of August he had had a hundred and forty passes. The count said forty with the obedience of a metronome, except when it didn't. The except-whens, copied out fair for nobody: *19 Aug, 05:50, still, lamps cold, generator off — 41. 24 Aug, 06:05, still, nobody below — 41. 27 Aug, 05:45, still, generator off — 41.* Generator days, forty, forty, forty, and he made the excuses for those mornings himself before anyone could make them for him: a bin counted as a room in cold smoke; his stride shortening at the far turn; the shelves of dead air making openings lie. Then he did the thing that took it out of comedy: he laid his except-whens over Priska's forecast sheets, which were pinned to the dig-house wall like weather for a country only she lived in, and every forty-one sat inside a pooled-air window like a coin in a slot. Later, after the order, he would count in a mask, and the forty-one would come the same, mask or no, which he would not tell Priska, because she had enough.

She looked at the overlay for a long time, photocopied it, and clipped it into her binder, which he understood by now was how she handled things she could not say she loved. "Your rooms drift in my weather," she said. "Everything on this hill drifts in it. If I am the control variable of this circus, I want it minuted that I object." Márton asked for the timestamps to the second, and came back two days later with his middle-gallery station in a column beside them. On the three forty-one mornings the station by the far niches had read lighter by fourteen microgal — "a room's worth," he said, "if a room were there; the 1998 room, in the 1998 place" — and on every other morning it read rock. He said it at the table without a joke in it, and the table noticed. "Two surveys, a century apart, both competent. I had assumed one hill and two bad maps. Suppose instead two good maps." He did not finish the supposition. "Priska will tell me that still air is heavy air, and she will be right by about a tenth of what I need. Do not tell Priska." Nilay read the table twice and handed it back and said, "Keep counting." The register stayed at forty. The forty-one she had crossed out in June had brothers now, and nobody wrote the brothers down, and Melek, asked what one did on a morning when the house was bigger, said, "Be polite."

His nights were the wick hour. He streamed from the mouth at dusk, the round, the lamps going into their niches one at a time if Melek allowed the phone at all, and after her look he framed the niches only, the flames, the tuff drinking the light. Eleven viewers was a good night. He knew them by then: the caver from Ljubljana who corrected his terminology; the boy in Eskişehir who could not sleep and said so; and gülce, small letters, a yellow flower where a face would go, who came on after two and stayed to the end and typed, some nights, one line. *the wicks are good tonight.* Or only: *again?* He had a private theory that she worked nights somewhere, a ward or a bakery, and watched with the sound low, because she never asked for anything the others asked for. The others asked what was down there. Gülce never asked what was down there.

On the last still night of August he gave the chat his theory whole, because they had earned it and because the numbers were his.

"Listen. It's not a mystery, it's infrastructure. The site's rendering two instances of itself — same map, two builds — and the sync's bad. Still days, the wrong one loads for a while. That's the extra room. It's not a thing, it's a rollback. Nobody drags a priest in for server lag; you just wait for the sync." The chat asked who ran the server, and he said, before thinking, "the women," and the joke landed somewhere he had not aimed it, and the chat moved on, and he sat a while afterward with what he had said. He said, quieter, "The knocking's a ping, by the way. You knock so the other instance knows you're loading in. The two breaths are latency." He had meant it as a bit. It came out as a gloss, and he ended the stream with the feeling of a man who has made a joke in a language he does not speak.

The first rain since July came two nights later, on the last night of August, and in the morning the hill was washed and ringing and the air below had not moved. He went down at first light, alone, which Dr. Vogel had opinions about, and he had learned which of them she checked. Her spare meter clicked at his hip, six strokes to the minute, because she had made him promise, and because the clicking had become, without his noticing, company. His phone left the house at eighty percent and showed nine by the middle gallery; he put it down to the cold, and to the update he had been avoiding since June.

He counted along, touching the openings, and the far room put the number in his mouth, and this time the number was forty-one, and this time the camera had it too. He stood in it a moment, breathing slowly, like a man at altitude, counting like one. In the stretch where the plans quarreled — where 1924 drew rock and 1998 drew a room — the night's rain stood in the low place in a sheet the size of a table, and he filmed it, because he believed, and had never once examined the belief, that a thing has not happened until it is watched.

# VII. The Breach

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me:*

*I will set down the guests from the deep road, because my teacher set them down for me, and hers for her, and the setting down is the same in every hand: seldom, courteous, and not to be feared.*

*The house does not end at the last lamp. Below the deepest round there is a way, and the way is the house's, and the round does not go there. The road does not come up. Except seldom. And when it comes up, it comes up as guests.*

*You will know them before you see them, by the flame. A lamp at rest stands its light straight. When they are on the road, the flame leans toward the deep rooms, the way a plant leans at a window, and there is no draft to blame, and you do not go looking for one. Looking for a draft is how keepers stop being keepers and become people with a house and a grievance.*

*They keep to the edge of whatever light you carry. That is the first courtesy, and it is theirs before it is yours. They give the name of their room — and it is a name you will not know, being from a part of the house you have not walked — and past the name, nothing. Our rule, kept from their side. That is the whole of the kinship between the near keeping and the far one, and it has always been enough.*

*The oldest keepers call them the far lamplighters, and the name is exact, though not the way the young first take it. My teacher's teacher said far down, as a well is far. Another said far aside, as the next house is far, the one you can hear through the wall and never enter. My teacher said far along. I set down all three, and mark mine for a guess, a guess being a thing a keeper may keep, if she marks it for one.*

*Twice in my keeping they have come. Both times the basin stood full when they had gone, and the water was carried water — you will know it by the kindness of it — and once they righted what a tremor had laid down in the far room, and set it by, and that was all, and it was much. Their courtesy is the house's courtesy, done at a farther reach.*

*When I was new I asked my teacher why they came at all, if they came for nothing and carried nothing away. She was a long time answering, which was her way of saying the answer was worth carrying slow. Because the road is long, she said, and long roads are kept by being walked. Then she sent me to trim the wicks, which is what she said when a thing had been said enough.*

*The elder of them, the last time, wore a wrap the color the hill is at evening, and the weave of it repeated nowhere — I looked, being young, and my eyes were better than my manners — and I have not seen the like since, not on the living. I say this against myself, having been taught that a keeper records what is, and not what is like nothing else. It was like nothing else. The lamp is lit. The mark is cut.*

*Do not fear them, and do not follow them. Fear spends itself where it is allowed to, and following is a kind of asking, and they are not here to be asked. When they go down, they go down, and I do not follow past the lamp. I never do.*

*There is no mark on the wall for them. We keep the wall, and they keep theirs, farther along; you will know their cutting by its finish, which our chisels do not make and our patience does not reach. Leave a small mark beside it, or none. The wall is long, and it is all one wall.*

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me: when the flame leans toward the deep rooms, fill the lamp and wait. Say the name of the room, and past the name, nothing. They keep the same rule. That is how you will know them, and it is the only how there is.*

Márton wanted the rig at the foot of the deep stair, over the heart of his residual. The room the lamp stood up was long and low and rounded at every corner, and it was finished. That was the first fact and the largest: finished, the way nothing in eleven thousand years above it was finished. The walls took the flame and gave it back with a wet look, polished like cooled glass; the ceiling was pale as the day of its cutting, and unsooted, and every ceiling Nilay had ever met in that hill wore black like a surname. The air was warm. It moved against her face mild and slow, like the air of a room that had just had people in it, and it smelled of rain on dust, and of nothing else at all.

The basin was cut in the floor near the far wall, shallow as a plate, and full to its lip, which is the way water is set out and not the way water stands. On a mat beside it, upright on its foot, wiped, facing the stair, stood Márton's camera. He crossed to it like a man claiming his own dog out of a stranger's yard, and picked it up, and turned it over, and said, "It fell."

The mats were under her boots before she had seen them — that was the order of it, the foot reporting first — and when she knelt, the weave was dark and close, and the thread was not wool, not flax, not anything her hands had met in nineteen seasons, and it repeated nowhere. She meant to lift a corner. Her hands declined, and she let them decline, because you do not fold the tablecloth at a table you are sitting at, and because the far edge of the lamplight was occupied.

There were two of them against the far wall, seated, hands in their laps — and at the hem of the light, where the polish gave back more than the flame had to give, a third shape sat, or the wall sat. She counted two and could not certify the third, and said so, later, on the record, before anyone could ask her not to. She had come down, she understood only then, for a boy of thirteen in house slippers, and none of them was a boy. They were human. That was not a finding; it was a pressure; the figures breathed slowly, evenly, in layered wraps the color of no dye she knew, and their stillness was not ambush and not accident. It was the stillness of people on duty at the edge of something. Nothing in the room was surprised. That was the whole of it, she thought, standing there: not ambush, not accident — a house that had been told.

Melek did not cross herself or greet. She set her lamp in the empty niche at the basin's head, which was where a lamp went, and then she laid the flat of her hand against the polished wall and held it there, the way you hold a hand on the flank of a horse you do not know. Márton, being Márton, stepped forward with his own hand out, European, grave, at the level of a handshake; the hand went out and hung with its manners on, and nothing took it, and nothing refused it, and he repossessed it slowly, like a man who has offered his hand to weather. After a while — Yusuf said two breaths; Nilay, later, could not make it shorter than the length of a drummed finger — the elder on the far wall raised his own palm and laid it flat against the stone on his side, and between the two palms the room was a room.

The elder rose then, in one motion, without hands, as the young do it, and he was not young; whatever his joints were, they were not negotiating. He came to the middle distance and stopped at the good distance. Yusuf, who had been making the sounds of a man keeping his breathing voluntary, unclipped his headlamp and set it on the mat between them, the way you set food down for a strange dog, and sat back on his heels. The old man considered it. Then he leaned, and cupped his hand around the burning lens as a man cups a match in wind, and said a short thing, unhurried, in a speech that was no language any of them had, and that each of them carried up in their own — Nilay's came up an old, smoothed Turkish; Márton's, he said later, came up Hungarian, which he had not dreamed in for forty years. What Nilay carried up the stair: *the little sun that doesn't burn.* He set the headlamp back on the mat facing the boy, and returned to the wall, and the smile he had arrived at on the way went to half and stopped, and the teeth in it were perfect, regular as a comb, and fewer than a mouth has — she counted, being a woman who counts teeth for a living — as if the mouth had been drawn rather than grown; nothing else about him had ever been near a clinic.

The woman rose once, in her own time, and kept rising, or the low ceiling made it seem so, until the new-cut doorway was at her shoulder, and measured it with her forearm, elbow to fingertip, twice, unhurried, without apology, and sat down again. Nilay had measured graves in five countries and knew the gesture's family. What it was doing at a door she did not ask; the room did not invite questions, being already, in some way she could not source, an answer. And twice, softly, at them — not calling; naming — the woman said a word of three syllables, the middle one long. It did not sit in the mouth. Nilay tried to hold it and it turned, like a coin with the same face twice, and let her go.

Once — only once — the one at the hem, the one the light would not insist on, asked, in the worn speech, the clearest sentence any of them spent down there:

"Is the fire mountain awake?"

And no one answered. Nilay opened her mouth and found that she could not certify the sleep of anything — there were two fire mountains in sight of the village, and awake was not a word her discipline could hold, and a wrong answer dressed as knowledge is the one theft she had never committed — and the question stood in the room, wanting nothing, and folded itself away.

The rest of the hour did things she could not itemize. Márton sat with his notebook open and wrote nothing. Against the foot of the far wall, a hand's-breadth of ash lay scattered, grey-white, fine as flour, and he laid the back of his hand above it and said "cold," and took up in a sample bag what a spatula holds, which was most of what there was, the ceiling above it wearing no smoke at all. Yusuf filmed, at the far turn, from behind them all. Somebody's stomach spoke, and was forgiven. The lamps stood where they had been set down; three went down, and at the head of the stair, afterward, only one was counted, and nobody that afternoon counted twice; and which of them it was that stood the hour without once being moved, the four of them would never afterward agree, and that was the first disagreement, and there were others behind it. Nobody counted twice. The room was one room, and for once the founder's rule and the keeper's seemed easy to keep.

Melek rose at last in her own time, and topped the niche-lamp to the knuckle of her thumb, and pinched the wick true, and left it burning, which is the way you leave a house that is not empty. "We have been seen," she said. "Now the house keeps its evening."

Nilay climbed out last. At the limit of the lamps, past the basin, a passage went off — polished like the rest, a sill at its mouth, and the dark beyond going on — and she put the lamp into it, and the passage took the light a long way and declined to finish, and the not-following was the hardest work she did underground that day, and she did not examine why; you do not open a letter in the street. On the sill, in a pinch no larger than a sparrow would spill, lay flour. Fresh-ground. She knew the smell from six seasons of grinding experiments at Göbekli Tepe, as one knows a kitchen; and in the flour, two whole grains sat, tight-hulled, small, the first wheat anyone anywhere is argued to have sown, a variety with no standing field left in the world. She bagged it with the ash, procedure reasserting itself the way a soldier polishes boots, and gave the ordinary accounts their turn on the climb: a heritage mill's paper bag in somebody's pocket; a cuff; a joke with a mortar. *To the one who keeps the lamps after me:*

*The wall keeps the days, one line low beside the others, and it keeps a second kind of mark besides, set apart, above the days' rows, and in all the wall's length there are few of them, and they are all one mark.*

*My teacher said them to me in order, and her teacher to her, and I say them to you in order, because the order is the inheritance; the dates are the wall's business, not ours.*

*That is the mark's saying. It is the only saying the mark has. I give it as it was given, without the why, because the why is not for saying, and you will not need it. A door is not explained to the one who must shut it.*

*They came with iron for the grandmothers' copper, and found none, for we do not keep copper; we keep what does not sell. Up they came with less than they carried down, and the mark was cut.*

*They came in the hungry years for the graves of others, not having graves of their own to tend, and they went about it loudly, and the loudness told us where they were, which is the use of loudness. What was the house's stayed the house's. The mark was cut.*

*They came with fire in pots, to see by, and the fire ate the air, and the air ate the fire, and they went up fast, and I do not know how their story finished, and the wall does not tell it. The mark was cut.*

*A man came alone on a trimmed day with a glass finger in his coat, and stood a long while before the first marks, the way the poor stand before bread, and then he took a finger's cup of the wall's blood into the glass, and stoppered it, and was sorry, if I read him rightly, and went up. We do not mend what is taken. We keep what remains. His mark is cut small, at the side, because he took little, and was polite, and stood the way one stands before bread.*

We sat with the lamps and let the house be eaten to the door it was always going to open, for the house opens what it opens, and that is the house's, not ours. When they had gone up, the basin was full, and the oil was to the knuckle, and the mark was cut.*

*They will come again. The wall is long, and it is all one wall.*

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me: the grandparents dug upward, all the days of the wall, and the house is here, and the lamps are trimmed, and that is the whole of the record, and it is enough.*

# IX. The Solstice Watches

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me:*

*I am at the end of my keeping. The room has known it longer than I have and has been kind about it in its way, which is stone's way, which is slow, and I will set down the last teaching while the hand still consents to be mine, because the last teaching is small, and small things go missing in a handover, like keys, like reasons.*

*The room chooses the one who stays.*

*I did not know that when I was taught. I believed my teacher had chosen me, out of all the children who could be spared, because my hands were quick and my want was quiet. It was years before I understood that her choosing had been only a reading aloud. The room had leaned. She read the leaning to me, which has no words, and handed me the oil, and called it her decision, the way the surface calls things, where things can still be changed.*

*Two came when the word went out that I was finishing. You should hear how it was, so that your own finishing does not surprise you, whichever side of it you are on.*

*The one had the hands. Quick, dry, exact; she trimmed a wick the first evening better than I trim them now, and I hated her a little, correctly, the way one hates the answer to a prayer one has outgrown. The other came up from the valley with her grief and asked for nothing but a floor to stand it on, and sat the nights without being asked, and the nights agreed to be sat.*

*Both filled to the knuckle. The room leaned once, at the third lamp, on the fourth night, the way warmth commits: suddenly, entirely, with weight. It leaned to one side, and it did not lean again, and I am not the one who chooses, and I gave the oil to where the warmth had stood, and the one with the quick hands went up into the morning and did not come back down, and I never learned what became of her, and the wall keeps no mark of it, the wall keeping days and diggings, not choices.*

*Understand me: the one who left was not refused. There is no refusing in this house; there are only rooms, and the room had chosen the one who stays. She could have stayed anyway — the door takes one finger, and nothing down here is shut against the living — and been a guest for forty years, and the guest is a true office, and I have known houses kept in that split manner, lamps and guest together, and they keep. But she had wanted to be chosen, and wanting is a surface thing, and she went up. I did not follow past the lamp. I never do.*

*I will set one thing against myself, since a setting-down is also a keeping. There was a winter I kept badly — out of spite, over a man, the reasons being mine — and I let the third lamp sit cold three nights together, and cut the marks all the same, one line, low, like the others. Nothing came of it. Nothing came of it that I ever learned, and I never learned whether that was the house's mercy or the house's inattention, and I have carried the not-knowing longer than I carried the spite.*

*She is beside me now as I set this down, trimming what I cannot, and she thinks I do not see her counting my breaths. I see. It is the correct instrument for the work, and I am glad my breathing has become data at the end; it is the career I had left for it.*

*As for me: my failing is not interesting. The body closes its accounts in its own hand. On a night when the lamps are full I will sit where the wall lets a body sit, and the round will belong to the other one, and the mark will be cut, one line, low, no different from the other lines, and that is the mercy, that it will not be different.*

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me: you were not chosen by me. Be equal to whoever did.*

Frost took the hill in the second week of November and kept it. The dig went down to its winter skeleton — Nilay cataloguing in the dig house with a stove that drew badly, Priska above ground among her ducts and thresholds, Yusuf gone to his mother's people in Derinkuyu for the frost weeks — and Márton stayed, because he had a hollow under the deep floors that had outlived every explanation he had shot at it, and a triangle that was still eleven centimeters short, obedient as a mule, re-run once in December from a different vertex order with the same deficit; and he had dismissed, in writing, his own counter-readings — winter stratification, tripod settlement, tuff dust on the returns — with the courtesy of a man dismissing relatives.

The Ministry's letter came up the hill on the post moped at the end of November. *Instrument access by night is granted for the solstice period, 21–31 December, at the request of the licensee's geophysicist, which request is noted.* It was dated the twenty-eighth of November. He had not applied. He had said at the committee, in September, that he would, and Seher's minute had recorded the intention, and the Ministry, it appeared, had read the minute and saved him the stamp — or someone had read it to them. He pinned the grant to the dig-house wall beside Priska's forecast sheets and did not examine who.

Melek read the sky over Erciyes and issued her permission under the Ministry's, as she issued everything under everything. "Dry," she said. "Ten nights, if the mountain keeps its sky. Not one past the first cloud." Then she looked at the felt case he had sewn for the watches, and at him. "You knock softly now," she said, and took up her oil can, and that was the whole of the blessing.

Dr. Ines Marques came in the second week of December, having escaped a workshop in Kayseri by a day of her own. She was small, exact, dressed for a lecture hall and therefore for nothing on that hill, and she carried tea in a thermos and drank it when she chose, which gave her the advantage over every other visitor the site had had. She had read the numbers — he had written in October, stripped of story, the triangle, the residual, the twenty-nine microgal, as one professional posts figures to another — and she had also, it emerged, read the withdrawn preprint of 1997, which someone had once sent her as a joke.

"Two clocks," she said, "is an anecdote. Three are a committee, you wrote me once. Committees can be bribed by warmth, and always are." She walked the hill with him in the flat light, stood at the mouth while he explained the duct, and declined, without looking at it, to be interested in the knots of solstice-watchers along the cemetery wall who had begun appearing at the mulberries because the internet had decided the solstice mattered; the internet is not told otherwise.

She spent her coldest hour in the Ninth Room. He watched it take the note out of her the way it took it out of everyone — sternum first, her chest finding the low A before any decision had been made — and she stopped herself mid-hum, annoyed, like a woman catching her own hand in a biscuit tin. "Resonance," she said. "Rooms sing. Cathedrals sing better. Geometry is not hospitable, Professor."

"Nevertheless," he said, and gave her the physics he had: the hollow, the triangle, the chamber under three meters of rock and one plug of tuff that took one finger.

"A throat wants negative energy," Marques said. "Negative energy is real — the Casimir effect balances its checkbook between two plates every day of the week — but the universe spends it like a miser: never in company, never at scale. To hold open anything you could walk a cat through, you would need it by the planetful. Whatever leans on geometry here is being fed, and the feeding is the physics, and nobody has ever shown me the feeding. Your throat violates every energy condition that has ever bought us a theorem." She looked around the room once, professionally, a suspect's tidy kitchen. "Show me the machine."

"Perhaps the machine is old," Márton said.

"Then it has a fuel bill," Marques said, "and somebody is paying it. Find the bill, no? Then I will come and look at your machine." At the mouth, before the hired car turned, she gave him her prescription through the car window: "Leave your watches a night. All three, one place, one temperature, unworn — bodies are weather. If they come up arguing by more than noise, write to me. They will not. Send the logs either way."

The women were sitting the middle gallery on the night of the twenty-first, eleven of them, lawful, lamps to the knuckle; the sitting ran its ledger two rooms away, and he passed it going down, and nodded, and was nodded to, in the economy of it. He had given his name at the mouth before he was asked — his own name, and he had heard how it sounded with no instrument after it — and at the head of the deep stair he knocked with the flat of his hand, twice, softly, and waited out the two breaths, having watched the boy do it all summer and having no opinion left about why he copied him.

The three watches rode down unworn, in the felt case, on the tripod, with a thermologger beside them; the receiver stayed above, on the shelf at the mouth, logging against the satellites when the sky permitted, which, in a valley, is a philosophy. Dusk comparison: unanimous to four-tenths of a nanosecond, punctual as they had been all July, all his life. Then he sat the night in the Ninth Room, in the manner of the women, motionless, and the cold came off the walls and then gave back what the day had left in them, and Priska's spare meter clicked at his hip, six strokes to the minute, which was company, a fact he declined to log. Once he permitted himself the hum, and the note went away from him and was set down somewhere he could not point to. Toward the small hours a warmth arrived at his right side, with weight; he was sixty-eight and knew the body's furnace and its drafts; he filed the warmth under furnace, and sat with the filing.

The dawn comparison he took with Nilay for a witness, her clipboard in one hand and her coffee steaming in the other. Two of the watches stood at the receiver within a nanosecond. The third stood forty point one nanoseconds away. Forty nanoseconds is nothing; forty nanoseconds is a number; the two are different sizes, and he stood in the road between them all morning. He downloaded the manufacturer's advisory himself, before anyone could do it for him: it existed, it described sporadic excursions of tens of nanoseconds in units that had undergone a synchronization transition during cold soak; his had undergone no transition below; and the advisory went into the log anyway, live, the way wounds stay. He sent Ines Marques the raw files, per instruction.

On the twenty-third he ran it again with the design tightened: the third watch above beside the receiver, living the control's life; two below with him, unworn, one temperature, the thermologger flat to two-tenths of a degree all night. Nilay witnessed the dawn. The two that had been below agreed with each other to three-tenths of a nanosecond, and both stood forty from the watch above and from the sky. Same sign. Same figure. Twice.

Twice is a result. He had said it to himself for thirty years about things that had never once obliged him, and at 06:12, entering the column, he wept at the laptop — without drama, the way it comes to old men, at a desk, over arithmetic — and let it have its ten minutes, and logged nothing of it, there being no field in the table. Nilay came as far as the door with the coffee and went away with it. Marques's reply was cold and fair: *Find the heat you are missing. Correlated excursions are not in the advisory — I checked; note that no one at that company ever wrote a sentence so exculpatory by accident; note also that engineers write annexes to please whoever is asking. Two nights is an anecdote with a witness. I will come in January and we will be unlucky together. — I.M.*

Priska, before she drove to Kayseri for cartridges and a seminar, sat with him and built his forks in a column — thermal, firmware, reference, and Márton, the fourth fork being himself, the common element he could not exclude — and left the page square on his table where he could not miss it, which was the tenderest thing anyone did for him all winter. "Your clocks came back changed," she said. "Mine only breathe. I do not like it, Professor, and I am not required to." The tea she did not pour. At the door she said, "Wear the mask below the second door," and he said, "The body is the oven, Priska; I am keeping the oven honest," and she looked at him the way she looked at a gradient she could not instrument, and left.

That night he stood a long time at the whiteboard, which had carried one line at the top since September — *A TRAVERSABLE THROAT NEEDS NEGATIVE ENERGY: WHERE IS THE GENERATOR?* — and under it he wrote the season: the Casimir pittance; 29 microgal; 14 µGal, three still mornings; 11 cm; 40 ns, twice; and under the season, the design of a third night. And at the bottom, in the small hand he used for letters home, unbidden, from a source he could not cite: *or: what is the fuel?*

On the twenty-sixth he wrote to Marques that he would wait for her. *January, then. I will not go down alone; Priska has made me a column and I have read it.* He wiped the design of the third night off the whiteboard, leaving the throat and the fuel, and slept that night better than he had all month.

The parcel came by post at an entirely ordinary speed, on the twenty-seventh, tied in twine and closed with a bone button. Inside, complete, the Carpathian file of 1997: the survey pages; the board minutes; the referee's report with its footnote and its word, *wishful*, standing where wounds are legally inflicted; his own withdrawn preprint, with his own pencil in the margins, though he had a memory of feeding that preprint to a wastebasket in Uppsala — the wastebasket had evidently been emptied into an archive; everything is, eventually. And the manufacturer's engineering annex, which he had never held or had held and left unopened through twenty-eight years of being reasonable, one sentence of which he read standing in the yard in his shirtsleeves: *The advisory condition cannot produce correlated excursions between instruments.*

The acquittal had been in the file all his exile. It was, like everything in that country, both clean and unclean: an annex is a document written by men who had never met his clocks, and engineers please whoever is asking; he knew all that; he stood with it anyway. At the bottom of the parcel, a card in the slope: *For your peace. — A. Halden.* How the Trust held the file, he did not ask. In July he had written that sponsors hold archives, and that it is what sponsoring is; the sentence had come home to roost, with a bone button. And there was one enclosure more, a stringer's note from the valley, filed as color: the old monk had told the survey crew that the undercroft had always had someone in it; that he swept it as his mother's people had; the stringer had kept the sentence because it was cheap. Márton read it twice and did not know what to do with it, and put it in the back pages of the log, where things waited to become data.

On the evening of the twenty-seventh he wrote the third night back on the whiteboard, from memory, in the same order: the watches worn, for once — the one variable he had never excluded was himself, and inclusion was a kind of honesty — and the night kept at the fourth door, at the head of the low way, at the mouth of the one place on the hill where every battery died and the strongest anomaly of the summer gave no reading at all. # X. The Autopsy of a Quiet Man

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me:*

*He came to measure in the year of the short harvest, in the cold season, on foot, old and knowing old, and he carried a chain of a hundred links coiled on his shoulder as one carries a sleeping child: because it cannot be left and it will not walk.*

*He gave his name at the mouth before it was asked, and he asked for the names of the rooms, which the measurers never do, and I gave them, and he set them somewhere inside himself with his other numbers, and was pleased, and I was pleased that he was pleased.*

*The brass instrument had a small eye in it, for looking along the world. He taught me its name and I have let the name go; the names of tools do not keep, down here, where the tools themselves keep for centuries. The chain was honest. A hundred links, every one the length of its neighbor, which is more than can be said for days.*

*He measured slowly, resting between the rooms as though between the rooms there were rooms. He was four days at it. On the fourth evening he ate nothing and said the count was nearly finished, in the voice of a man reporting a mercy. In the night the fever found him — fevers travel the roads also — and by the grey he could not be carried, the road up being long when the legs have resigned their post, and he would not have the road down either, being where he had asked to be. On the sixth morning he asked whether his count would be kept. We told him the wall is kept. He heard it the way stone hears rain, which is wholly, and in no hurry, and that was well.*

*He went out on the ebb, the breath shorter, and then the breath part of the general quiet. We did what is done. You will do it too, and it is better to have heard the manner than the man, for the manner stays and the men do not.*

*We have always buried the ones who come to measure. Priska Vogel had stood at seven recoveries underground in twenty-six years, on three continents, and at every one of them the air had been guilty. That was the etiquette of her trade: air is guilty until it is ventilated. Guilt had given her a profession, and a mercy to hand out with it, and the two had always arrived together, which was the whole of her luck and most of her character. She had never once stood at a cave mouth in the position she held on the last morning of December, which was the position of a chemist whose instrument had just been asked to acquit a hill.

Melek found him, because the round is the round. She came to the dig house in the grey with the oil can still in her hand and said, in the voice she kept for weather: "The professor is at the fourth door. His lamp is out. He does not answer me." And at the step, having reported the whole of it: "I did not touch him. You will want him as he sat."

Priska had come back from Kayseri the evening before, in snow, past his window, and the window had been dark, and she had thought: sensible man, and gone to her stove and her tables. She thought about that all the way down the hill to the co-op phone, and the phone was answered at the second ring by a man who asked her twice for the name of the village, as if the village were the difficult part.

The gendarmes came up from the town in two cars, and the prosecutor's men behind them, and they would not go below the second door without her. Nobody in that country ever had. So she went first with the meter, reading the air down as she had read it up all summer, and the numbers behaved like stairs in reverse: 0.5 at the second door, 0.4 at the third, and at the fourth, mild and slow, 0.4 and holding — the best air on the hill, measured on the only instrument licensed to hold the opinion. The snow had come on after he went down, and his sky had held at the mouth; Melek said so to the gendarmes, and nothing else.

He sat where the wall lets a body sit, at the head of the low way, upright against the rounded stone, his right hand flat on his knee as she had last seen it flat on his own table, and the stillness of him was procedural, total, already not his. The lamp stood where the lamps go, cold, burned down to the last of its oil, the wick folded black over. The three watches were on his right wrist — *where the artery is closest*, he had said in July, of the left. The scene officer gloved them off one at a time. Two had stopped, at 03:12 and at 05:41, in the cold, on the wrist of a dying man; the third was running, and agreed with the sky, and went on agreeing in its evidence bag, and the young gendarme who carried it out could not stop looking at it, and neither could she.

Nobody had knocked back that night. Nobody had waited out the breaths with him. The dark had had him on the plainest terms there are, and there was a reading for that too — he had knocked, softly, all December, and been answered, or not — and she stood at the mouth in the snow and could not make herself pick it up.

Her readings went into the file under her signature, and it was in signing them that the splinter from the summer went off. *Considered indicative*: the Trust's word had sat in her binder all winter, splinter-sized. Now she stood in it to the wrist. The acquittal of the air was anecdotal. Its conviction, had there been one, would have been anecdotal too. The hill stood exactly where the Trust's lines had always left it — unconvicted, unacquitted, and open — and she could not prove the man had died in good air, and could not prove the contrary, and had never in her life been unable to prove the air of anything.

The manufacturer was asked about the watches, and was sorry. The advisory covered excursions; it did not cover endings. Send the rest either way. — I.M.*

The inquest sat on the ninth of January in the room above the co-op, which had counted consents in June and explanations in September and now, with its stove drawing badly and its ledger weights out, counted a man. The prosecutor's clerk read the scene sketch aloud, and among its elevations, flat as all the rest: *at the threshold of the fourth door, one thousand one hundred forty-two point five meters above the sea.* She had heard a figure spoken twice into that hill once, in July, by a man who believed that figures spoken twice are harder for the world to quietly amend. He heard her out with the whole of his attention, which was the most disquieting thing about him, and then he asked, presently, whether the thirteenth bore of her canton was still closed for lining. # XI. The Fence

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me:*

*She came down in the dry season, on the arm of a boy fetched from the threshing floor, and she carried her lamp wrapped against her chest the way one carries a thing with a bone in it. I knew her before I knew her name, and I never learned the name, and it did not matter. Keepers know each other by the hands. Hers had kept the round so long that the round had gotten into them, and they went on with it while she rested on the boy's arm, filling and pinching at the air of the stair, small fractions of the office, faithful as water finding its level.*

*She had kept this house a long time. In the year the house changed hands she had gone up, and had carried her lamp with her, and no one had said her nay. A keeper who goes up takes her lamp; that is the custom, and the custom does not explain itself any more than the rest of them do. Now she was old past the arguing with, and she had come down the stair one last time to do what she had been meaning to do for a lifetime, which was to set the lamp down.*

*It is not given back, she told me. A lamp is not a debt. It is set down, and someone after sets it going again, and that is the whole of the manner. There is no ceremony. The filling is the ceremony.*

*I filled it for her, to the first knuckle of her thumb, because hers was the knuckle the rule had been measured on, and she watched the oil with the attention of a woman counting change she did not doubt. The wick she pinched herself, once, with nails that had forgotten nothing, and I brought the flame and held it near, and waited, and the wick came to it, and she said the words that are said at a lighting, which are no words, only the breath out.*

*Then she asked for the wall. I walked her the length of it, slow, and she read the lines with her finger held a finger's width off the stone, touching nothing, the way the taught read, and at the far end, among the oldest cuts, she stood a long time. I did not ask at what. She did not look for herself among the lines. She had taught me that herself, without ever saying it: looking for yourself in something long is how people get lost.*

*She slept by the lamps. In the morning she ate a little, and was carried up into the grey, and I did not follow past the lamp. I never do.*

*Her lamp stands in the ninth niche. The black above that niche was hers for forty years before it was mine, layer under layer, and it is the only archive she ever needed, and it is full of her, and of everyone before her, and it has never once been opened, having never once been shut.*

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me: when one comes with a lamp to set down, take it, and fill it, and give it a niche, and do not make a ceremony of it. The filling is the ceremony. A lamp set down is a lamp begun again. That is the whole of the teaching. It is short, and it has always been enough.*

The thaw came in March, and the pilgrims came with it.

The Ministry fence had gone up in February, galvanized, from the cemetery wall to the thorn, with a gate and a lock and a sign in two languages saying nothing either of them meant. The frame had stopped being news by then and become infrastructure. The pin on the map was a fixture with reviews. Weekends put candles in jars along the mulberries, thermoses, a queue for the flat stone; the torch man from Kayseri had found stock.

Recep was Melek's nephew, her sister's son, and all winter he had sold water and parking on the flat stone below the mulberries, politely, out of his aunt's sightline. In April he had the list laminated. Knocking tour, story, view; children half price; the queue at dusk. A pilgrim asked him, that May, which side of the rail to photograph. "Both," he said. "Inside is extra." The etiquette itself he gave away free, and it had survived the internet intact: two knocks with the flat of the hand, and two breaths of waiting. Melek, who owned no phone, knew the price list to the lira. "Company," she said, when Nilay asked her about the queue, "is a word for the right amount."

The knock was first answered on a still evening in May. Low, once, from under the threshold, and the season never went back to before it. The answers came at a rising rate after that, on still evenings, in still air, and a clip of one of them did numbers that were a little sister to four million, and the fence grew a second rail.

Nilay listed the explanations: the shaft's own tide, which breathed on a schedule nobody had ever identified; the tuff ticking as the day's heat let go of it — either of which Priska could have instrumented; boys, since gates have cousins; Kaya Bey's hermits, a hidden order with room in it for a knock. She filled the ninth lamp to the knuckle. "He was not a curious man. Thank God for it. A curious man would have asked after children, and there were none to ask after."

"You had a husband," Nilay said, because the village had one, and kept it for her like a spare key.

"The village gave me a husband, because a woman who carries water carries it for somebody. I let them. It was nearer the truth than most stories, and cheaper than most." She set the lamp in its niche. "If it was him, he kept his manners. If it was not him, then I have been faithful to a room. Either way the water was drunk by morning, and going out I cried, every year, and not from sorrow, kızım. Because it had been enough." And at the end of it, flat, both hands: "I never settled it. Forty years, and I never settled, and now I am out of winters, and the not-settling is the steadiest thing I own."

She died in the third week of June, in the grey, with the round done. She had put the oil can into Nilay's hands two days before, with both hands, the way she poured tea. "The room chooses the one who stays," she said. "It chose me sixty years ago and I argued for a month, which was a month wasted. Don't argue with it. It has more time than you." Then: "Company comes. You be the right size." And last, the instruction that was not one: "They're yours on trial." — "Until when?" — "Until they're not."

They buried her by the low wall, and the grief had the shape of a sending-off, and no one said where she was being sent, there being no need; the village had always assumed it, as it assumed the harvest, without dates. Sungur Hanım said, at the head of the grave, to no one the eye could find: "Forty years she carried the water down. Let it be poured for her now." The women sang her song, badly, twice, and Nilay's mother's mouth moved with it, which was memory or which was the village, and there had never been a test that could tell those apart. Halime stood at the edge of the graveside, where the women had put her by putting her nowhere, and Nilay stood beside her for the whole of it, and neither of them asked the other anything, and the burial went on. The other sentence went round the graveside after, arriving everywhere like carried water: the room chooses the one who stays. Yusuf came up from Derinkuyu and stood at the back and left before the bread, which was its own attendance. Priska stood the burial hour at the watcher's stone and calibrated at the hour she always calibrated.

On the third day the women came to the dig house with a school notebook bound in oilcloth, swollen with years of oil, its corners gone soft as cloth. "The round is not taught in mourning," Sungur Hanım said. "Not this year, and not by us; don't ask which of the two is the reason, because we couldn't tell you either. She left you her hand. The book is hers, turned to the first page; the wall will teach you the rest. Before first frost, kızım. The house can go a night dark. It cannot go a winter." At the door: "They're yours on trial." — "Who says?" — "The house," she said, and would not improve on it.

No document had handed Nilay anything in that country, and this was not a document. The office had come to her by refusal, which was how offices moved there: what is given can be resented, and what is refused must be taken up, and the taking up is the whole of the training.

On the ninth day she went down with the tape. The order still stood — no one below the second door, pending everything — but orders accrete manners the way hills accrete paths. The boy at the gate was sixteen and had been issued one instruction by whoever issues boys their instructions: the keeper passes. He passed her with the gravity of a boy holding his whole duty in one sentence, and she went down unlawful and appointed, in the same body, which was, she supposed, the definition of a keeper that the county had arrived at.

She gave her name at the mouth. At the head of the deep stair she knocked with the flat of her hand, twice, softly, and stood the two breaths entire, and nothing answered.

The singing room took the song the way it took everything, sternum first. She set the little machine on the stone where the lamps go and pressed what was not the record button, being scrupulous about that in advance, for a reason she declined to read. Melek's voice went down into the room, and the room did with it what it did: set it down elsewhere, in rooms her legs would never enter. The tape ran out at the end of the second verse, and the spools turned free, small and mechanical, and the after-quiet came in like furniture.

And in the after-quiet, once, the closing phrase came back.

Not from the machine. From the room's own air, off no wall she could point to — a young woman's voice, level, without performance, in the manner of someone returning a borrowed thing: *and the lamps are kept.*

She stood the two breaths. Then she answered, once, because that is the etiquette, and past the answer, nothing: "The singing room." And the quiet after, which is where it has always happened.

She built the explanations on the climb, methodically. The room returns notes; perhaps it returns whatever it is given, and a voice is a note with a family. Her own ear had been fed the phrase all week — the women had sung it at the grave, badly, twice — and an ear fed that long will forage. The machine's batteries were two winters old. And there was Kaya Bey's order, which allowed for a young woman with a cold, or a calling, in the deep rooms of a fenced hill, uncaught by a boy with one sentence. She laid all four side by side, and none of them would carry the weight, and none of them would break.

At the dig house she scrubbed the tape. Both passes of the song were Melek's, only Melek's, and the silences between the verses held nothing a machine would agree to. She did not take the tape down again. A second time would have been asking, and she had been thirteen months in that country and knew the manners by heart. In the notebook she wrote: *Heard: the closing phrase, once, in a voice that was not hers and was not the machine's. Cannot make it a finding. Am not trying to.* And under it: *I don't know what I heard. I know what I answered.*

Seher was at the mouth the same dusk, on her mat, inside Priska's threshold, with a pile of unmarked homework going grey beside her.

"I asked the Doctor, in the winter, what I was supposed to do now," she said. "I know now. When your order lifts, I'll sit below again, where it used to come to my left. And I can name what it costs, Hanım, if you're writing it down. It costs the question. You put the question down the way you put down a load at a door, and then your hands are free, and the hands are all the work there is."

"And if the question is all you had?"

"Then you carry it a while first," Seher said. "That's the other half of the price. Nobody collects it all at once."

Nilay kept her first round from the book on the last dusk of June, alone, steady, corrected by nothing. The oil went to the first knuckle of her own thumb and no further, and her knuckle was not Melek's knuckle, and the difference was millimeters, and the difference was everything. The wick she pinched with her nails, pinched and never cut; her nails were a committee where Melek's had been a decision, but the third pinch stood true. Then the flame, carried cupped and low, near the body, offered and not pushed; near, and wait; and the wick came to it, and the first niche filled, and she stood in the light of a thing that was not hers, being only the latest hand on an errand older than the oil.

She came up at full dark with the oil smell on her hands. At the mouth of Kırk Oda the air shaft breathed. The breath went out for a long while, mild, carrying stone and cold water and something like the inside of a pocket; then a pause long enough to be mistaken for stillness; then in again, the same length, the same patience — a tide with no sea. Anyone with a watch and an hour to waste could time it, and anyone who timed it came away with the same figure: twenty minutes, and twenty again, obedient to nothing anyone had ever identified, keeping its own time the way the sea keeps the moon's.


# XII. The Threshold

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me:*

*The house is quiet tonight in the way I like best, which is the way of a long room after the round, when the oil is sweet and the counting is done and the dark is only dark, being nobody's errand until morning. On such a night I will set down the far lamplighters, because you will be asked about them — by the curious, or the frightened, or once by a child who was neither — and it is better to be asked with the answer already ripened.*

*I have never seen them. My teacher saw them twice; her teacher once, or in a story; and at this depth a story and a memory are one water. What we hold of them is the manner, and the manner keeps itself: the basin filled, the fallen thing set upright, the edge of the light kept to, the name of a room given and nothing asked past it. Manners travel farther than people do. That is what manners are for.*

*The far lamplighters walk the road farther along than we do.*

*I said that for years in my teacher's voice, because it was hers, and a guess costs nothing to inherit. It is my belief now. I could not tell you the day it changed, because there was no day. Nothing happened. I kept the lamps, and the guess ripened, the way oil sweetens if it is kept past all arguing with. If you want the whole of this teaching in one sentence: a belief is a keeping, kept.*

*Do not trouble the length of the road. Far is a word the surface needs; the house has no use for it. The house is long, and the road is one road, and the light at the far end of it is not another light than this one — the same errand, at a longer patience. When they come, and they come seldom, you will find you have nothing to say to one another, and nothing is the correct amount; the water will be set out, the visit will be the visit, and it will have been enough. It has always been enough.*

*I do not know what they keep. Something with a wall in it, I think, from the manner of their waiting. We keep ours, and they keep theirs, and the wall is one wall, at many hands' distances.*

*There is no mark on the wall for belief. The wall keeps days and diggings. Beliefs it keeps another way, by keeping keepers, which is slower, and surer.*

*The oil is measured. The wicks are pinched. The lamp beside me stands its light straight, which is how I know the road is quiet tonight, all of its length that it is mine to know.*

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me: when the guess ripens in you — and it will; keeping ripens it — you will not be able to name the day either. Do not try. Say it low, to the lamps. They were ripening before either of us.*

Yusuf spent the summer of his twentieth year in a house that drank downward, keeping his uncle's feed accounts in a school notebook because his uncle distrusted any arithmetic that could not be checked by hand, and the phone stayed dark, and on Tuesdays he sat at his cousin's laptop for ten minutes, with the door open to the yard, and let the world say what it had to say. The world said it monthly, in the tone of men who had never once been underground.

The first came in April and the second in May, and they were one animal with two signatures: a consultancy that was two surnames and an ampersand, an office in Ankara and another in Düsseldorf, subjects ascending politely from his channel to anomalous chronometry in subterranean assets. They requested raw materials, logs, metadata; they named their fees at standard rates. The May one cited the failed transfer at ninety-seven percent — a number he had said aloud exactly once in his life, at minute forty-one of his last stream, in September, and had gone back twice, on the cousin's laptop, to hear a flat young voice in his own accent say, with no memory anywhere in him of being that voice. There was a per diem in it, and travel, and a preliminary conversation concerning timing resilience in GNSS-denied environments. He understood about a third of the sentence and all of it: clocks that could not be told the hour by the sky. Caves were where you would school such clocks. He was twenty years old, and it was the first sentence all year that made the hill sound small.

The third, in June, offered to purchase recovered media assets outright, and thanked, in smaller type beneath the signature, a directorate in Ankara for its continued interest. That was the appetite, in a footer.

In July one came on paper, franked by a machine, addressed in a font that had never met him, and his aunt read the envelope's shape from the door of the yard and said nothing, and brought it in with the eggs. The inbox had found the family. He answered none of them. The changelog stood in the field book, by hand: *received 9.iv; received 12.v; received 3.vi; received, paper, 19.vii; answered: 0.*

The Registrar's letter had arrived in the middle of April, by post, copied out by hand in the slope they all knew now. It was dated the tenth of April. The first email had come on the ninth, to an address he had never once made public. He laid the two dates side by side, the way he laid everything now.

*Dear Mr. Demir, — The Trust asks me to observe that correspondence of a technical character will find you in the ordinary course, and that you are under no obligation to begin what you have not begun. An archive answers to its keeper, and to no one else. — A. Halden, Registrar.*

He spent an evening on the ordinary accounts, and they were good ones: a directorate in Ankara that talked, as directorates do, to whoever wrote to it by hand; a registry with friends; the Trust's young woman, still in the country. And under those, the other reading, the one that warmed: a man whose business was knowing when appetite woke, warning a boy out of the road of it — and the warning worth exactly as much as the knowing, and both a kindness, and both something else. He stopped needing to choose between them, and noticed that he had stopped, and filed the noticing. Some things you sign in April and only find out in June.

In the middle of July he took the channel down.

It had been dark since the autumn, and dark was not gone: four years of evenings still stood there, four million people's door onto a hill that now had a fence on it; and in the drafts folder sat the big post, unsent since the Monday and Tuesday he had drafted it, a loaded thing in a drawer of a house with four million windows. He had held the channel for four years. He had never once kept it. Holding was a grip; keeping, the hill had taught him at the hill's own speed, was an errand — set by somebody, carried to somebody — and he was not on that road, and had no right to hold its door either shut or open.

Before he touched anything he found gülce, small letters, yellow flower, and scrolled her column: *the wicks are good tonight*, in August; *again?*, in October; *still here*, in December; *again?*, in February; nothing since April. A night-shift diary in one-line entries, from a person who in four years had never once asked what was down there. He answered the last one, months late, in the small letters that were the register of that place:

*you asked for the wick hour. it isn't coming back from me. the lamps are kept — someone else keeps them now, and she was taught. i'm taking it all down. you wrote that some of us watch for the lamps. keep watching. they're kept.*

He thought, before typing it, of gülce's column — four years of two o'clock, a ward or a bakery somewhere — and typed a second line after it, and sat looking at what he had typed: *science replaced the priest at the bedside. who replaces the lullaby?* He deleted it, because it was a sermon and she had never once asked him for one, and pressed the button.

The platform asked him whether he was sure, and he was, and it asked again as though it had not heard the first sure, and in the pause before the second confirming he found the decision already in him, load-bearing, older than the clicking, and could not have said when it had been put there. He clicked. Then it said, courteously: *This action cannot be undone. Your data will be prepared for download.* The platform had learned, at the end, to write like the Registrar — final, quiet, promising paperwork — without any of the reasons.

Fourteen days later, to the Tuesday, the link came. The archive was four years of his life in folders, and he went through it as you go through a house you are leaving. The drafts folder held the big post, word for word, the village and the road and the table. He deleted it unwritten a second time, and this time the deletion was a decision, and the decision had a date. And there was a folder the manifest called RECOVERABLE — the servers, in whatever cold north they lived in, had spent the winter holding what the card had lost; the update he had avoided since June had turned on, somewhere in its settings, an uploading he had never asked for; error-checking keeps what memory loses, which is the whole difference between a server and a person. Most of it was grey squares and checksums apologizing. One item the manifest listed as playable. 0:14. The September date.

He did not play it. He sat with his finger over it for a while, and the reasons filed themselves in order, like men at a gate. Four honest people had come up from that hour with four different hours, and a fifth hour could only break the tie, and that tie, if it was ever to break, would break in a register and not in a yard on a Tuesday, in the mood of an algorithm. There was the basin besides, which his own report had already put one extra person into, and he did not know whether he wanted that confirmed or cleared, and not knowing was the kind of information that should cost more than a click. He copied the single file to a stick bought at the phone shop in Derinkuyu for the price of two coffees, and logged it by hand in the field book: *recovered: 14 sec. watched: 0.* The zero was the proudest number he had.

Viewed: no.* She wrote *viewed: no* as a property of the object, the way one writes dimensions, and he understood that this was the most respectful sentence anyone had spent on his four years of showing things. Above the gate the duct's fan turned, six strokes to the minute, somewhere between a clock and a breeze; the spare meter had clicked like that at his hip all one season, and he had liked it, company being company. Inside the fence, on the flat by the mouth, a mat, and someone on it, still the way furniture is still; the order allowed the mouth to be sat, and somebody sat it, every night, and he did not need to know who.

The last light went off Erciyes, and the hill put on the color it put on, and at the lip of the first door — where the overhang's shadow had already eaten the grey — a figure stood.

Layered. The color the hill was at that hour, which was the color everything was at that hour; he took that explanation at once, gratefully, and held it. The figure stood with its toes at the threshold stone and not over it. One step from inside. The step not spent.

Then it bent, unhurried, the way nothing in a queue is unhurried, and laid a forearm along the threshold, elbow to fingertip, and held the measure, and took it once again. He had seen that exact spending of attention one other time, in September, a woman measuring a cut door in a polished room. The gesture was a word in a language he did not speak, and he knew it again as one knows a face.

His body did the learned thing before he was asked: went still. His hand went to the pocket where the phone slept and rested on it, as you rest a hand on a retired dog. And because the manners were in his body now and made no objection to absurdity, he knocked — there was nothing to knock on but the top rail of the fence, and he knocked on that, twice, with the flat of his hand, softly — and waited out the two breaths.

Below, faint and far down the throat of the hill, a lamp came up — the round reaching the near rooms — and the light lay along the floor of the mouth and out to the threshold, and touched nothing's feet. The dusk thickened by one more degree, like a page turning. The lip stood empty. He could not say whether it had gone in or gone up the hill; in the last light the two were the same size; and he did not go to the fence and lean and look, and he did not examine the not-going. He had paid that hill enough tuition to know what examining cost.

Kemal's mother gave him the bed behind the stove and her opinions about the fence, and in the night the weather turned, and rain went over the hill in the dark, steady, like everything that country sent. Under the overhang of the first door, where no rain had been able to reach, the dust held a print. Woven. Human. And the print was old. Not made-at-dusk old. The edges stood rounded, the ridges of the weave worn shallow, dust settled back into it as though it had carried weather. Rain deletes; every winter of his life had taught him so. He could not make the print read new. It read as weeks, while the rain outside the overhang had taken every other mark the season had made. Old dust under an overhang holds marks for weeks; the co-op wove those sandals; the age of a print in old dust was nothing a boy could date and nothing Yusuf could either — he had watched a wall of marks for a season and never once caught one admitting its age. He entered it in the field book, by hand, with the date, and no verdict, and gave his name at the mouth on the way down, aloud, to nobody, first, which the boy let pass without comment, being sixteen and wise.

Nobody was coming. The lamp did not mind — being an errand, and not a show — and he stayed until the wick wanted pinching, and pinched it, and went up to bed with the light burning below him in the house: the first thing he had ever kept that could not be viewed.


# XIII. The Measure

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me:*

*I said I would set down the manner of the last mark, and the round is done, and the oil is measured, and there is no better hour coming, there being no better hours, only this one, with the lamp beside me.*

*The wall is long. You will say it also; every keeper says it; and every keeper is surprised by the same thing, which is not the length but the ending of it. I had cut my lines low, beside the others, all my keeping, and never once looked for the end, having been taught not to look for myself in anything long. This morning I went to cut the day's mark, and there was no beside left.*

*Room, room, room, and then stone that is only stone. I put my hand flat where the next line goes, and the hand said the truth, which is that the wall has a tomorrow exactly one line wide, and after that it has what walls have.*

*You will want to ask whether it is the last of my keeping or the last of the wall's. The question stands on wrong feet. Nothing in the manner distinguishes them, and the manner is the whole of what I know. The first mark was cut by a hand that did not know it was first. I have thought about that all day. The wall begins in ignorance, the way the day does, and it is right that it end there also, and I have not been paid the ignorance, and the not-being-paid is what the hesitation is.*

*I did not cut it this morning. I set that down plainly, because the teaching is in the not, and because you will stand here and will want to know that a keeper may keep a night uncut and the house not suffer for it. I trimmed lamps that did not need trimming. I swept where I had swept. The stone waited where I had left it, being stone, and the waiting of stone is the best company there is for a hesitation, being the same temperature as one.*

*Understand the hesitation before you inherit it. It is not fear. Fear spends itself where it is allowed to, and I allowed it an hour at noon and it spent. It is not doubt of the mark; the mark is a small thing, one line, low, and the hand is willing. The hesitation is a courtesy. The wall has set down every day it was given, in order, without one skipped and without one explained, and the last of them should not arrive sideways, in a hurry, with the oil still wet on the keeper. If the wall can be patient the length of a hand, the keeper can be patient the length of a night.*

*And there is the other half, which I will say and stop. The lines before my line were cut by keepers who never met me, and the wall will be walked by keepers I will never meet, and where I am standing the two facts are the same size, and I have not decided which side of my own hand I am on. Perhaps that is not a keeper's decision. Perhaps it is the only one there is.*

*When it is cut — at the grey, or whenever the hesitation has spent itself entire — it will go in as the others went in: one line, low, no different, no deeper. That is the mercy, and I will not be the keeper who breaks it. Then the wall will be all wall, first mark to last, and the dark after the last will be the dark before the first. I know this the way you know a round is finished: not by the count, which never closes, but by the house, which quiets.*

*The knife is good. The stone is good. The bowl is covered against the dust. The hand is the third thing, and it is willing, and it is waiting, and those are not the same.*

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me: when the lines run out under your hand, do not count what room is left. Cut the line, or keep the night, or both. The wall has never once been in a hurry. It is not going to begin with you.*

It was bound in green gone the color of bottle glass, and the gilt of its spine had browned to a rumor, and what the gilt still said was *Nothing to Record*, and above the words, small, the shelf-mark: *0*. She had expected the founder's hand and got it — the hard hand of the slip in her pocket — page after page of it, an old man's inventory of an idea.

The Kırk Oda notes of 1924 came first, and in them she met a donkey she knew. *The keeper's clock retards one hour upon each night below; she sets it by the church bell; a clock is a donkey, she says; you beat it and it walks. Nothing to record.* Melek's grandmother, Melek's story, Melek's voice over the wicks — and under all of them, a century down, the founder's pen, which had heard it first and filed it as furniture. She turned the page, and there was the sampling: *Took a finger's cup of the hand-mark, lower wall. The keeper was told. Was sorry.* Item fifty-one had been sorry. She sat with that a moment and did not laugh, and did not entirely not.

At the back of the volume the annual lines were bound in, one to a page, forty and more, typescripts and hands aging down the stack, never varying by a word. And before them, the List itself — each site, one line, some in the hard hand, some later:

> *Newgrange — no lamp after 1974.*
> *Derinkuyu — date unknown; rock cannot testify.*
> *Ħal Saflieni — lower level, 1940: do not correct.*
> *Dowth — A.H. — no further vigils, 1971.*
> *Ayios Nikandros — kept nightly; quiet since 1961.*

Two lines on the page were in the slope, Dowth and Saflieni, and neither explained itself. Under *do not correct* she heard a guide's voice on a cold stair: *The Trust does not correct what it did not record.* And *no further vigils, 1971*: at Kırk Oda there had been no further vigils below the second door since January either, bar one old woman's, and she had not thought until now to ask whose tea that January had been poured at. She was on the next site before she felt the page's temperature. Ayios Nikandros had a folder. A drowned chapel — the reservoir had taken it in '55 — and quarterly remittance counterfoils in the founder's hand, regular as a pulse, ceasing in 1949, with no letter of explanation. And one page that was nobody's own: the water authority's summer wage-lines, 1955 through 1961 — *fetched the lamp; set the lamp; paid* — paid by nobody the page named, and after the summer of '61 the line stopped mid-column, unreconciled, the way lines stop when whoever keeps them stops.

Someone had taken a lamp out by boat to a drowned church every summer night for seven years, on a wage nobody would put a name to, and then had stopped; and the List's line under the chapel's name said what came after: *quiet*.

Folded among the last counterfoils, unenveloped, unsent, four pages in the hard hand. And paper-clipped to the front of it all, a photograph, browned, squared-off: a man at the lip of a sunken passage with his hat in both hands, taken from above and behind, so that the face was three-quarters available and no use whatsoever. She did the arithmetic twice. It declined both times to close. The man in the photograph was the man across the desk, or his father, or the son of a family in which stillness bred true; and beneath the photograph, in the slope, was written: *A.H., Dowth, Michaelmas 1949.*

"Nineteen forty-nine," she said.

"Yes."

"The Trust has had two registrars."

"The Register records the sites, Dr. Arat. It does not record the recorders."

The room accepted this the way rooms accepted him, without anyone being able to say afterward what had shifted. She read the letter instead, because the letter was easier, being only a dead man's whole position on everything.

> *To the Trustees — and to whichever of them holds the Register when this is read, if it is ever read, which I doubt, having no intention to send it.*
>
> *A place can be known, or believed, or left. I had thought these three doors into one room. They are three roads, and I have walked each to its end on paper, which is the only place I have the legs for.*
>
> *Known, a place becomes an asset: measured to its end, fenced, tolled, and loved to death by people who mean well. Believed, it becomes a banner, and banners are carried into wars. Left, it goes to the rain, which asks no questions and keeps nothing.*
>
> *I stopped the Nikandros remittances in a season of economies, as an economy. The ledger since says the keeping continued, unpaid, which I had no right to expect and cannot now put right. This letter is not sent, because sending it would be recording it, and I will not found a registry of my own wrong guesses. Whoever holds the Register after me: pay the keepers. We have one buried hill already on the books, and no way to ask it whether it was satisfied.*

The keeping had continued — the List said *kept nightly*, and the reservoir said since '55 by boat, and the line said *quiet since 1961* — and whether the chapel had been kept faithfully to the last and gone quiet anyway, or had been kept only to the edge of the money's memory and then not at all, the folder did not say, and the lake could not be asked. The remittances had never resumed. There was nothing left to remit to. She could not choose between the two, and did not try.

One thing more in the volume, and he turned to it himself, in his own time, which she understood to mean it was the reason for the evening. A protocol, numbered, the founder's manner at its most notarial:

> *The Vigil of the Sealed Hour (the founder's manner, 1924).*
> *1. The keeper writes, by her own hand, a letter, and says nothing of it.*
> *2. The letter is sealed in wax before witnesses, who countersign the seal.*
> *3. A watch is stopped at an hour chosen and witnessed.*
> *4. Letter and watch are laid in a box of tin, and the box in the niche that answers, before dark.*
> *5. The vigil is kept to the morning. What the morning holds is not prescribed.*

And in the margin, in a hand between the hard hand and the slope, aged by water or by nothing: *Result: inconclusive. Box returned to this office by post, 1951, wax unbroken, contents as deposited, plus one item: [inked out].*

The ink had held for seventy years. It would hold for seventy more. She tried the explanations because her hands wanted work: an accomplice; the founder's own retrieval; a deposit never made where the depositor believed; a hoax with a timetable and excellent handwriting. The annals did not record the item's name, having recorded everything else to the comma, and the comma was the cruelest part.

She had brought her own matters as one brings a purse into a market, meaning to spend carefully, and she found she had already taken them out.

"The calibration memorandum," she said. "Priska's certificate surfaced in October, after the season had used it." The explanations for that one stood in a row, worn smooth from handling: a filing failure; a service covering itself; a registry that read reports more closely than ministries do, and did not touch the canon, only the number's provenance. None of them had ever once required a villain, which was the property of the thing she minded most about it.

"And the closure," she said. "The fourteenth. Priska wrote on the fifteenth."

And she stopped. The third was in her mouth and would come out as nothing but an accusation, and accusations want a court, and she did not have one; she had a drawer.

He supplied it himself, as a courtesy, the way a clerk supplies a folio number.

"And the hospital?"

"The transfer," he said, "was the least I could do."

The tea had been cold for an hour. Outside, Valletta put on its lamps, and the office did the work of nothing in particular, and the sentence stood in the room the way weather stands, being true in every direction she turned it: a kindness, arranged; an arrangement, done kindly; the least of a great many things that could be done. There was a fourth hinge, the oldest, in the same slope, dated the seventh of September 1999. She knew it by heart. She set it down nowhere, and he did not pick it up, and between them, on the desk, it took up no room at all.

"You have kept excellent records," he said.

She could not find the sentence's weight — whether it was praise, or a receipt, or the edge of something with a great deal more behind it — and she had learned, in a year in that country, not to lift what she could not find the weight of.

He wrapped the volume himself. Brown paper, twine, a bone button; the tin box and the wax on top, undescribed. "It doesn't leave this floor," she said, reciting his rule back to him because it was the only handhold in reach. "It has never yet had cause."

He gave her the weight of it in both hands, the way one gives tea, and at the head of the stair, holding the cupboard-wide door, he asked the only question of the evening that she would carry home unopened.

"Would you want it explained, if explaining it ended it?"

She did not answer. He had not expected her to; that had never been the office the question held.

"Consider overnight," he said. "Safe over the water, Dr. Arat." And the grey stair took her down past the eucalyptus, and nothing in that room had happened. She checked twice on the landing. Nothing in that room had happened, and she was afraid of it, and could not have written the sentence in any report on earth.

On the plane, in the dark over the sea, the parcel lay cold on her knees like a paving stone, and she did not open it, and the thing inside it kept its own hours.

She landed on the third. Her mother took the good scarf out of her bag at the minibus and said, "Well?", and accepted the parcel for an answer, being a woman who had waited out larger ones.

At the mouth the season had thinned to its bones: the queue gone with the school holidays, Recep's list laminated for next year, the fence standing in its own silence with the mulberries going early. The schoolteacher's mat was where the order allowed it, at the mouth, and the schoolteacher was on it. The gate boy came up with the key on its bootlace. And inside the fence, along the second rail, a grey figure went at the pace of a man with no appointment he intended to share — the Ministry walked its fences in September; the fence was the Ministry's — and where one of the queue's lamp-jars had gone down in the wind at the second mulberry the figure set the thing upright in passing, without breaking stride, and went on toward the thorn. She gave it no further thought. She had the oil to carry, and the round to keep, and tomorrow was the fourth of September.

She gave her name at the mouth, aloud, to nobody, first, and went down past the second door with the parcel's string biting her fingers, past the Ninth Room's note finding her sternum like a doorman who knows the step, down to the nine niches and the black above them. The oil went to the first knuckle of her own thumb and no further. The lamps were hers on trial, until they were not, and later, on the table above, the parcel would lie under the oilcloth notebook while the hill kept its evening. First frost by the week's end. The women had said: before first frost, kızım. The wall was long, and she had the rest of it to learn, and one night to decide in, and the dark between the two was only the road the errand travels.


# XIV. The Fourth of September

The fourth of September came up the hill clear and dry, two days ahead of the frost, and the village kept it the way it had kept it for twenty-seven years, which was by not keeping it: bread as usual, the argument at the co-op as usual, and at every gate half a second's hesitation that nobody named. Her mother said it to the bread oven, not to her: "He would be forty." Then she shut the door of the sentence, and they ate, and the day went on being a Friday, which the day managed.

She wrote the letter in the morning, at the dig-house table, in the hand she kept registers in, and said nothing of it, per the manner. It began with his name. It ended with the date. What was between was hers, and is.

Priska came up on the minibus at noon, early of her own letter by two days, because Nilay had sent her one line — *the founder's manner wants a witness. The fourth, before dark* — and she had read it, and packed the wintering, and come, and asked nothing. At the table Nilay gave her the whole of the protocol and none of the reason, and Priska received both portions without comment, warming the Registrar's wax, gone dark red with age, over the stove.

"What do I have to believe?" she asked, when the wax was ready.

"Nothing. That's why it's you."

"Then it will hold," Priska said, and pressed her initials into the seal, which is the whole of what a witness owns. Nilay took off her own watch, the one every season of her life had lived on, and she set its hands to twenty past nine and stopped it there — an hour she chose herself and did not explain — and Priska wrote the hour in her book. "A witness's whole duty is the hour," she said, and clipped her spare meter to Nilay's belt, because she had made everyone promise, and the keeper was no exception. "The rest is the keeper's."

Before dark she went down. The gate boy let the keeper pass. She gave her name at the mouth, aloud, to nobody, first. She kept the round first, all nine, oil to the knuckle of her own thumb; the trial was the keeping, and the keeping came first. Then, at the shelf cut at the height of a heart, its back sooted black and shining, the stone to its left worn smooth to the height of hands, she laid the tin box — letter, stopped watch, wax down — and knocked twice with the flat of her hand, softly, and stood the two breaths entire, and past the breaths said nothing, there being nothing in the manner that wanted words. The vigil was not announced. It was announced by being kept. She sat where the women sat, back to the wall, motionless, in the still air of a still night — the kind the women called generous and a forecast sheet would have called pooled — and the dark was present the way a full room is present.

You wake where you sat down. That is the first fact, and it arrives before any room does. Under you is a mat that was not yours — close-woven, dark, repeating nowhere — and the wall your shoulder leans on gives back a polish your hill has never owned. The air is warm and moves against your face mild and slow, the air of a room that has just had people in it, and it smells of rain on dust, and of nothing else at all. The lamp beside you is not your lamp: a bowl on a tall stem, straight-flamed, burning something that is not your oil. Your cuff smells of your own. You did not lie down. You are sitting as you sat, hands open on your knees, keeping the vigil, and the vigil has kept you.

At your knee, tin, cold: the box. In it, a watch still stopped at the hour a witness wrote down, and the letter. The wax is whole. You break it and it comes away in one piece, like a lid. Inside the letter are two sheets. The first sheet is yours, sealed yesterday before a person who believes nothing, which is why it holds. The second is in your hand — your hand, down to the loop of the y — in your phrasing, and at the head of it, the date: the fifth — today, and when the wax went on, tomorrow. Under the date, the morning you are currently having. *I woke where I sat down. The wax came away in one piece, like a lid. There is oil in my cuff. The lamp is not my lamp. Later today I will write this, word for word, because I will not be able not to.* And at the foot of the page, one line more, which you have not lived yet: *and I did not cry until the ribbon.*

You are reading a letter you have not written. You will write it today. You know it the way you know a round is finished — not by the count, which never closes, but by the house, which quiets.

Then she was herself again, forty-five, with two lamps' oil on her cuffs, and the morning proceeded. At the limit of the lamplight a passage went off, polished, patient; neither she nor the morning looked at it.

The round came with the oil. Steps at the sill, at the pace of an office, and a man came into the light carrying a tin can that smelled of the press and of some older oil than this year's, and he was in his forties, and he had their mother's jaw, the one that ruled one room, and he did not look at her, because the round comes first. He topped the lamp to the first knuckle of his thumb and no further. He pinched the wick with his nails, once, and it stood true. Then he took something from the fold of his wrap and laid it on the mat at the good distance — farther than your wanting, nearer than hers — and sat back on his heels.

A strip of red ribbon. Bazaar red gone the color of brick dust, folded on its old creases, faded the way twenty-seven years fade a thing, or longer. She cried then, as the sheet had said, and not before.

"You filled the lamp badly, that night," he said, to the wick. "Past the knuckle. The greedy or the frightened."

She said, "Emre," once — she had said it once into another dark, and the humming had not changed — and he said the name of the room first — three syllables, the middle one long; she had heard it twice before, once in the dark without knowing it, and once, knowing it, in a polished room, from a woman who measured doors, or had heard its grandmother, or its grandchild — because the manners are the manners, and past the name, nothing, and then he broke the office for the first time in twenty-seven of her years:

"Nilay."

He set out water for her, in a small jar, cool, and she drank, and it was the best water she had ever drunk, being carried water, and neither of them said so, and the argument she had owed Priska since August arrived with it, whole — comfort, or knowledge — and she let it stay unfinished, and drank. He told it plainly, in the office's grammar, with the boy surfacing in it. He had gone after the kid; it would not come in with the evening flock; he followed it past the thorn, and it went up the hill and he went down. He had his slippers on. He meant to be ten minutes. He went in far enough to be brave, and then one door more; the land cleared its throat, the way it did all that year, and a stone he had not marked came home in its socket, and the brave was finished.

"The first night a man sat with me," he said. "Grey. At the good distance. He had a light that didn't flicker, and he wrote something down by it, and asked me nothing, and in the morning, while I slept, he went up and took the light with him. I thought he was a keeper." He turned the can in his hands. "Keepers leave you the lamp." She did not say the name. It was in a margin in Valletta, with the date.

"Somebody came with a lamp on the third day," he said. "Or the third year. I had it settled for a while, and then I stopped settling things." He looked at his feet, in woven things, boot-shaped, none of them his. "I have never once had shoes, in all of it. The house never minded."

"I went up the first winter," he said. "The mouth opened on a morning I couldn't read. Strangers' smoke. A dog I didn't know." He weighed the oil can in his hands. "I came back down, and that was the last time I asked the year. A man ought to ask the year now and then. I say that now." The sentence had been Melek's, at the wicks, the June before. The office said what the office said, through whoever held the oil; there was no test that could tell them apart, and there had never been.

"There was a girl behind a stone once," he said, "in the storm month. Crying with her whole body, the way the tired pay down a debt they have carried too long. I sat where the wall lets a body sit and hummed the way the keepers hummed for me — the tune everyone's mother used, the one nobody owns, with nothing in it to follow but the going on."

"It was me."

"I know it now."

"You said my name once," he said. "Mine. Past the name is how the shamed were treated once, and you had done nothing, and had been done-to enough. So I gave you the true answer instead, the only one I had. The name of the room, in the place where the words go." He considered the flame. "I have been sorry for it for—" and stopped, the number being a country he had emigrated from.

"Mother is alive," she said. "She keeps one room."

"Small and exact," he said, and it was not a question, and they let it be.

She told him about the lamps passing to her, and about the keeper who had died in June, and about the woman who had gone down at the end of winter for forty years with four sentences and a jar of water. He listened the way stone hears rain, which is wholly, and in no hurry.

"We know her," he said. "Every keeper keeps that niche some winters. I kept it nine. We don't ask what's new, and we don't ask the year — asking is a lamp you hold up on somebody, and the dark down here has enough light put to it. The answer is the name of the room. Once. Past the name, nothing." He considered the flame. "The water was the best water in the year. Being carried."

"She never settled whether it was him."

"I believe it was somebody keeping," he said, low, to the lamps. "Taking her for somebody of their own. That is what I believe; I believed it before I had a right to." And Nilay carried that the rest of the way like a coal in both hands.

Near the end — the lamp low, the water drunk, the ribbon in her lap — she asked the question with the whole year folded in it. "What is this place, Emre."

He looked at the flame while he answered, the way the office answers.

"You've had it backwards. It isn't a door to anywhere. It's a road that's long instead of far. A place that's kept long enough stops being in only one of its times — it's in all of them, the way a road is in all its miles. Keeping is the thread. The rooms come when they're kept. I never left, Nilay. I've been here since the summer I fell. It's been longer for me. The ones below — I think they keep too. From where, I couldn't tell you. Farther than I've been, and not the way you're going. We never asked each other; it isn't asked. I don't think any of us ever met anyone but each other. And you'll go farther back than any of us. The wall already says so."

"And the law of it," she said, when she could. "Why kept places. Why ten thousand years should open a road—"

"I don't know the law," he said. "I know my road."

The words for it came to her later — on the stair, or at the mouth, or in October; she never fixed where they landed — out of a bad film and a lecture hall, two small clumsy words, *time travel*, and she turned them over once, the way you turn over a borrowed tool, and gave them back.

Some of it re-keyed itself without her consent, on the climb and for weeks after: the counts, the plans, the watches — true things, partial things; she let them go as true things go. And Priska's air had been true too, and measured — four percent at the fourth door, fifteen events in twenty-two nights, two in twenty-six — and whether it had brought the small mercies or had only been in the room when they came, none of her figures said. And some of it the road could have carried, and she did not make it carry them: a voice under a knocking that two listeners had disagreed was a voice; water that had held one more than the people; a coin paid for a lamp and gone from its foam. She laid those flat on the palm and left them where they lay. The drawer kept them, beside a tune that paused where the words would be. The drawer was hers now.

He took her up the way keepers go, past doors she knew by their counts and not by their names, to the lower wall where the minutes lived: the lines, one beside another, thousands, and above the first marks, in red gone brown, the print of a right hand. His own newest cuts stood pale at the low end — this year's, whichever year his year was. The last of his cuts had been cut over an erasure, shallow under deep, as if a hand had kept a night before it was sure; she did not ask, and he did not offer. The bowl sat covered against the dust, and he uncovered it: the ground red, and charcoal, and the mixing of the two, the wall's own ink, older than any writing on earth to sign a thing with. She wet her right hand in the bowl and set her palm on the stone above the first marks, over the old print, which had always been hers, and which she would press, in her own farthest keeping, before there was a Wall to press it on. The two prints sat together at the head of the counting, a thing and its shadow, and neither of them was first.

"The room chose you," he said. "Don't argue with it. It has more time than you."

At the head of the going-up he stopped at the good distance and did not follow, and of course he did not follow. "I never do," he said, and it sounded like quotation, because it was. "Fill to the knuckle. You know the rest." And then, in the grammar the wall used: "To the one who keeps the lamps after me." She went up into the grey.

She came out at the mouth at first light on the fifth of September, twenty-seven years and a day, and Priska was at the watcher's stone with the thermos, at the hour she had chosen, which was her way of attending. The times went into the book: down before dark, up at first light, one night, honestly accounted.

"The wax?"

"Broken. By me, below."

"Then what happened below is yours," Priska said, and poured, and that was the whole of the interview, and it was the correct whole. At the gate she read the strip on her spare meter, a healthy line, 0.6 from end to end, and looked once at the date at the head of the second sheet, standing written over a morning the two of them were standing in, and read her own initials in the seal it had come out of, and was silent with her entire science for a while.

"It kept, then," she said at last.

"It kept."

"Then the manner works," said Priska, "whichever way it works," and went down to see about the duct, because the fan's bearing would not survive the frost alone, and neither, that morning, would anyone else's certainties.

Later that day, at the table, Nilay wrote the letter — word for word, dated the fifth, the hand not once hesitating where she would have sworn it must — because she could not not. All three sheets went back into the tin box: hers of the fourth, and two of the fifth that differed in nothing she could find but their creases. The box went into the drawer, with the two plans and the crossed-out count and the stick — she had taken it out once, the night before, and put it back unplayed, having learned from the boy what a tiebreaker costs, and chosen the sealed hour instead — whose label said *viewed: no* and would go on saying it, one problem laid on another, which was how that drawer kept things.

At the bread queue that evening Nilay stood beside Halime, where the women had put her by putting her nowhere, and gave her own name first, as at the mouth; nothing else was said, and the queue saw it.

Going down for the evening round she passed the schoolteacher's mat. Seher did not open her eyes. "Was it kept, Hanım?"

"It was kept."

Seher nodded, at the inside of her own eyelids, and settled, and the mat kept her, and that was the whole of the colloquy.

The ribbon had lain all afternoon on the table by the bread oven, where Nilay had set it down. Her mother found it there and asked nothing. She dried her hands on her apron, though they were dry, and took it up, and folded it on its old creases, which her fingers found before her eyes did; and when it was folded her thumb went on smoothing the top fold, long after there was nothing left to smooth. Then she put it in her apron pocket and kept her hand there. At dusk she stood at the door to the yard, the hand still in the pocket, and watched her daughter go out with the oil can to the evening round, the way she had once watched a boy go out in house slippers, and did not call anything after her.

The parcel's brown paper, unfolded flat at last, gave up one thing more: at the bottom, under the volume, an envelope in the slope, which she had not opened because it had not asked to be. Two ordinary documents. The first was a certificate of determination: *Sample — pigment, charcoal-bound, hand-mark, lower wall (phial, A.V.L., 1924). Age: 10,720 ± 90 years before present.* The laboratory's date at the foot of it was July of the year before: the week she had sat on that floor drinking tea that went cold by itself, the answer to the oldest open question in her discipline had been in the building, dated and filed. She laid the two Julys side by side, and this time she had the word, and did not write it either.

The second was a copy of the inquest memo of January, and pinned to it a chit in a technician's idle hand: *Funny — the old print (Trust photograph, item 51, 1924, submitted by the sponsoring institution for elimination, as a courtesy to the inquest) matched someone in the elimination file. Friction ridges don't lie. 31 points, right hand.* The elimination file had been taken at the co-op table, between the ledger weights, in the ordinary way, on the coldest week of the winter. The card it named was hers. Her file records one sibling: a brother, missing.

Ten thousand seven hundred and twenty years, give or take ninety, before the present: her hand, at the head of the counting, older than the counting. It was the one measurement taken all year that no fork could carry, and she held it the way you hold a lit thing: cupped, and low.

She sent the certificate's copy to Valletta with two sentences: *The print is mine. The Trust will know what to do with nothing.* She wrote a third, with two dates in it, the fourth and the fifth of September 1999, and struck it through, because accusations want a court, and she had a hill. The keepership sheet went in the same post, its date filled in; and with the lamps had come the List, and with the List its quiet line — kept nightly, quiet since 1961 — and the wage-line that stopped mid-column; whatever keeping weighed, and whatever its ceasing had cost, was hers now to carry up the hill with the oil. The reply came by return post, in the slope: *Received. Filed. The Trust's line for the year stands as cut: Nothing to record. — A. Halden, Registrar.* And under the evenness of the slope she thought she could see the last image the man would give anybody: the pen set down at rest, on a desk that kept nothing it had not read, one floor above a pharmacy, beside a cloth that had kept a ring from happening for thirty years, and a margin in a green volume, *4–5.ix*, that had kept a boy off every record for twenty-seven.

On the fourteenth of September, at first light, she opened Book Zero at the first blank leaf behind the bound-in annual lines, scored it once with the little knife from Melek's oilcloth, one line, low, the way the wall is cut, and under the line wrote:

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me: the fourteenth of September. Emre Arat kept here, from the fourth of September 1999. The keeper wrote this before she lived it.*

No name at the foot, after the formula; there never is. The seat belongs to whoever is sitting in it, and someone always is. She had written one true line where he would have written none, and a name in it, in a book that did not record the recorders, and closed the volume, and walked up through the mulberries with the oil can for the evening's round.

At the mouth of Kırk Oda the air shaft breathed. The breath went out for a long while, mild, carrying stone and cold water and something like the inside of a pocket; then a pause long enough to be mistaken for stillness; then in again, the same length, the same patience — a tide with no sea. Anyone with a watch and an hour to waste could time it, and anyone who timed it came away with the same figure: eighteen minutes, and eighteen again, obedient to nothing anyone had ever identified, keeping its own time the way the sea keeps the moon's.

In October the Ministry's report wanted conclusions, and she was on the Şanlıurfa side, alone. The hill her enemies would not let her finish arguing about lay under the heat haze with its backfill under it, generation laid on generation, a house closed the way you close a house you mean to go on being a house. She stood on it a while. Then she took her water bottle and wet one exposed stone, the way she had wetted a thousand carved surfaces for the light a camera needs — and stopped, halfway through, her hand still open, because she could not tell the method from the rite; and went on wetting it anyway, and did not decide.

That winter she rendered the wall's two new lines, and then the old ones, notebook after notebook, the way she had rendered them for six years without reading them — half transcription, half what the hand did while the eye counted; she had known that for six years and had never before had to know what it meant. The new lines said what the wall always says, in the small grammar of days and diggings: company came up the sealed road; the water was carried. Above the first marks there was one print, as there had always been; she held her palm a finger's width off it and could not say which of the two it was. And at the end of the rendering she found what had been standing at the start of her first notebook all along: a keeper, teaching a child to tend a lamp — oil to the knuckle, wick pinched true, the dark taken a spoonful at a time. The wall began where it ended. She could not tell which end she had begun from, and did not try. Nor could she say how much of what she read was cut and how much arrived — things true in that country came by more than one road, and the island's round had come by one of them, carried in a hand that had visited the island once; she filed the not-knowing with the rest, and kept rendering.

The lamps were kept.

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me:*

*I taught the child today, and I will set it down the way I said it, so that it stays said.*

*First, the lamp is not a light. A light you strike for yourself, and it is finished when you are. A lamp is an errand. Someone set it for you, and you will set it for someone, and the dark between the two of you is only the road the errand travels.*

*Watch my hands, I told her, and not the flame. The flame will lie to you about being difficult. The hands do not know how.*

*The oil goes to the first knuckle of the thumb and no further. Oil past the knuckle is for the greedy or the frightened, and the lamp can tell the two apart, though I never managed it. The wick is trimmed with the nails, pinched, never cut. What is cut bleeds. What is pinched remembers.*

*The flame you carry cupped and low, near the body, the way you carry a thing you are only holding for someone: as if it were yours. You offer it to the wick the way bread is offered, not pushed. Hold it near, and wait, and the wick comes to it. Then the lamp took the dark a spoonful at a time. That is the true speed of every morning there has ever been. The child wanted it faster. They all want it faster. Faster is how you learn the smell of your own eyebrows.*

*Doors go down and rooms go along, and the count of one is never traded for the count of the other; keep them like two names you must not call by the wrong one in the house of a proud family. The round is this: the near rooms first, then the far, then the deep, then the near again, so that the last thing in your eyes on the climb is something small and close. You count as you go. Counting is not arithmetic. Counting is noticing, with numbers, so the rooms know they have been seen. When the round is finished you cut the day's mark, one line, low, beside all the other lines, and you do not go looking for yours among them. The wall is long. Looking for yourself in something long is how people get lost.*

*She asked me who lit the first lamp. The question is on wrong feet, I told her. Nobody lights a first lamp. You come down and they are lit, and someone is owed a measure of oil and a thank-you, and that is every morning there has ever been. She frowned. The frown is the correct face for a true answer.*

*If company comes, you say the name of the room, and then nothing. To speak past the name is how the shamed were treated once, and we are not here for shaming. If it stays, it stays. If it goes, it goes. You are not the reason for either. You are the lamp.*

*Sleep above the fourth door when rain has been on the land. That one is not a mystery; it is weather. Water pushes the old air up, and the old air does not love you. The rules that keep the body are rules too, and first. A keeper who is not kept keeps nothing.*

*I am tired in a way sleep does not find. The room has known it longer than I have. When your turn comes, the room will know that too before you do, and will be kind about it in its way, which is stone's way, which is slow.*

*At the third lamp her hands shook, and at the fourth they did not, and at the ninth she yawned, which is the true ordination. If you want the whole of teaching in one sentence: fear spends itself where it is allowed to.*

*The oil is measured. The wicks are pinched. Before, it is dark the way it always was, and after, it is dark the same way, and between the two there will have been light for as long as my knuckles hold out, and after that for as long as yours.*

*To the one who keeps the lamps after me: begin.*
