## 2. Verdict: is it finished?

**Yes, as a story. It needs one short trimming pass, not another revision.**

The structure closes: the opening letter and the closing letter are the same letter, and the last chapter shows you why ("The wall began where it ended", l. 1417). Every one of your rules is kept, and most of them are kept by several things at once, which is what makes an ending feel inevitable rather than arranged. The reunion does the job you asked for. The villain is this book's own creation and works the way you wanted.

What remains is small and precise: four sentences in chapters XII and XIV that tell the reader what an image has just shown them (Finding S1), plus eight quibbles that could go either way. I have not invented faults to fill a list.

I read a manuscript, not a finished book; judgements about pace and fatigue are mine from the page and are marked as such in section 8.

## 3. Your rules, one by one

**Time travel is the very last reveal, confirmed at the end, and ties the mysteries together.** Kept. Nothing states it before chapter XIV. The nearest earlier approaches are guesses in the keepers' letters ("far down, as a well is far ... far aside, as the next house is far ... far along", ch. The plain words are then said once and handed back: "two small clumsy words, *time travel*, and she turned them over once, the way you turn over a borrowed tool, and gave them back" (l. 1363). The confirmation is physical and witnessed: a letter dated tomorrow inside a seal a disbeliever made yesterday (ll. 1295, 1379), and a hand-print dated "10,720 ± 90 years before present" that matches her own card (ll. 1399–1403). ## 4. Notes by sequence

### Sequence A, chapters I–II: arrival, and the storm night of 1999

**The opening letter is the physics of the ending (protect).** "Nobody lights a first lamp. You come down and they are lit, and someone is owed a measure of oil and a thank-you" (l. 17). At the time it reads as a keeper's wisdom. At the end it is the literal rule of what happens: Nilay reads a letter she has not written and will write that day (l. 1295), and her hand is on the wall "older than the counting" (l. 1403). A loop with no first cause is the hardest kind of time-travel idea to make feel earned; here it is earned because the first page already said the rule as a way of life. Held by three jobs at once (voice of the house, rule for the reader, and the mechanism of the ending). Do not touch it.

**The flaw is shown small before it matters (protect).** Nilay's habit is to file what she should ask. "She noticed the *in advance*, and liked the letter's courtesy, and forgot it" (l. 37); "filed it with the ninety harmless things" (l. 57); and the one question she has never put to Halime "has sat in Nilay for twenty-six years like a stone in a fruit" (l. 146). This is the flaw the whole book turns on, and it pays off in three places: she asks Halden to his face (l. 1252), she asks Emre to come home (l. 1351), and she stands beside Halime at the bread queue and gives her own name first (l. 1389). Chapter I flags each filed item with a small dismissal; that is a tic, but it is her tic, and line 372 cashes it: "nothing in it had ever once been harmless."

**The 1999 night is the engine (protect).** The ribbon tied on the door iron and gone in the morning (l. 144); the tune that "paused where the words would be" (l. 138); "She said, 'Emre,' once. The humming did not change and did not stop" (ll. 140–142). Every one of these is collected in chapter XIV: the ribbon comes back "faded the way twenty-seven years fade a thing, or longer" (l. 1303); the pause was the name of the room "in the place where the words go" (l. 1327). And Melek's remark years later, "You filled it badly. Past the knuckle" (l. 154), is the same sentence Emre says at the reunion (l. 1305), which quietly tells you Melek knew more than she said. This is the book's best plant-and-payoff run.

### Sequence B, chapters III–V: the measurers

**Márton's three watches (works).** Two reasons are given for them, one true and one truer: "Blood heat ... This was true, and it was not the reason" (l. 189). The 1997 story is told "in the voice he used for instrument logs" (l. 245) and ends on the best sentence about evidence in the book (l. 251). The refrain "an average is where anomalies go to be forgiven" (l. 217) is later borrowed by Priska when she refuses to average the shaft (l. 971), which is how the book shows one scientist teaching another without a scene of teaching.

**Malta (works).** Halden is introduced through what he does not do: "drank none himself" (l. 308); "He opened the door at the moment knocking became necessary, so that she never quite knocked" (l. 308). The margin "*4–5.ix. Kept the hours. A.H.*" (l. 314) and the two leaves of September 1999 (l. 328) are the villain's crime set down in plain sight in chapter IV, unreadable until chapter XIV. The phial of 1924 pigment (l. 330) is the ending's evidence sitting on a shelf ten chapters early. The child's shoe (l. 334) stays open and should.

**Priska's chapter (works).** The cough at 0.5 is the book's finest single beat about belief: the theory "took all four forks and survived. What it did not survive was the half-second in which she had not wanted it to survive" (l. 437), and the log entry is "the cleanest reading of her career and the only dishonest page in it" (l. 439). Seher's schoolhouse scene (l. 459) puts the theme in two palms and refuses to choose; that scene is the model for how the rest of the book argues.

### Sequence C, chapters VI–VIII: the boy, the breach, the reports

**Yusuf (works).** The "server lag" theory is the parallel-reality hint in the one voice that could carry it without solemnity (l. 510). The tea drunk and not drunk (l. 516) is the cleanest dissociation in the book because nobody explains it; his field-book entry does the whole job: "*tea: drank it (me) / didn't (cup). different build?*". The letter thanking him for restraint before he has shown it (ll. 524–526) is the villain's method shown small before the closure memo shows it big.

**The breach (works, and is the book's set piece).** Everything in the room is given as pressure and not as finding: "That was not a finding; it was a pressure" (l. 593). Márton's handshake "offered ... to weather" (l. 595); the woman measuring the new door with her forearm "twice, unhurried, without apology" (l. 599), which Yusuf later sees again at the mouth (l. 1122); the elder cupping the headlamp "as a man cups a match in wind" (l. 597), which is the keeper's letter of chapter VI seen from the other side ("the sun in his hand"). The flour with two grains of the first wheat (l. 611) is the future-humans hint at its most deniable, because flour "travels in pockets" (l. 750).

**Four Reports (works as a document chapter).** The coin paid for a lamp (l. 766) and "That's what shops are" (l. 768) is the one place the far keepers' economy is stated, by the one person who would say it. Priska's line "Memory is not an instrument" (l. 724) is the chapter's spine. Leyla Hanım's silence is the one loose thread here (Q3).

### Sequence D, chapters IX–X: the solstice and the death

**Márton's last night (works).** The build is exact: two nights unworn giving "Same sign. Same figure. Twice." (l. 827); the parcel that acquits 1997 (l. 837); the whiteboard's "what is the fuel?" (l. 833); Halden's visit and his one sentence, "The sites ask little but attendance, Professor. You have attended" (l. 847); and the last paragraph, watches on the wrong wrist, "he had wanted, all his life, a longer baseline" (l. 851). The physics thread and the villain thread meet here: the "fuel" Marques demanded is attendance, and the Trust collects it. The book never says this; it lets 833 and 847 sit three pages apart. Protect that distance.

**Priska's autopsy (works).** "a chemist whose instrument had just been asked to acquit a hill" (l. 880). The wrist noticed (l. 888); two watches stopped and one "agreed with the sky" (l. 888); "the village remembered weather" (l. 898). Her scene with Halden (ll. 906–912) is the best villain scene because he takes nothing from her but a fact she did not know she gave. Her line to Nilay, "There was a fix, and I had it ... Now there is nothing to fix. There is only a file", answered by "I know. I have one from 1999" (l. 924), is the two women's whole relationship in four sentences.

### Sequence E, chapters XI–XIII: three successions

**Melek's confession and death (works).** "Forty years, and I never settled, and now I am out of winters, and the not-settling is the steadiest thing I own" (l. 1005). Her last vigil, coming up "without the lamp ... 'A lamp is not a debt, kızım'" (l. 979), is the keeper's letter of chapter XI done in the flesh. The singing-room voice returning "and the lamps are kept" (l. 1023) and Nilay's note, "I don't know what I heard. **Yusuf's chapter (works, with one trim).** "*recovered: 14 sec. watched: 0.* The zero was the proudest number he had" (l. 1092) and Nilay writing "*Viewed: no*" as "a property of the object, the way one writes dimensions" (l. 1108) are the book's answer to its own question about showing. The figure at the lip that measures the threshold with a forearm (l. 1122) and the old print under the overhang (l. 1136) are the far keepers' last appearance and are kept properly deniable ("Sandal people, all summer. The tour has a story for them", l. 1134). The last paragraph has one sentence too many (S1, instance d).

**Valletta, second visit (works).** Book Zero's spine, "*Nothing to Record*", shelf-mark "*0*" (l. 1191); the photograph "A.H., Dowth, Michaelmas 1949" and "The Register records the sites, Dr. Arat. It does not record the recorders" (ll. 1205–1213); the founder's unsent letter, "pay the keepers" (l. 1223); the protocol with its 1951 result "plus one item: [inked out]" (l. 1234). The narration's gloss on that line (l. 1250) is one I would keep: the menace is in the third sense of an idiom, and an ordinary reader needs the nudge. The grey figure on the fence righting the lamp-jar (l. 1272) is the best-hidden line in the book.

### Sequence F, chapter XIV: the sealed hour

**The choice that matters is on the page (works).** Nilay's true choice is not whether to leave the chamber; Emre forecloses that before she can ask it ("The room chose you ... Don't argue with it. It has more time than you", l. 1369). Her true choice comes after, and both options are visible: publish the date that answers "the oldest open question in her discipline" (l. 41) and the fingerprint that proves her hand, or file them. She sends the certificate to Valletta with "The Trust will know what to do with nothing" (l. 1405), fills in the keepership date, and then does the one thing Halden never does: writes a name in his book, "*Emre Arat kept here, from the fourth of September 1999*" (l. 1409). Under the rule "say the name of the room, and then nothing", speaking past the name once, in writing, for the shamed boy, is the book's whole theme made into a single act. The cost is felt, not stated: at Göbekli Tepe she cannot finish wetting a stone (l. 1415).

**The reunion (works).** The boy surfacing in the man is handled by sentence, not by speech: "for a moment the grin was thirteen" (l. 1317); "the number being a country he had emigrated from" (l. 1327). His refusal to come up is a real choice between two goods (his mother's face, and sparing her a stranger in woven shoes), and it is what lets Nilay carry the ribbon instead of him. The mother's scene (l. 1397) does the impossible job wordlessly: "She dried her hands on her apron, though they were dry", and folded it "on its old creases, which her fingers found before her eyes did". One small stumble in the dialogue (Q1) and one thing I would hold more firmly (Q2).

**The witness (works, quietly).** The sealed-hour test is the one falsifiable test in the book, and it passes. What stops it becoming proof is custody, which Priska states as protocol before she has seen the result: "The wax?" / "Broken. By me, below." / "Then what happened below is yours" (ll. 1375–1379). That is the answer to Halden's "could you sign?": she has the only signable thing in the book in her hand and, by her own rule, cannot sign it. It is very quiet (Q7).

## 5. How it fits together

Put as a question about how to act, the book's problem is: *when you cannot know, what do you owe the ones who wait: an answer, or your attendance?* Every main character answers it differently, which is what a theme needs. Priska answers: explain, then fix (the masks), and ends attending above ground with a nightly calibration that "proved nothing about the night any more than a repeated office proves the thing it is about" (l. 936). Márton answers: measure, and attends below until it kills him. Yusuf answers: show, and ends keeping a lamp nobody can view. Seher answers: sit, and names the price, "It costs the question" (l. 1033). Melek answers: keep, and never settle. Kaya Bey answers with an explanation "that fitted ... everything that had happened and nothing that would happen next" (l. 902). Nilay answers last: ask the question at last, then keep, and write one name.

Halden is the theme's dark mirror, and the mirror is fair because the book gives you the exact difference. The keepers' rule is consent: "You do not get to choose your size. You only get to consent to it" (l. 397). Halden arranges other people's attendance without their consent: a thirteen-year-old left with no lamp; a man handed "peace" three days before he dies at the door; a keeper's hospital bed booked the day before she falls. Melek keeps her own vigil for forty years; Halden keeps everyone else's for them. That is the difference between belief as keeping and belief as being kept, and it is why he is frightening without ever raising his voice.

The world's rule expresses the theme rather than sitting beside it: "A place kept long enough is in all of its times at once" (l. 1345) means keeping *is* the fuel, which answers Marques's demand for the fuel bill (l. 819) and explains why "The sites ask little but attendance" (l. 847) is both a courtesy and a threat. The plot events that are also theme moves are many: the masks (l. 447), the post (l. 528), the coin for the lamp (l. 766), the unwatched fourteen seconds (l. 1092), the sealed hour, the keepership date, and the line in Book Zero. That is enough meeting points that the plot is never a puzzle with a theme bolted on.

Point of view holds: each chapter stays on one shoulder (Nilay, Márton, Priska, Yusuf, Priska, Nilay, Yusuf, Nilay), the keepers' letters give the other side, and the one switch into "you" is used once, at the one moment when Nilay is not sure whose morning she is in.

## 6. Findings

Each finding gives the chapter and passage, the problem, why it matters, and a concrete fix. **SUBSTANTIVE** means a careful reader would notice it and it weakens the book. **QUIBBLE** means it could go either way, or only readers could settle it.

### S1 — SUBSTANTIVE. One habit, four sentences: the narration names the meaning after the image has already carried it (chs. XII and XIV).

This is the habit your revision set out to remove, and it is mostly gone. Four sentences survive, all in the last pages, where they cost most, because the last pages are where the reader should be left holding images and not captions. I tested each by asking whether cutting it would leave anything unclear; in each case the meaning is already on the page in an image or a line of dialogue.

(a) **ch. XIV, l. 1311.** "and the argument she had owed Priska since August arrived with it, whole — comfort, or knowledge — and she let it stay unfinished, and drank." The drink already carries the answer ("it was the best water she had ever drunk, being carried water"). The dash-clause labels the theme at the climax. *Fix:* cut "— comfort, or knowledge —", or the whole clause from "and the argument" to "unfinished,", leaving "and drank."

(b) **ch. XIV, l. 1405.** The portrait of Halden's pen at rest is good and should stay; the last clause is a caption: "and a margin in a green volume, *4–5.ix*, that had kept a boy off every record for twenty-seven." Nilay has already said this to his face (l. 1252) and Emre has confirmed it (l. 1313). *Fix:* end the sentence at "a margin in a green volume, *4–5.ix*."

(c) **ch. XIV, l. 1411.** "She had written one true line where he would have written none, and a name in it, in a book that did not record the recorders, and closed the volume, and walked up through the mulberries..." The entry at l. 1409 and Halden's own "It does not record the recorders" (l. 1213) already say all of this. *Fix:* "No name at the foot, after the formula; there never is. The seat belongs to whoever is sitting in it, and someone always is. She closed the volume and walked up through the mulberries with the oil can for the evening's round."

(d) **ch. XII, l. 1144.** Two routes to one job in the same paragraph: "being nobody's content" and, as the closing words, "the first thing he had ever kept that could not be viewed." Each is removable alone; together they say the same thing twice, and the second one explains. *Fix:* end the paragraph on "and went up to bed with the light burning below him in the house." Keep "being nobody's content" earlier in the paragraph.

*Why it matters:* these are the sentences a reader remembers last, and each one hands back a meaning the reader had just earned for themselves. Cutting all four takes ten minutes and changes nothing else.

### Q1 — QUIBBLE. A boy's question is left hanging in a way that reads like a dropped line (ch. XIV, ll. 1317–1319).

"'Mother's going to kill me about the slippers. Did the kid ever come in?' / 'I went up the first winter,' he said." Two consecutive speeches by Emre with an unanswered question between them; a careful reader wonders whether Nilay's reply fell out. It could be deliberate (the mountain question is also left unanswered), but that one is framed as unanswerable and this one is not. *Fix:* one line from Nilay that fits the book's counting theme, e.g. "'I don't know,' she said. 'Nobody counted the flock that week.'" Or keep the silence and mark it: "She did not know. Nobody had counted the flock that week."

### Q2 — QUIBBLE. The mother's recognition of the ribbon rests on an inference the page does not quite make (ch. XIV, l. 1397; ch. II, ll. 122–148).

The ribbon scene is the emotional payoff of "consequence", and it works on the body: "She dried her hands on her apron, though they were dry." But the red ribbon was Nilay's system, bought and tied during the fortnight when "Her mother, that night, was four floors up in Kayseri with wires on her chest" (l. 148). The page never shows the mother seeing the ribbons, so a careful reader may ask what exactly she recognises. *Fix:* one clause in chapter II, e.g. after l. 130: "...the strips were still on the gateposts, gone the colour of brick dust, when her mother came home from Kayseri in the spring." That makes the mother's fingers finding "its old creases" beyond question.

### Q3 — QUIBBLE. A tease with nothing behind it: Leyla Hanım's silence (ch. VIII, l. 740).

"one of the two who saw him at the fountain at last light, and she had spent her whole life since not saying his name. The village had three explanations for that, and she had furnished none of them." Nothing later bears on any of the three; Emre's own account of that evening (l. 1311) says nothing about the fountain. By the method's own test, a working open question is one where each reading is held by something on the page; here none is, so it reads as a promise not kept. It is small, and it does do a job (another villager who never asked, mirroring Nilay). *Fix:* either cut the sentence about three explanations, so the silence is texture and not a tease, or give one reading a foothold without settling it, e.g. Emre at l. 1311: "Leyla was at the fountain. She called after me. I didn't turn."

### Q4 — QUIBBLE. The keepers' letters share refrains, and two of them end on nearly the same sentence (ch. VIII, l. 656; ch. X, l. 876).

"The days are ours. The marks are the house's. It has always been a fair division." and "The days are ours. The marks are the house's. The men were never either. It has always been a fair division." Add "it has always been enough" (five times), "not for saying" (three), "the wall is long" throughout. The repetition is a liturgy and is clearly meant: chapter VIII's letter says so, "the setting down is the same in every hand" (l. 541). Every letter also has a distinct job (I checked all fourteen: rules; the storm girl; the rope; the island; the four sentences; the sun in the hand; the far lamplighters; the diggers; the room's choice; the buried measurer; the lamp set down; belief ripening; the last mark; the frame). So this is an audience question: some readers will find the voice hypnotic and some will skim it by chapter IX. *Fix, if you want one:* vary the closing formula of the chapter X letter, since it is the one that repeats chapter VIII's almost word for word; leave the rest.

### Q5 — QUIBBLE. The scientists sometimes speak in the keepers' cadence (ch. V, l. 435; ch. III, l. 217).

"a time series is the only apology a scientist can make to an event" (Priska); "an average is where anomalies go to be forgiven" (Márton). These are fine lines, but the theme's friction comes partly from two grammars, the measurers' and the keepers', and when the measurers' narration turns aphoristic the two grammars blur. The book knows this and makes it a point (Priska's calibration as "a repeated office", l. 936), so it may be exactly what you want. Only an audience can settle it. *Fix, if any:* none I would insist on; if anything, keep the epigrams in the scientists' dialogue and let their narration stay flatter.

### Q6 — QUIBBLE. A general maxim explains what the women's refusal has just shown (ch. XI, l. 1013).

"No document had handed Nilay anything in that country, and this was not a document. The office had come to her by refusal, as offices moved there: what is given can be resented, and what is refused must be taken up, and the taking up is the training." The scene before it (Sungur's "The round is not taught in mourning ... She left you her hand", l. 1011) already shows the office passing by refusal. *Fix:* cut from "The office had come to her" to the end of the paragraph, or move the thought into Sungur's mouth at the door, where it would be character and not commentary.

### Q7 — QUIBBLE. Priska's declined proof is so quiet a reader may not see that she declined anything (ch. XIV, ll. 1375–1385).

Halden asked her "What, today, on your instrument, in your hand, could you sign?" (l. 912). At the gate she holds a sheet dated tomorrow inside a seal she initialled yesterday, and by her own custody rule ("Then what happened below is yours") she cannot sign it. That is a real answer to his question and a real choice, but "Then what happened below is yours" reads, on a first pass, as kindness rather than protocol. *Fix, if you want it visible:* one clause, in her voice, that names custody without explaining, e.g. after l. 1379: "The witness signs the wax, not what comes out of it." If you would rather it stay buried for the second reading, leave it; I lean that way.

### Q8 — QUIBBLE. The dose of "future" in one line (ch. XIV, l. 1345).

"The ones below keep too, from farther than I've been, and not the way you're going." Because Nilay is told she will "go farther back", "not the way you're going" points forward. It is Emre's belief, said in his grammar, so your rule (never provable) still holds, and your concept does want the reader to sense a far future. But it is the one place the three guesses of chapter VII (down, aside, along) collapse toward one. *Fix, if you want the triad kept open to the end:* "from farther than I've been, and by a road I haven't walked." If you want the reader nudged, leave it.

## 7. What to protect

These are held by several jobs at once; a later small change could quietly break them.

- "Nobody lights a first lamp" (l. 17) and the loop it licenses at l. 1297. Do not add an origin.
- The pause in the tune (l. 138) and its answer (l. 1327). Do not let anyone name the tune's words.
- Melek's "You filled it badly. Past the knuckle" (l. 154) and Emre's same sentence (l. 1305).
- The three pages between "what is the fuel?" (l. 833) and "You have attended" (l. 847). Do not bring them closer.
- The wrist (ll. 189, 851, 888), the elevation (ll. 213, 898), and the shaft (ll. 71, 534, 1041, 1413). Nobody explains any of them; keep it so.
- The paragraph at l. 1365 that ties some mysteries and leaves three "on the palm". It is explanation, but it is the explanation your rule requires, and the three it leaves are what keep the dead-contact reading alive.
- "Then what happened below is yours" (l. 1379). It is the custody rule that keeps the sealed hour from becoming proof.
- The grey figure righting the lamp-jar (l. 1272). It is the only action Halden ever takes, and it tells you what he is.
- "Is the fire mountain awake?" (l. 603). Never answer it.

## 8. What I am unsure of

- **Whether an ordinary reader will see that the opening letter is Nilay's.** The page supports it ("you'll go farther back than any of us. The wall already says so", l. 1345; the hand-print at 10,720 years; "she found what had been standing at the start of her first notebook all along: a keeper, teaching a child", l. 1417), but the frame can also be read as simply "the tradition". Both readings are held and nothing settles between them, so this is a working open question and not a gap. I would not add a line.
- **Chapter VIII (Four Reports) is the chapter I felt most ready to skim**, between the breach and the solstice. That is a feeling from the page, not a finding: every part of it does a job (the statements, the coin, Leyla's vigil, the Trust's "tending is unelectable", l. 754). I report it only so you can ask another reader.
- **How much of the villain an owner who is not a writer will feel on one reading.** The four hinges are spread over nine chapters and assembled only at ll. 1238–1252. That is the design, and it is the Johan method (nothing to point at until it is too late), but it does mean the menace arrives late. I would not add more; I would only make sure the lines already there survive every future pass.
- **Whether the sameness of voice (Q4, Q5) is a strength or a fatigue.** The book's own answer is "at this depth a story and a memory are one water" (l. 1050). Readers will divide.
