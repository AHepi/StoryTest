## Ana, 34, nurse

### Panel report: Ana

*34, ward nurse, reads on night shifts. I read the whole thing across two breaks and a very quiet 3am, start to finish.*

### 1. Where I was gripped, and where I drifted

**Gripped:**

- **Ch. I, the first keeper's letter.** I was in from the first page. "What is cut bleeds. What is pinched remembers." "Faster is how you learn the smell of your own eyebrows." Then Nilay on the threshold with the warmth leaning on her "the way a cat commits itself," and the night-bus child. I know that stillness from sitting with patients.
- **Ch. II, the 1999 flashback.** This is the best stretch of the book. Emre in his house slippers, meaning to be gone ten minutes. The paper boats. Nilay at eighteen buying red ribbon and keeping the search list: "The first register she ever kept was the absence of her brother." The shut room, the humming, "Emre," once, and the humming not stopping. Then the mother on the cardiology corridor: "Which ninni, kızım? I sang the one everyone sings." And Halime, whom the bread queue made into the body the comfort needed. I didn't breathe much during this chapter.
- **Ch. III, Márton's 1997 story** and the line about never having loved "anything that could say no." And then noting his own happiness in the log "because happiness, at his age, in his field, was a reading too."
- **Ch. V, Priska.** For me this was the strongest chapter. Havva surfacing: "She was cross with me for the hour." Priska's father's cough at 05:40, and "the cleanest reading of her career and the only dishonest page in it." "When the mask works and the visitation stops, what did we cure?" I have watched patients with a high CO₂ go peaceful and start talking to their mothers, and I have watched us correct it. "A symptom and a mercy look the same at three in the morning" is simply true, and I've never seen a novel say it. Havva's daughter's "Four percent, and nobody watched her" stung in the right way. So did Seher: "Wanting is also a gas" and "When your fan runs, I hear it until morning."
- **Ch. VII, the room under the floor.** The camera standing upright and wiped. Márton's hand out "to weather." "Is the fire mountain awake?" The teeth, "fewer than a mouth has." That was properly eerie.
- **Ch. VIII, Leyla Hanım's vigil.** "Every vigil in that hill was somebody's Emre." This is where I understood what the sitting nights are for.
- **Ch. IX's ending and all of Ch. X.** Márton weeping at the laptop over arithmetic, the cellist granddaughter, "he had wanted, all his life, a longer baseline." Melek at the dig house door: "I did not touch him. You will want him as he sat." Priska's "If the air had taken him, I could have fixed it," and Nilay's "I have one from 1999." Seher's "I am thirty-four. What am I supposed to do now?" I'm thirty-four. I put the book down for a minute there.
- **Ch. XI, Melek's confession.** Forty winters of four sentences into the niche. "If it was him, he kept his manners. If it was not him, then I have been faithful to a room." And the moment the singing room hands back "and the lamps are kept." I'd put this chapter up with Ch. II.
- **Ch. XIV, the reunion.** He had their mother's jaw. The ribbon. "You filled the lamp badly, that night." And then he broke the office for the first time: "Nilay." Then the mother, finding the ribbon on the table, refolding it on its old creases while her thumb keeps smoothing the top fold "long after there was nothing left to smooth." That is the image I'll carry out of this book.

**Drifted:**

- **Ch. III, the middle:** microgal, density tables, the triangle eleven centimetres short, refraction. I followed it but I was waiting for Márton to become a person again.
- **Ch. IV, the Malta archive paperwork:** duplicate accession numbers, clause eleven, the two leaves. The Saflieni child's shoe with the worn edge pulled me back in. Nothing else in that stretch did.
- **Ch. VI, the server-lag stream and the post going viral.** It was a long way round to get to gülce. More on her below.
- **Ch. VIII, the committee** ("who elected the Trust?") and the lab results: tritium, hair, grain.
- **Ch. IX, Dr. Marques's physics:** Casimir, negative energy, "a throat." It felt like another genre walking into the room.
- **Ch. XI, Recep's tour, the geophone week, Kaya Bey's correlation.**
- **Ch. XII, the consultancy emails** about "GNSS-denied environments." That's a techno-thriller subplot that goes nowhere.
- **Ch. XIII, the List, the drowned chapel, the Vigil of the Sealed Hour, the inked-out item.** The drowned chapel with the lamp taken out by boat is haunting on its own terms. But this is chapter thirteen of fourteen, and the book was opening new doors just when I wanted it to narrow toward Emre.

### 2. What confused me, and whether it paid off

**Deliberate, and worth it:**

- **The keeper letters.** At first I thought they were ancient. Around Ch. II I thought the humming keeper might be Emre. By Ch. VIII I understood they came from the other side of the house, when the keeper describes the drill as "iron that ate downward." That was a real chill. In Ch. XIV, when Emre says "I kept it nine," I wanted to go back and reread the Ch. V letter knowing that Melek had been answered by Nilay's brother for nine of her forty winters. That's the good kind of confusion. It turned into feeling.
- **Who the people in the polished room were.** I read them as ancient "grandparents." Only when Emre said "not the way you're going" did I work out they might come from far ahead, and the tritium and the hair suddenly meant something. I liked realising it afterwards. I wouldn't have liked being told.

**Just unclear, or noise to me:**

- **The pile of small anomalies.** The shaft going eighteen, nineteen, twenty, eighteen minutes. The elevation 1142.6 against 1142.5. Watches on the left wrist, then the right. Forty and forty-one. Every document dated before the thing it answers: the consents, Yusuf's thank-you letter, the closure memo, the solstice grant, Melek's hospital transfer. I noticed all of them. I got the idea (time leaking, the Trust knowing in advance) by about the third. After that they read like a checklist left for puzzle readers, and I stopped feeling anything when a new one turned up.
- **The "three explanations" habit.** After nearly every strange event someone "builds the forks" or "gives the ordinary accounts their turn": wet plaster, a stray lamp, bad air. It's in character for scientists. But by Ch. VIII I was sighing at every set of three.
- **Márton's death.** The Ch. X letter about burying "the ones who come to measure" pushed me toward a supernatural reading. The chapter itself is a plain death with clues sprinkled on it (the wrist, the elevation, two watches stopped at 03:12 and 05:41). I wanted to grieve him and was being asked to decode him. I still don't know whether the house took him, Halden did, or his heart did. Maybe that's the point, but it cost the death some weight for me.
- **Yusuf's lost two nights and the full cold teacup (Ch. VI)**, the Seher/Yusuf "who said it first" argument, the young woman's voice in the singing room, the forearm measuring. I couldn't place any of them. I let them go without much regret.
- **Small things.** "Havva Nine" and "Fatma Nine": for a page I thought Nine was a surname before I guessed it meant grandmother. The switch to second person in Ch. XIV ("You wake where you sat down") made me think for a paragraph that the narrator had changed.

### 3. What I guessed, and when

- **Ch. II:** that Emre was alive somewhere below, and that the humming in the storm was him or someone keeping for him. The humming not stopping for his name gave it away. Confirmed in Ch. XIV. I didn't mind knowing. The reunion works because you've been waiting for it.
- **Ch. IV:** that Halden knew exactly where Emre went. There's the "4–5.ix. Kept the hours" margin, and the 7.ix letter advising that nobody go below the fourth door. From then on I read him as the man who let a mother's heart and a girl's whole life run on a silence.
- **End of Ch. IV:** that Nilay would become the keeper. Melek "let her arrive at" the lamp.
- **End of Ch. IX:** that Márton wouldn't come up. "You have attended," plus the longer baseline. The letter opening Ch. X then more or less told me he was dead before Priska found him. That drained the suspense but added dread, and I think that trade was fair.
- **What I didn't guess:** that the Neolithic handprint was Nilay's. I only got it when she pressed her palm over it. Then the radiocarbon certificate and the fingerprint chit confirmed it twice more, which brings me to my one real complaint (section 5).

### 4. Characters I cared about, and ones I didn't

- **Priska, most.** She's the one who doesn't get the miracle. She logs the cough nowhere and moves out to the watcher's stone. Her job is explaining the symptom and handing out the mercy, and she's lost when there's nothing left to fix. I know that person. Some nights I am that person.
- **Melek.** She's the heart of the book. She's funny ("Poor thing," to the gravimeter), exact, and her forty winters broke me. Her signing herself out against advice because "a homecoming is not an accident" is exactly right. I've had that patient.
- **Márton.** He's vain, tender and rigorous, three watches in committee. I loved him and I'm still a little angry about how he went.
- **Seher.** She has few scenes and every one lands. "It costs the question."
- **Nilay: admired more than loved.** At eighteen she's alive on the page. At forty-four she's mostly a very good filing system for strange things. She holds everything so tightly that I spent the middle of the book outside her. She came back to me in Ch. XIV. Her last act, writing Emre's name into a book that "does not record the recorders," is the most important thing she does. As a nurse I know what it means to put a name on a record for someone the paperwork lost.
- **Emre.** The boy of Ch. II is vivid. The man of Ch. XIV mostly speaks in the keepers' grammar, which is the point ("the office said what the office said"), but I got less of him than I wanted. "I have never once had shoes" was the closest he came to being thirteen again.
- **The mother.** She's barely on the page and she gets the best moment in the book. That's the thing I'll carry out of it.
- **Yusuf.** He grew on me, and his ending lamp in the pickle stair is lovely. gülce needs its own note. A night-shift watcher, "a ward or a bakery," who never asks what's down there, only for the lamps. I felt seen. I also felt slightly flattered on purpose, and I noticed that.
- **Halden: I didn't care, and actively disliked him.** He's charming the way a well-run institution is charming. He sat with a lost thirteen-year-old on the first night, took his light away in the morning, and filed "Nothing to record." "The transfer was the least I could do" chilled me. What jarred was that the book seems to admire him. It gives him "the last image the man would give anybody," the pen at rest. Nobody is ever angry at him. Nilay strikes out the accusation because "she had a hill." I wanted one person to be angry.
- **Functions rather than people:** Kaya Bey, Dr. Marques, Recep, Demircioğlu, Mrs. Vella.

### 5. Lines I'll remember, and what jarred

**Lines:**

- "What is cut bleeds. What is pinched remembers."
- "The first register she ever kept was the absence of her brother."
- "Which ninni, kızım? I sang the one everyone sings. It was never mine to have."
- "A symptom and a mercy look the same at three in the morning, from the outside."
- "It was the cleanest reading of her career and the only dishonest page in it."
- "Wanting is also a gas."
- "If it was him, he kept his manners. If it was not him, then I have been faithful to a room."
- "A man ought to ask the year now and then."
- "Every vigil in that hill was somebody's Emre."
- "I left the house a lamp and was paid for it at the going rate." / "That's what shops are."
- "I am thirty-four. What am I supposed to do now?"
- "The first thing he had ever kept that could not be viewed."
- The mother's thumb on the fold of the ribbon.

**What jarred:**

- **Everyone talks the same way.** Melek, Halden, Márton, Priska, Seher, Kaya Bey, a Maltese tour guide and a Portuguese physicist all speak in the same polished aphorisms: "which is…", "the whole of it", "it has always been enough", "flat on the palm", "cupped and low", "I never do." In the letters it's liturgy and it's beautiful. Across 50,000 words of prose it becomes a lacquer. The only real change of voice is Yusuf's lowercase statement in Ch. VIII, and it felt like opening a window.
- **Dr. Marques's wormhole lecture, and the words "time travel" in Ch. XIV.** I understood why Nilay "gave them back." They still clanked. So did Emre's thesis-statement explanation ("a road that's long instead of far"). It's too neat for a man who says "I don't know the law. I know my road."
- **The ending over-proves itself.** Halden asks the book's best question: "Would you want it explained, if explaining it ended it?" The whole novel argues for leaving things unsettled. Then the biggest mystery gets settled by a lab certificate and a 31-point fingerprint match. Pressing her palm over the old print was enough. The paperwork turned wonder into proof.
- **Timing.** The new threads in Ch. XIII (the List, the drowned chapel, the sealed box) come right before the climax, when I wanted the book to narrow.
- **The mother, again.** The ending tells me that Nilay will one day go "farther back than any of us." That woman has already lost one child to that hill, and the book never looks at what the daughter's destiny costs her. I kept thinking about her after I'd closed it.

### 6. What I think it was about

It's about grief for someone with no body and no answer. It suggests the way to live with that isn't to solve it but to keep something: a lamp, a vigil, a small repeated act done for someone you'll never be sure is there. Science can fix the air, but it can't replace the lullaby.

### 7. Score and recommendation

**7 / 10.**

The heart of this book is a 9. Ch. II, Priska's cough, Melek's forty winters and the ribbon are as good as anything I've read on a night shift this year. It loses points for how much puzzle scaffolding surrounds that heart. Maybe a third of the length is clues, documents, instruments and institutions, and they mostly add up to "time is odd here," which I'd already felt in Ch. I.

I'd recommend it to anyone who has lost someone without a funeral, and to readers who liked *Piranesi*: a kept house, manners, wonder without explanation. I'd tell them to push through the middle chapters, because Chs. X, XI and XIV repay it. I wouldn't hand it to a colleague who wants a story that moves. And I wouldn't hand it to a puzzle reader either, because they'll be annoyed that the best parts of the book don't care about the puzzle.

## Tom, 52, engineer

### Panel report: Tom, 52

I read all fourteen chapters and every italic letter, start to finish, with a notebook open. I check dates, I redo sums, and when a story gives me a number I write it down. Here is what I wrote down.

### 1. Where I was gripped, and where I drifted

**Gripped**

- **Ch. I, the count.** She counts forty-one openings, then forty, and the form has "a crossed-out forty-one in her own pencil." That line hooked me. The clock story in the same chapter was the first thing I flagged.
- **Ch. III, the whole chapter.** Márton is the best thing in the book. He wears three watches because "two clocks can only argue." His survey triangle comes up eleven centimetres short, every session, and he refuses to average it because "an average is where anomalies go to be forgiven." He resents his watches for agreeing with each other. Then there's the 1997 story, with the referee's footnote that says *wishful*. I would have read a whole novel about this man.
- **Ch. V, 05:40.** Priska sits in clean air, 0.5% CO₂, and hears her dead father's cough beside her. She writes "0.5, still", and the book calls it "the cleanest reading of her career and the only dishonest page in it." Later there's the calibration memo, a single hand-copied page that doesn't touch her conclusions and only casts doubt on where her numbers came from. That's the smallest, neatest bit of sabotage I've seen in years, and it pleased the crime fan in me.
- **Ch. VI, the overlay.** Yusuf lays his forty-one mornings over Priska's pooled-air forecasts, and they sit "like a coin in a slot." Then Márton's gravimeter reads 14 microgal lighter on exactly those mornings. That gives three independent instruments pointing at one room. It's the best moment in the first half. The cold full teacup after he remembers drinking it was a nice chill too.
- **Ch. VII, the breach.** "It is a cylinder. They stand, or they roll." And the woman measuring the new doorway with her forearm.
- **Ch. VIII, the four statements.** This is how to do a Rashomon. The rule that falls out of it, "Where the hour had touched matter, the hour agreed with itself. Where it had touched people, it did not", is the cleanest idea in the book. Nilay catches her own notebook contradicting her own typed statement and staples both into the file. I stopped and admired that. And the empty circle in the grey foam, "holding, with perfect fidelity, nothing at all."
- **Ch. IX–X.** The two watches that were below both read forty nanoseconds off, twice, and Márton weeps at the laptop at 06:12 because "there being no field in the table." Then the body at the fourth door, and Halden asking Priska, "What, today, on your instrument, in your hand, could you sign?"
- **Ch. XIV, the ribbon.** More on that below.

**Drifted**

- **The italic letters, from about Ch. VIII on.** Each one taken alone is lovely. Together they turn into a litany. I counted, because that is who I am: in about 50,000 words, "the whole of" appears 31 times, "two breaths" 19, and "I did not follow past the lamp. I never do" 7. By letters X to XII I was reading them for data, not for pleasure.
- **Ch. XI, first half.** Recep's price list, the fence growing a second rail, Kaya Bey correlating shrine reports with road traffic. It's all fine. I was waiting for Melek.
- **Ch. XII, first half.** The consultancy emails and "GNSS-denied environments." As an engineer I perked up at the phrase, then lost interest until the figure at the threshold laid a forearm along the stone.
- **Ch. XIII, the List and the drowned chapel.** It's a moving fable about paying your keepers. But it's a detour two chapters from the end, when I wanted the tin box.

### 2. What confused me: deliberate or just unclear?

**Deliberate, and it paid off**

- **Who writes the letters.** The planting is in Ch. I: her renderings of the wall are "half transcription and half what her hand did while her eye counted." The last pages pay it off: the letters are what she read off the wall, and the island letter came through "a hand that had visited the island once." That's fair play. I didn't see it until the end, and it's fair that I didn't. My one niggle is that short carved strokes carry that much prose. But the book told me the hand fills it in, so it was stated up front.
- **Who the breach people are.** This was the best puzzle in the book, because I could work it out from the evidence:
  - the grain is a herbarium wheat that radiocarbon-dates as fossil carbon;
  - the hair matches "every reference population a little and none well";
  - the man's teeth are "regular as a comb, and fewer than a mouth has";
  - and the woman's forearm spans the 0.74 m door width while the 1.96 m doorway reaches her shoulder. I did the arithmetic: she is about two and a half metres tall. Márton's statement: "My own forearm is forty-six centimeters. I note it." For a reader like me, that's the best line in the book.

  One honest caution about the tritium. "Below detection" doesn't prove the future. Future rain would carry natural tritium again, so Priska's fossil-aquifer reading is the straight one. The author left that clue ambiguous instead of rigging it, and I give credit for that.

**Unclear, or not paid off**

- **The grandmother's clock.** It's the very first rule the book offers, and mechanically it doesn't work. A wall clock at home can't lose an hour because its owner spent the night somewhere else. The founder's 1924 note repeats it word for word, so it's meant to be taken seriously, and nothing ever explains why the *house clock* would lag. I waited thirteen chapters for the fix.
- **No consistent rate for time.**
  - Yusuf spends two nights with the keeper inside one morning.
  - The breach party loses about 2h40: the instruments say 3h40, their heads say an hour. That one is clean, because the meter strip settles it, and 10:04 to 13:44 really is 3h40.
  - Nilay's last vigil is one night on each side.
  - Emre has spent 27 years that were "longer for me," yet he's "in his forties."

  Then Emre says, "I don't know the law. I know my road." That's honest. But it comes after Ines Marques said "find the fuel bill," and the book's answer, "keeping is the thread," is poetry, not a bill.
- **Three drifting constants.**
  - The shaft's breath goes 18 minutes (Ch. I), 19 (VI), 20 (XI), then back to 18 (XIV).
  - The elevation at the fourth door is read out as 1142.6 m in Ch. III, deliberately "spoken twice", and comes back as 1142.5 at the inquest.
  - Márton wears his watches on his left wrist in Ch. III and his right in IX and X, and Priska explicitly notices.

  Only the breath pays off: it comes home to 18 once the loop closes. That was satisfying. The other two were highlighted by the author and then left. "Some of it re-keyed itself without her consent" is the book asking me not to ask. I asked.
- **"The grandparents dug upward."** I tried three frames: keepers in the past, keepers in the future, and "down means future." None of them made the sentence sit still. My best guess is that the village's "grandparents" are really the grandchildren. But the letter applies the phrase to diggers, and I can't make that fit. It may be my fault.
- **The sealed box doesn't close.** In a loop story this is the one thing that has to close. She breaks unbroken wax and finds a sheet dated the fifth, in her own hand. Later that day she writes it out "because she could not not," and then puts *both* copies in the drawer. The copy she writes never goes back into a sealed box. So the found sheet has no origin, and now there are two of them. The book even says they "differed in nothing she could find but their creases," which means it saw the duplicate and walked past it. The founder's 1951 box came back "plus one item: [inked out]", which suggests the house returns things. If so, show me the new sheet going back.
- **Márton's stopped watches.** They stopped at 03:12 and 05:41. Priska's dishonest reading was at 05:40. Plant or coincidence? I couldn't tell, and I don't like not being able to tell.
- **Malta.** The 1940 school party and the child's worn left shoe from a sealed niche. Very evocative. The only payoff is "do not correct."

### 3. What I guessed, and when

- **Ch. I:** the clock story means time runs wrong down there. Right.
- **Ch. I:** forty versus forty-one means the room is sometimes there and sometimes not. Right, later called "two builds."
- **Ch. II:** the red ribbon missing from the iron door, "Wind, or anyone." I wrote in my notebook: *that ribbon comes back*. It came back twelve chapters later. It still hit, because of the mother, not the ribbon.
- **Ch. II:** the keeper in the letter who hums to the girl is on the other side of that door, probably Emre grown up. I was about 60% sure by the end of II and 90% by IV. Confirmed in XIV.
- **Ch. IV:** the margin note "4–5.ix. Kept the hours. A.H." plus the Trust's letter of 7.ix.1999, "advises that none be entered." I guessed then that Halden sat with the boy and then kept the search out. Confirmed in XIV: "a light that didn't flicker."
- **Ch. IV:** the founder's phial of pigment from item 51 gets its own paragraph, which makes it a Chekhov's phial. I guessed "it will be dated" in IV and "the print is hers" in XIII, when the wall runs out ("the dark after the last will be the dark before the first"). From that point I also knew the book would end by repeating the first letter.
- **Ch. V:** the woman who comes to the niche every year is Melek. Suspected in V, confirmed in XI.
- **Ch. VI:** the boy with "the sun in his hand" is Yusuf and his phone. I got that at once.
- **Ch. VIII:** the breach people are from the future. The lab sheets gave it away.
- **End of Ch. IX:** Márton dies at the fourth door. "He had wanted, all his life, a longer baseline", then a chapter titled "The Autopsy of a Quiet Man." Halden's visit read like a farewell.

**What I didn't guess:** the fingerprint. I expected the pigment to be radiocarbon-dated. I did not see the elimination prints coming, even though they were taken in plain sight at the co-op table in Ch. X. That is proper fair play and the best crime-fiction beat in the book. My only quibble is 31 matching points from a 1924 photograph of an ochre palm on rough tuff. I'll allow it, grudgingly. The date itself is sound: "charcoal-bound" is the right detail, because you can't date ochre, but you can date the charcoal mixed into it.

### 4. Characters

**Cared about**

- **Márton, most.** He's me with a better vocabulary. His death hurt. It hurt more that the book gave it "the plain word" and didn't blame it on the house. That was the right choice.
- **Priska.** Her explanation wins, gets a stamp, and ruins her. Then comes the father's cough she never logs. And "What could you sign?" finished her and nearly finished me.
- **Yusuf.** A man who keeps changelogs. "recovered: 14 sec. watched: 0. The zero was the proudest number he had." Standing at the fence and knocking on the top rail because the manners are in his body now: I believed every step of how he changed.
- **Ines Marques.** She's in two scenes. She asked the only question that matters, "Show me the machine... find the bill," and the book never answered her. Keep her.
- **The mother.** Three lines, then the ribbon. Her thumb goes on smoothing the top fold "long after there was nothing left to smooth." That is the best moment in the book, and it's barely a sentence.

**Didn't care about as much**

- **Nilay.** I admired her but never warmed to her. She's the camera and the registrar, and her grief is kept behind glass on purpose. By XIV I needed more of her than "I did not cry until the ribbon."
- **Melek.** Magnificent as a function, a dispenser of perfect one-liners. She's only a person in XI, in the confession: "If it was not him, then I have been faithful to a room."
- **Emre.** The reunion works on the ribbon and the mother, not on him. He talks in the voice of the letters. The boy who raced paper boats surfaces for one line ("I have never once had shoes") and then goes under again.
- **Halden.** Interesting, and I didn't like him. I think the book lets him off. By my reading he sat all night with a trapped thirteen-year-old, left in the morning and took his torch, and three days later advised the gendarmerie not to go in. That's a crime. Nilay strikes out her accusation "because accusations want a court." For a crime reader, that's the one scene I wanted and didn't get. He's also the plot's postman: every time a document is needed, it arrives "at an entirely ordinary speed." By the fifth delivery (the Carpathian file, the certificate, the fingerprint note) I could see the author's hand up his sleeve.
- **Kaya Bey.** A good foil: "Then you were anticipated, Doctor."

### 5. Lines I'll remember, and what jarred

**Remember**

- "An average is where anomalies go to be forgiven."
- "It is a cylinder. They stand, or they roll."
- "The laboratory does not interpret zeros."
- "Wanting is also a gas."
- "A symptom and a mercy look the same at three in the morning, from the outside."
- "What, today, on your instrument, in your hand, could you sign?"
- "Four million includes the state."
- "The transfer was the least I could do."
- "Would you want it explained, if explaining it ended it?"
- "Nobody lights a first lamp." This is the story owning its paradox on page one, which is the fair way to do a loop.
- "It's a road that's long instead of far."
- The mother's thumb on the ribbon.

**Jarred**

- **The second-person switch in XIV** ("You wake where you sat down"). It works once you're in it. But reciting the letter inside the letter felt like the author making sure I got it.
- **"Time travel... and gave them back."** The book names its genre, then hands it back. Commit or don't; the wink cost more than it bought.
- **Emre's explanation speech.** It's the book's only lore dump, and it lands in the reunion, the one scene where I wanted anything but lore.
- **Evidence that keeps vanishing.** Zero-byte folders, twice, with "one frame" recovered each time. A camera that won't wake. A coin gone from sealed foam. Both lab samples "consumed." A video nobody watches. Each is fine on its own. As a set it's the author's thumb on the delete key, and the book admits it: "It lets me keep one." By the coin I was annoyed. I understand the design: everything can be denied except the one fingerprint. But it's heavy-handed.
- **The "anticipated" document gag.** The March letter, the April consents, the Tuesday letter, the memo dated the 14th, Márton's grant, the hospital transfer: six times. The first three landed. By the fourth I was braced for it, and the hospital reveal lost its punch.
- **Two small ones.**
  - 4% CO₂ is the immediately-dangerous threshold: headaches and panting. It usually doesn't knock someone out. An 84-year-old dozing at the bottom of a sump, fine, but I noticed.
  - The letter to Yusuf "thanked him for a thing he had not yet done." By Tuesday he had already held back, so that one is weaker than the text claims.
- **Credit where due.** The dates check out. 4 Sept 1999 was a Saturday, 4 Sept 2025 a Thursday, 4 Sept 2026 a Friday. 13 + 27 = 40. Forty nanoseconds overnight is hundreds of times more than a few metres of depth could explain by gravity, so the book is right to call it anomalous and not hand-wave it as relativity. Somebody did their homework.

### 6. What it was about

It's about tending something you'll never be allowed to understand, and about grief for someone who was never buried, carried by an institution of women sitting in the dark. Underneath is the idea that a place kept long enough exists in all its times at once. The real question is Halden's: would you want it explained, if explaining it ended it? The book answers no. That is where it and I part company a little.

### 7. Score and recommendation

**7/10.**

On evidence it plays fair, better than most crime fiction I watch. The fingerprint payoff is earned, and the lab sheets reward anyone who does the sums. It loses points for:

- refusing to show the machine after setting up a physicist to ask for it;
- a time loop that leaks one sheet of paper;
- two planted constants (the wrist and the elevation) that go nowhere;
- a Registrar who is both the mystery and the delivery service;
- prose that turns into a chant at 50,000 words.

I'd recommend it to people who liked *Dark*, *Arrival* / "Story of Your Life", *Roadside Picnic* or *Piranesi*, and to anyone who reads the footnotes or has ever refused to average a bad reading. I would not give it to anyone who needs pace or needs the mechanism explained. My brother-in-law would quit in Chapter II. My wife would forgive everything for the mother and the ribbon, and honestly, so nearly did I.

## Priya, 23, film student

### Panel report: Priya (23, film student)

I read it start to finish, the italic letters included, and the reprinted opening letter at the end too. Short version: it's a real puzzle box, with plants that pay off and a bootstrap loop that closes. It's also the most repetitive thing I've read this year, and for long stretches the repetition is the texture rather than the structure.

### 1. Where I was gripped, and where I drifted

**Gripped:**

- **Ch. I, the census scene.** She counts 41, then 40, 40, 40, and there's a crossed-out 41 on the form. Right after that, the pre-filled permit says "Brother — missing, earthquake, September 1999" and the day has worn off it. Two quiet hooks in two paragraphs, and I was in.

- **Ch. Nobody answers, and the question folds itself away. It plays like a scene you'd shoot in one long take.
- **Ch. VIII, Four Reports.** A Rashomon chapter where matter agrees and people don't. Structurally it's the smartest idea in the book, and it was the one chapter where the repetition was the whole point.
- **Ch. XIII, "And the hospital?" / "The transfer was the least I could do."** It's chilling because it's polite.
- **Ch. XIV, the vigil.** The switch into second person when she wakes, and the letter in her own handwriting that she hasn't written yet ("and I did not cry until the ribbon"). Then the ribbon arrives, and I cried at roughly the same point she did.

**Drifted:**

- **Ch. III, the gravimetry.** Some of this wore me down: tables, drift, dunking offcut blocks, the refraction argument. The Priska/Márton banter rescued it, but I was reading for the jokes, not the data.
- **Ch. IV, the first half of Malta** reads like a travelogue until Halden opens the door.
- **Ch. XI, the pilgrim and tourism montage.** Recep's laminated list, the knock "first answered in May," the gendarme's log against road traffic. It's all summary, and it felt like the book marking time until Melek's confession. Things finally sharpened at "Forty years I have gone down at the end of winter…".
- **Ch. XII, the consultancy emails.** "Timing resilience in GNSS-denied environments" is a paragraph I read twice and still didn't care about. The Yusuf chapter only woke up at the figure on the threshold.
- **Ch. XIII, the Ayios Nikandros drowned chapel.** A brand-new subplot (remittances, wage-lines, an unsent letter, "pay the keepers") arrives in the second-to-last chapter. I could see what it was for, since it hands Nilay the moral debt of the office, but it's a digression when the film should be tightening.
- **The explanation routine.** Something uncanny happens, a character lists three or four ordinary explanations, "none of them confessed," and it gets filed. Nilay does this, then Priska, Yusuf, Márton, Nilay again, Yusuf again. By the time the sandal print turns up in Ch. XII I could have written the paragraph myself. It's thematic, but it's the same beat on a loop.

### 2. What confused me, and whether it was worth it

**Confusing in a way that paid off:**

- **Who writes the italic letters.** At first I read them as one old keeper. Then Ch. II's letter was clearly someone sitting with Nilay at 18, Ch. V's was the far side of Melek's niche, and Ch. VI's "boy with the sun in his hand" was Yusuf with his phone and Priska's clicking meter. Working out that they came from different keepers at different times was the best "lean forward" pleasure in the book. The last paragraph of Ch. XIV hints that the letters are partly what Nilay's own hand "rendered" off the wall ("how much of what she read was cut and how much arrived"). I had to reread that paragraph to catch it, and when I did it recolored every chapter opening.
- **The air-shaft refrain.** The same paragraph appears four times, with the breath period at 18, then 19, then 20, then 18 again. The number drifting and coming back after the vigil is a lovely detail you can only catch by noticing the repetition. It's one of the few places where the book uses repetition as a device and not just a mood.
- **Márton's elevation.** He reads 1142.6 aloud twice "so the world can't quietly amend it," and the inquest reads 1142.5. The world amended it. Nice.

**Just unclear, or withheld too hard:**

- **Márton's death.** The watches move from his left wrist to his right, and the text flags the change itself. Two stopped at 03:12 and 05:41, and the third kept running and still agreed with the sky. Halden visited on the 30th, and "no visitor was entered… the village remembered weather." Prints were lifted from the watch case. I can see the book pointing at something (a mirror build? Halden? did he go "along"?), but I couldn't assemble it, and nothing later helps. For the most emotional death in the book, I wanted at least one more clue.
- **The Ch. IX and XI letters.** "The room chooses the one who stays", with the quick-handed candidate and the grieving one, and "in the year the house changed hands she had gone up." I couldn't pin down who was writing or who was chosen. I think XI is Melek setting her lamp down on the far side, but the "hers for forty years before it was mine" line tangled me.
- **The Ch. XIII letter about the wall running out**, and Emre's last cut "over an erasure." I got the mood but not whose hand it was.
- **Melek's "If it was him."** Him who? The village gave her a fake husband, so who was she really speaking to at the niche for 40 years? Emre's answer ("somebody keeping, taking her for somebody of their own") is graceful, but I still wanted to know who *she* thought it was.
- **The young woman's voice in Ch. XI** ("and the lamps are kept"). Is it Nilay's own future voice, or the child from the first letter? I think it's meant to stay open. Fine, but it's one more open thread in a book full of them.

### 3. What I guessed, and when

- **Some kind of time slip: end of Ch. I.** The clock that loses an hour per night below, "thanking her *in advance*," and consents dated April for a June meeting made that pretty clear. The book shows its hand on purpose, and I didn't mind.
- **Emre is alive below and was the humming presence: Ch. II.** The italic letter plus "hers, and not hers, *as she would one day be told*" basically announces the reunion. The missing red ribbon on the door iron told me it would come back. I was right, and the payoff still landed, so the telegraphing was fine here.
- **Halden was there the night Emre vanished, and the Trust kept it off the record: Ch. IV,** from the margin note. What I didn't guess was that Halden sat with Emre the first night and then *took the light away*. That's the book's coldest turn.
- **Melek is the woman at the niche in the Ch. V letter: Ch. V.** It was confirmed in XI.
- **Márton will die down there: the moment Ch. IX ended and the next heading read "The Autopsy of a Quiet Man."** The chapter title gives it away before the letter even starts. Throw that out, or rename it. Watching him go down alone with "he had wanted, all his life, a longer baseline" should be dread, not a spoiler.
- **The handprint (item 51) belongs to someone we know: Ch. I, vaguely.** The prose insists too hard that it's nothing ("Item fifty-one took her no longer than item fifty"). I got there fully around Ch. XIII–XIV. I did not see the elimination-prints trick in the inquest coming. Using a death investigation to quietly fingerprint the heroine is a great hidden-in-plain-sight plant.
- **The breach people are from far "along" (the future) rather than the past: Ch. VIII.** The tritium zero, Márton's "or the last of it has finished decaying," and teeth "drawn rather than grown" got me there. I'm still not sure, and I like that I had to work for it.

### 4. Characters

- **Márton** is my favourite. The three watches, the 1997 file, "an average is where anomalies go to be forgiven," "never with anything that could say no," and logging his own happiness as a reading. When the parcel acquits him and he still goes down to be his own witness, I was fully invested, which is why the murkiness of his death annoyed me.
- **Priska** was a close second. She's the skeptic who wins administratively and loses personally ("I legislated myself out of the room"). The father's cough, the "considered indicative" splinter, and Seher's "What am I supposed to do now?" pressing on her all worked. She has the best arc in the book.
- **Seher:** small part, big lines. "It costs the question."
- **Yusuf** was charming. His content-to-keeping arc is clean, maybe too clean: the channel comes down, he refuses to watch the 14 seconds, he lights a lamp in Derinkuyu. The gülce thread is lovely.
- **Melek** is iconic, but for the first half she's a machine for aphorisms. The Ch. XI confession finally made her a person.
- **Nilay** I cared about in Ch. II and Ch. XIV. In between she's mostly a registrar: she files, declines to ask, and lays things "flat on the palm." That's thematically right, and it makes her the least active protagonist I've followed in a while.
- **Emre:** I cared about him as an absence. Once he's onscreen in XIV he talks in the keepers' voice ("the good distance," "past the name, nothing"), and apart from "I have never once had shoes" I didn't hear a boy who grew up. The mother's jaw was a better piece of characterization than anything he says.
- **Halden** is fascinating as a presence and a blank as a person. That's probably the point.
- **Didn't care about:** Kaya Bey, who is a rationalization dispenser, and Recep. Leyla Hanım is introduced and buried in one paragraph to set up a vigil. Halime has been hanging on a hook since Ch. II and gets a single nod at the bread queue. I didn't know her well enough for that to land.

### 5. Moments I'll remember, and what jarred

**Remember:**

- "It fell." / "It landed." / "It is a cylinder. They stand, or they roll."
- "Is the fire mountain awake?"
- "At 05:40 she wrote: *0.5, still.*"
- The circle cut in the grey foam "holding, with perfect fidelity, nothing at all."
- "Would you want it explained, if explaining it ended it?"
- "The transfer was the least I could do."
- Nilay's mother folding the ribbon on its old creases, her thumb smoothing the top fold "long after there was nothing left to smooth," and watching her daughter go out the way she once watched a boy go out in house slippers. This is the best shot in the book, and nobody speaks in it.
- The reprinted opening letter at the end. Reading "Nobody lights a first lamp" again once you know who lit it is a proper last-shot-mirrors-first-shot move.

**Jarred:**

- **Ritual phrases as filler.** "I did not follow past the lamp. I never do." "Past the name, nothing." "Oil to the knuckle, wick pinched true." "Knocked twice with the flat of the hand, softly, and waited out the two breaths." "It has always been enough." Each of these appears so often that I started counting them. Emre's "I never do," which "sounded like quotation, because it was," is a real payoff for all that. Cut the other instances by half and it would land harder.
- **Recycled images.** Something carried "like a coal in both hands" twice, "the moon's laundry" twice, "the same size" constantly, and "which was the whole of it" everywhere. "Nobody counted twice" appears twice in one paragraph of Ch. VII.
- **Emre's "It's a road that's long instead of far" speech.** It's a good line wrapped in an exposition dump. Then "time travel" gets named outright, even if it's handed back. I'd rather the book trusted me the way it did in Ch. VIII.
- **The handprint reveal lands twice.** First it's narrated ("which had always been hers"), then it's confirmed by a lab certificate and a fingerprint chit. The document is the stronger beat. Drop the narrated line and let the paper do it.
- **Timing:** the "Autopsy" title (see above), and the tense slip in Ch. II ("Nilay has never asked her directly. Halime is alive…"), which pulled me out for a second.
- **The second-person switch in Ch. XIV** is jarring in a good way. "Then she was herself again, forty-five" is a great hard cut back.

### 6. What I think it was about

Keeping versus knowing: some places, and some griefs, survive only as long as you attend to them without demanding they explain themselves. The price of that keeping is putting down the question you most want answered. Under that, it's a sister going looking for her brother and finding that the search itself was the job she'd been given.

### 7. Score and recommendation

**7/10.** On structure alone it's closer to an 8.5: the Rashomon chapter, the drifting shaft numbers, the fingerprint plant and the bootstrap letter are all genuinely clever. On pacing it's closer to a 6, because the rituals and the explanation routine eat the middle, and Chs. XI–XIII sag before the finale.

I'd recommend it to patient readers who liked *Piranesi*, *Arrival* or *Dark*, the kind of people who enjoy rereading the first page after the last. I wouldn't recommend it to anyone who needs momentum, or who'll be irritated the fifth time someone "waited out the two breaths."

**Would I recommend it?** Not to my thriller mates, or not without a warning: stick with it until the drill goes through the floor in Ch. VII, and it's worth it for the last chapter. I'd give it to my sister-in-law, who likes the slow book-club sort. Most of all, I'd give it to anyone who has lost somebody and never had a grave to stand at. They'll get the ribbon.

## Lena, 41, philosophy teacher

### Panel report: Lena (41, philosophy teacher, churchgoer)

I read it straight through over two evenings, the keeper's letters included, and the prologue a second time when it came back at the end. I teach philosophy of religion to seventeen-year-olds and go to Mass most Sundays. So I read it the way I read anything: what does it think belief is, and what does believing cost the people who believe?

---

### 1. Where I was gripped, and where I drifted

**Gripped**

- **I, the opening keeper's letter.** A set of craft rules that turns into a spirituality without ever saying so. "Fear spends itself where it is allowed to." "Faster is how you learn the smell of your own eyebrows" made me laugh out loud. After that I trusted the voice.
- **II, the 1999 search.** An eighteen-year-old becomes "a clerk of the search" with red ribbon from the bazaar. The sergeants' "*No findings*" is called "the kindest sentence the summer produced and the cruelest." Then the night behind the stone: she says "Emre" once, "the humming did not change and did not stop," and "that was either nothing or everything, and in the dark the two were the same size." Until the last chapter this was the strongest stretch of pure story in the book.
- **IV, "Anchorhold."** I knew the word before the dictionary did: Julian of Norwich, walled in with a window onto the altar. I was glad the book let it sit unexplained. Then the margin in the green volume, *4–5.ix. Kept the hours. A.H.*, and the hair stood up on my arms.
- **V, the sitting nights.** This chapter is the book's argument, and it argues fairly for both sides. Havva at four percent, calm, saying "She was cross with me for the hour." Priska's father's cough at 05:40, and "*0.5, still*. It was the cleanest reading of her career and the only dishonest page in it." Seher: "Wanting is also a gas." "It is not talk that is missing, Doctor." Then Priska's question, which I will steal for a lesson: "When the mask works and the visitation stops — what did we cure?"
- **VII, the breach.** The finished room, the unsooted ceiling, the camera stood upright and wiped, facing the stair. And "Is the fire mountain awake?" with nobody answering, because "a wrong answer dressed as knowledge is the one theft she had never committed." That is intellectual honesty as a moral act, and it moved me.
- **XI, Melek's confession at the wicks.** For me this is the whole book in one paragraph (see section 5).
- **XII, Yusuf alone at his uncle's stair in Derinkuyu** with a tin lamp from the co-op: "the first thing he had ever kept that could not be viewed." I did not expect his thread to end up where my heart was.
- **XIV, the ribbon.** "You filled the lamp badly, that night." Then the mother at the bread oven, her thumb smoothing the top fold "long after there was nothing left to smooth." I cried at the ribbon, as the sealed sheet said Nilay would, and not before.

**Drifted**

- **III, the gravimetry.** Microgal, density tables and the triangle eleven centimetres short. I understand the precision is the point, a man measuring something that says no. My eyes still slid. The Márton–Priska sparring ("Your socks, Priska, have never once been asked to close") is too polished. It reads as written to be charming rather than overheard.
- **VI, second half.** The viral numbers, the list of comment types ("the *part 2* people"). It is familiar satire and slower than the rest.
- **VIII, the committee** ("who elected the Trust?") and the lab-result roll call. I felt the book filing things rather than living them, even though filing is one of its subjects.
- **IX, Marques on negative energy and the Casimir effect.** This felt like the book taking out insurance with physics. I didn't need the wormhole. I needed Márton, and the chapter keeps interrupting him.
- **XII, opening.** The consultancy emails, "GNSS-denied environments," a directorate thanked in a footer. The idea that the state is hungry for this lands in one good line ("Four million includes the state") and then the thread leaves.
- **XIII, the List and Ayios Nikandros.** Told through counterfoils and wage-lines, and on first reading I lost track of who rowed the lamp out to the drowned chapel and when. The founder's unsent letter pulled me straight back.

---

### 2. What confused me, and whether it paid off

**It paid off:**

- **Who writes the italic letters, and when.** At first I took them for one keeper's diary. In II, the letter shows Nilay's night behind the stone from the other side. In VI, "the sun in the hand" is a phone and the ticking box is Priska's meter. In VIII, the list of "diggers" includes our own drill ("iron that ate downward"). By the last pages I understood they are the wall's minutes, as Nilay's hand renders them ("how much of what she read was cut and how much arrived"). That was a real reward, and it made me want to reread the whole thing.
- **The small quiet amendments.** The air shaft's breath is timed at 18, 19, 20 and back to 18 minutes. Márton's elevation, spoken twice so the world couldn't change it, comes back at the inquest as 1142.5 instead of 1142.6. The watches are on his left wrist in July and his right in December. Yusuf's tea was drunk and not drunk. I liked noticing these and did not need them explained. They made the "different build" idea something I felt, not something I was told.
- **Melek's "him."** Whom did she grieve at the niche for forty years, if the husband was the village's invention? The book never says, and it shouldn't.

**It was just unclear:**

- **The far lamplighters.** Past or future? Teeth "drawn rather than grown," a coin "either very worn or very new," water with no tritium, an einkorn with "no farmers," a Hittite word for "you-who-are." I accept that the contradictions are deliberate. Past a certain point, though, the contradiction reads as a puzzle box set for a different reader than me, the kind who keeps a spreadsheet.
- **"The grandparents dug upward" (VIII).** I read it three times and still can't keep straight which way is up. If it means that *we* are someone else's grandparents and someone else's visitation, that the living are the ghosts in another keeper's night, it is one of the best ideas in the book, and it is buried too deep.
- **The VI letter.** A boy carrying "the sun in his hand" sleeps two nights by a keeper's lamps. When did Yusuf do that? In his future? I couldn't place it, and it felt like a dropped stitch, not a mystery.
- **Halden.** Is he one of the far keepers? The grey figure at the fence in XIII sets a fallen jar upright without breaking stride, which echoes VII's lamplighters who "righted what a tremor had laid down." I can't tell whether the book intends me to connect them or I'm over-reading.

---

### 3. What I guessed before the story told me

- **End of I:** that Nilay would finish as the keeper writing "to the one who keeps the lamps after me." It was more or less confirmed when Melek lets her light the ninth lamp in IV.
- **End of II:** that the humming keeper behind the stone was Emre. The letter's "she had done nothing, and had been done-to enough," and "hers, and not hers, as she would one day be told," gave it away. By IV I was nearly sure.
- **IV:** that Halden was with Emre on the night of 4–5 September, and that his letter of 7 September kept the search away from him. XIV confirmed it ("a light that didn't flicker").
- **The handprint.** The book flags item fifty-one in I so pointedly ("took her no longer than item fifty") that I made a pencil note. The bottled ochre in IV raised it again, and the inquest taking elimination prints "palms and fingers" in X made me certain. So the technician's note in XIV confirmed what I knew rather than revealing anything. It still landed emotionally.
- **Márton's death:** from the last line of IX ("he had wanted, all his life, a longer baseline").
- **The hospital transfer:** as soon as XI gave the date on the authorisation. Halden's "the least I could do" confirmed it.
- **What I did *not* guess:** that Melek was the woman with the four sentences and the jar of water. That was a genuine surprise and the best kind. Nor did I guess that Emre kept that niche for nine winters, so that Melek and Nilay's brother may have sat on either side of the same wall, each taking the other for someone else. That irony is almost unbearable, and I mean that as praise.

### 4. Characters I cared about, and ones I didn't

**Cared about:**

- **Melek**, most of all. She is dry and exact, and never once explains herself. "Greedy or frightened. Which are they?" — "Both. They love you." — "Then it's the right oil." Her death and her lamp "set down" gave me the sense of a whole life being handed on.
- **Seher**, the young widow. She is the book's clearest believer, and the book never talks down to her. She can draw Priska's diagram for the children and still say the diagram leaves out "the one night I needed." Her "What am I supposed to do now?" in X is the true cost of the scientific fix, and her answer in XI ("It costs the question. You put the question down the way you put down a load at a door") is the best short account of faith I have read in a novel in years.
- **Priska.** A scientist of the thing that killed her father. She wins administratively and loses everything else. I loved "Then the manner works, whichever way it works."
- **Nilay's mother.** Three appearances, and the ribbon.
- **Yusuf.** He grew on me. "recovered: 14 sec. watched: 0." "The zero was the proudest number he had."

**Admired more than cared for:**

- **Márton.** He is a lovely type, the old empiricist with three watches, but he is a type. The one moment he broke through was weeping at the laptop over arithmetic, "there being no field in the table."
- **Nilay.** I respected her, and her reserve is the point, but it kept me at arm's length for twelve chapters.
- **Emre.** As a boy in II he is fully alive: the grass whistle, the paper boats, the performed contempt of thirteen. As a man in XIV he mostly speaks in the keepers' grammar, and I missed the boy. "I have never once had shoes, in all of it" was the one line where he came through.

**Did not care about:** Kaya Bey (a function), Marques (a lecture) and Recep (a sketch).

**Wanted more of:** Halime. She carried a village's suspicion for twenty-six years, and her reward is Nilay standing beside her at the bread queue and giving her own name first. The gesture is right, but it is small against what she bore.

**Halden** fascinates me, and I don't like him. That brings me to consequence (section 6).

---

### 5. Lines and moments I'll remember, and what jarred

**I'll remember:**

- "Whoever she takes you for, be the right size." (V) I will take this to church. It describes what a priest does, and a godparent, and anyone who stands at a graveside in someone else's place.
- "The words land on whoever is in the room. You do not get to choose your size. You only get to consent to it."
- "If it was him, he kept his manners. If it was not him, then I have been faithful to a room." And "the not-settling is the steadiest thing I own."
- "A belief is a keeping, kept." (XII)
- "Nobody lights a first lamp."
- "A question is a lamp you hold up on somebody, and the young cannot stand the light of it."
- The founder's three roads: "Known, a place becomes an asset… Believed, it becomes a banner… Left, it goes to the rain." The whole book proposes a fourth road, kept.
- "Would you want it explained, if explaining it ended it?"
- The Registrar's letter to Yusuf, thanking him for a restraint he had not yet shown.
- The review page in XI: "five stars, finally an explanation," after which the knock goes out of use and the answers thin. It is disenchantment in one sentence, with no sneer in it.
- The last image: Nilay at Göbekli Tepe wetting a stone for the camera, then stopping halfway because "she could not tell the method from the rite; and went on wetting it anyway, and did not decide."

**What jarred:**

- **The house style.** Almost every character speaks in the same aphoristic cadence: Melek, Halden, Priska, Márton, Kaya Bey, Marques, even a Hittite professor and a laboratory. By the middle of the book I could predict a sentence's rhythm before I finished it: "which is the whole of it," "and it was enough," "flat on the palm," "the way you…," "I never do," "load-bearing." I understand the refrains are the liturgy. But liturgy works because the rest of life isn't liturgy, and here too much of the rest of life is.
- "Oxygen debt writes sermons, Priska. The theology is finished; the fumes remain." This one is too pleased with itself.
- **The recognition scene.** When she says "Emre," the sentence breaks into a long parenthesis about the three-syllable name, its grandmother and its grandchild. That is the one beat I most wanted left clean.
- **Emre's explanation** ("a road that's long instead of far… Keeping is the thread. The rooms come when they're kept"). This is the one place the book explains its machinery in dialogue. Having Nilay name it "time travel" and hand the words back helps, but only partly.
- **The technician's note** ("Funny — … Friction ridges don't lie. 31 points, right hand") and the radiocarbon certificate. Two envelopes do in a page what the story had been doing through experience for thirteen chapters. The note in particular reads as convenient paperwork.
- **XI's compression.** Melek's collapse, the hospital, signing herself out, setting down the lamp, the confession and her death come so close together that I had to re-sort the order in my head.

### 6. What I think it was about

Belief as a practice rather than a proposition: something you keep, not something you settle, and keeping changes the keeper long before it proves anything. The other half is the cost of knowledge to the people who live on consolation.

A little more, because this is what I read for:

- **The ending settles what Melek said should not be settled.** For thirteen chapters the book holds knowledge and comfort in fair tension: Priska's clean reading against Seher's fortieth day. It honours not-settling as a virtue. Then XIV gives a literal answer. The brother is alive in time, and the handprint is dated to 10,720 years, "the one measurement taken all year that no fork could carry." That is knowledge, not faith. Emotionally I was grateful. Philosophically, the book blinked. To be fair, it half answers its own question: Halden asks whether she would want it explained if explaining ended it, and it is explained, and it does not end, because she goes on keeping. I can accept that. But Melek's forty years mean slightly less once the reader knows who sat behind the niche.
- **The theology runs sideways, not upwards.** It is a sacramental world: oil, bread "offered, not pushed," carried water, vigils, and an ordination that is a child's yawn. But there is no giver outside the circle. "Nobody lights a first lamp" refuses the first-cause question and answers it with a closed loop, Nilay's own hand at the head of the wall. It is beautiful as an image of tradition, a communion of saints with no one to make saints. Some believers in my parish would find that hollow at the centre. I found it honest about what it is.
- **Consequence, and Halden.** He sat one night with a thirteen-year-old and then left, "and took the light with him. Keepers leave you the lamp." His letter of 7 September steered the search. The cost was a mother's heart, a sister's twenty-six years, and Halime's name. The book calls it "a kindness, arranged; an arrangement, done kindly." Nilay strikes out the accusation because "accusations want a court, and she had a hill." I understand, and I see that the loop makes blame murky: if the record is fixed, can he be blamed at all? But the book lets determinism do its moral work for it. XIII's keeper, hesitating over the last mark, is the book's best statement that there is freedom inside a fixed record ("the hesitation is a courtesy"). I wish that freedom had been asked of Halden, and not only granted to keepers.
- **Márton.** Halden visits, leaves "For your peace," says "You have attended," and Márton breaks his promise to Priska and Marques, goes down alone, and dies. Did the kindness kill him? The book declines to say. Of all the places where it refuses to judge, this is the one where the refusal felt like courage rather than evasion. It is the thread that will stay with me longest.

---

### 7. Score and recommendation

**8 / 10.**

I would recommend it to:

- my church book group, where it would start the best argument we've had in years;
- anyone who teaches or studies philosophy of religion (faith and knowledge, falsifiability, belief as practice, foreknowledge and freedom);
- readers who loved *Piranesi*, *Gilead*, or Le Guin's quieter books.

I would not recommend it to readers who want a quick plot or a clear account of how the time mechanics work. I would give one gentle warning to anyone whose family member is missing: the last chapter gives what life almost never does, and some readers will be hurt by that rather than consoled.
