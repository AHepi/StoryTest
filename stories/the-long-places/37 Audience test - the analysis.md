# Audience Analysis — *The Lamps Are Old*

## Scores

| Reader | Score | One-line take |
|---|---|---|
| Ana (nurse, 34) | 8/10 | Emotional architecture is some of the best she's read in years |
| Tom (engineer, 52) | 8.5/10 | "Would argue about it afterward, which is the highest compliment" |
| Priya (film student, 23) | 8/10 | Structural masterclass; trim 20% and it's a 9 |
| Joe (general reader, no age given) | 6/10 | Three gripping hours and three hours of work |
| Lena (philosophy teacher, 41) | 9/10 | "The most honest book about belief I have read in years" |

**Average: 7.9.** Four of five gave it 8 or above. The one low score came from the reader most interested in plot and least interested in lyricism — his praise for the emotional scenes was just as high as everyone else's, but he felt the book asked for patience he didn't want to give.

---

## Where the Panel Agreed

Nearly everyone agreed on the following:

- **Melek is the best thing in the book.** All five readers named her as the character they cared about most or among the top. Her confession at the wicks — forty winters, four sentences into a niche, "I never settled it" — was called the emotional peak by Ana and was one of the most-cited moments across all reports. Nobody had a single negative word about her.

- **Chapter II (the 1999 backstory) is the engine.** Every reader was gripped from the moment Emre went out in slippers and never came back. The lamp filled past the knuckle, the red ribbon, the humming in the dark, the mother in the cardiology corridor — all five flagged this as the part that made them keep reading through everything else.

- **The fingerprint reveal is the best surprise in the book.** All five readers said they did not see it coming. Four of five called it a highlight. Even Joe, who had the lowest score and felt confused by the ending, said the handprint "blindsided" him. Lena called it "a thunderclap." Tom noted the book is smart enough to know you've guessed something and turns the reveal into a "how" instead of an "aha."

- **The science and measurement sections caused drift.** This was the single most consistent complaint. The gravimeter readings, CO₂ tables, microgal figures, triangle measurements, and solstice watch details lost Ana, Tom (in spots), Priya, Joe, and Lena (in spots). Joe called those parts "work." Priya said "the third time around I was skimming." Even Lena, who gave the book a 9, found Chapter III's density tables a stretch. The consensus: the book makes the same point three times (instruments can't close on this hill) and the third telling feels like running out the clock.

- **Kállai's death is devastating.** All five found the scene at the fourth door — hand on his knee, watches disagreeing at last — powerful and dignified. Even readers who drifted during his measurement sections came back hard for his Carpathian confession and his ending.

- **Emre being alive below was guessed early.** Every reader figured this out by Chapter II or III — the warmth at the right shoulder, the humming, the mirror to Nilay's later experiences. The book didn't try to hide it. Most readers felt this was correct: the story makes you sit with it rather than trick you.

- **The keeper's instructions/manuals are beautiful but polarizing.** Ana, Tom, Priya, and Lena found them extraordinary — the interludes were called "the most extraordinary prose in the book." Joe mostly skipped them. The opening line ("A lamp is not a light…") was remembered and quoted by all five, even Joe, who admitted he skimmed those chapters.

- **The reunion in Chapter XIV works.** All five responded to the fourth of September — the letter written before it's lived, the ribbon on the mat, Emre's "You filled the lamp badly, that night." Joe called it "brilliant — told through the lamp-keeping rules." Even readers who found the circular time mechanics confusing still felt the emotion.

---

## Where the Panel Disagreed

- **Priska.** Ana and Lena loved her deeply — Lena called her "the book's conscience" and "my favorite." Tom respected and related to her. But Joe found her "hard to like" and "stubborn and rigid," and Priya wanted more personal scenes and fewer threshold tables. She is the character the panel is most split on.

- **The final time-travel reveals.** Ana, Tom, Priya, and Lena found the fingerprint, the letter-before-it's-written, and the circular wall deeply satisfying. Joe found the same passages confusing and said that confusion "did NOT feel rewarding." He wanted the reunion and felt the book "tricked" him with the letter and the handprint and the ten thousand years. This is the biggest single disagreement in the panel.

- **Yusuf's chapters.** Ana called his subplot "the weakest current in the river" and said it felt like a different book trying to get in. Priya found his summer chapter too long. But Tom found his arc "clean" and Joe liked him a lot — "the boy with the phone." Lena appreciated his small, perfect moments. He's not disliked, but three of five felt he takes up too much space for what he contributes.

- **How much explanation to give.** Lena and Tom wanted slightly more orientation — "not an answer, a boundary" (Lena) and "the rules of the Trust, or it needs to stop showing the Trust being right about everything" (Tom). But Ana and Priya loved the withholding. The book is being asked to hold two things in tension and can't please both sides.

## Where Attention Drifted (specifically)

In order of how often it was mentioned:

1. **Instrument and measurement data** — gravimeter, microgals, triangle dimensions, nanoseconds, density tables. Mentioned by all five, most strongly by Joe and Priya.
2. **CO₂ and air chemistry sections** (Chapter V) — Ana, Priya, Joe. Everyone loved the Havva Nine moment and Priska's father's cough; the data tables around those moments dragged.
3. **Solstice watch chapter** (Chapter IX) — Ana, Priya, Joe. Lena loved it and Tom found Márton's death earned, but the setup ran long for three of five.
4. **Yusuf's livestream analytics** (Chapter VI) — Ana, Priya. Chat logs, view counts, gülce column.
5. **Valletta office scenes** (Chapter IV) — Priya, Tom. "Elegant bureaucrat with tea" twice in a short span. The Trust's worldbuilding was felt to be beautiful but slow.
6. **Keeper's instruction sections** — Joe skipped most of them; Tom found one (the far lamplighters, before Chapter XII) too abstract at a point where he wanted momentum.

## What Readers Guessed Early vs. What Surprised Them

**Guessed early (by Chapter II–V for most readers):**
- Emre is alive and kept below (all five)
- Halden/the Trust know more than they reveal (most)
- Márton won't survive (several, especially Tom — "the foreshadowing is heavy and deliberate")
- The 41st room is an anomaly that won't be explained away (Priya, Tom)
- The breathing shaft pattern is intentional (Tom checked four times; Priya, Joe, Lena all noticed)

**Not guessed — genuinely surprised:**
- The fingerprint/handprint is Nilay's (all five, no exceptions)
- The letter written before it's lived (Joe was blindsided and found it disorienting; others knew *something* non-linear was coming but the specific content surprised them)
- The exact content and mechanics of the final time collapse

## Confusions That Paid Off vs. Confusions That Didn't

**Paid off:**
- **41 rooms vs. 40.** All readers who noticed it loved that it was never resolved. Priya: "That's the mystery working the way the hill works."
- **The breathing shaft cycling (18→19→20→18).** Noticed by at least four. Tom re-read the opening to check. Lena felt "the whole architecture shift under me." Joe wasn't sure if it was intentional. It works better the closer you read.
- **The circular structure** — the wall beginning where it ends, the opening keeper's text being the ending. Ana: "paid off beautifully."
- **The keeper's texts as a collective voice.** Priya worked out they were the same office across centuries and found the confusion rewarding. Lena: "manners instead of ontology, and manners I can follow."
- **The Trust being all-knowing and possibly negligent.** The founder's letter about cutting the remittances was enough for Tom to feel the book was playing fair — he wanted more of it, but the seed was there.

**Didn't pay off:**
- **The far lamplighters vs. the Breach figures.** Priya couldn't tell if these were the same beings or different. "Unlike the good confusions, this one didn't reward the not-knowing."
- **The Trust's precise rules.** Tom: the distinction between attending across time and intervening across time "never gets made crisp enough." He doesn't need cave rules but does need Trust rules.
- **The final time-mechanics paragraph (Chapter XIV).** Joe: "I had to read it three times… that confusion did NOT feel rewarding." Ana found the "time travel" label almost too neat. Even Lena (9/10) called the naming of "two small clumsy words" a slight flinch.
- **Halime's thread.** Three of five mentioned she's set up and then dropped too softly. Priya: "needs either one more scene or to be cut." Ana and Lena agreed. The story seems to mean "the question of who hummed is never settled," but the setup overshoots the payoff.

## Small Wording and Timing Quibbles

Things critics leave to an audience — and that the audience actually noticed:

**Bothered readers:**
- **"Server lag" as a chapter title** (Priya). Every other title is old and quiet; this one feels like a Twitch stream. It breaks the spell.
- **The Casimir effect / negative energy paragraph** (Ana) — "reads like a Wikipedia article dropped into a psalm." "Hypercapnia" and "40,000 ppm" also yanked her out.
- **"Time travel" being named and given back** (Ana and Lena). The gesture is right but the label is a flinch. The book was doing fine without it.
- **The breathing-shaft paragraph appears almost verbatim three times** (Priya, Ana). The number shifts but the prose doesn't. The third time, Priya skimmed. Ana felt the same. Recommendation: compress on reprises.
- **"I do not follow past the lamp. I never do."** Said about eight times across the manuals (Priya). First time: haunting. Sixth: wallpaper.
- **The Trust's list of sites** (Newgrange, Dowth, Derinkuyu) — Ana felt it read like a museum placard.
- **Márton's "good hour / worse evenings with better instruments"** right before his death (Priya) — slightly too neat as a farewell. Let the death speak.
- **"Comfort that lays hold of you is only weather with manners"** (Tom) — good sentence, but in a passage already doing three other things well. One aphorism too many.

**Only one reader noticed:**
- **Elevation discrepancy: 1,142.6 in Chapter III vs. 1,142.5 in Chapter X** (Tom). A 10-centimeter difference in a book otherwise meticulous about figures. He couldn't tell if it was intentional or a typo, and it stuck out precisely because everything else is so tight.
- **"cupped, and low"** appearing three times (Lena). The third felt like the book checking its own motif.
- **"Yusuf's 'nobody drags a priest in for server lag'"** — Ana winced; Joe and others liked Yusuf's voice generally.

**Nobody noticed or minded:**
- Specific microgal figures and their decimal places
- The exact CO₂ ppm numbers (they blurred together)
- The "figure spoken twice" ritual (Tom felt it was over-signposted but didn't jar)

## The Three Changes the Panel Most Supports

**1. Trim the science chapters by 20–30%.** This is the single change that would raise every score. The gravimeter measurements, CO₂ data tables, and solstice watch setup each re-demonstrate the same structural point (instruments can't close on this hill), and the third telling loses people. Cut the data, keep the feelings — Kállai weeping at the laptop, Priska's 05:40 page, Havva Nine saying she spoke with her mother. Those scenes don't need the numbers around them to do their work; they need the numbers around them *less*.

**2. Compress repeated prose on reprises.** The breathing-shaft paragraph appears almost word-for-word three times; "I do not follow past the lamp" appears about eight times; "cupped, and low" appears three times. The first occurrence of each is powerful. By the third, the motif has become wallpaper. On the second and third passes, compress, echo, or vary — don't repeat wholesale. This is a fix that would take an afternoon and improve the middle of the book substantially.

**3. Resolve or trim the Yusuf/Halime/Seher balance.** Three of five readers felt Yusuf's internet subplot takes up too much space for what it delivers, and three of five wanted Halime to get one more scene or be cut entirely. Seher's "I am thirty-four, what am I supposed to do now" was called out by Ana as deserving more space. The suggestion is not to add characters but to rebalance: give Halime one clean beat (or remove the setup), give Seher one more moment, and tighten Yusuf's stream analytics and consultancy letters by a third. The emotional payload of his chapters — the unplayed fourteen seconds, the lamp in the stairwell, the phone face-down — is strong. The setup around it is the drag.
